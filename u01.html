Derivative rules: power, sum, product, quotient

Constant, power, constant multiple, sum and difference, product, and quotient rules. Marginal cost, revenue, profit.

LessonEvery way it is askedLevel 1Level 2Level 3Level 4Level 5
The big idea

These two sections replace the slow limit definition with a few short rules, so you can find any derivative of powers, sums, products, and fractions in a couple of lines. Before you use a rule, rewrite every root and every fraction like \(\tfrac{1}{x^3}\) as a power of \(x\). Products and quotients have their own rules: the derivative of a product is never the product of the derivatives.

In business problems, the word "marginal" just means "take the derivative." Its value is about how much one more unit adds.

Section 4.1: Techniques for finding derivatives

Why we need rules

The derivative of a function \(f\) at \(x\) is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at that point, and it is also the instantaneous rate of change of \(f\). Until now you found it with the limit definition

\[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}.\]

That works, but it is slow. For a function like \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x} + 1}{x^2 + 3}\), the algebra would be painful. Chapter 4 replaces the limit with a short list of rules. Each rule was proved once with the limit definition (the professor does the proofs in lecture). So you can now use the rules directly. Some rules are general and tell you how to handle sums, differences, constant multiples, products, and quotients. Others are specific and give the derivative of one kind of function, like a power of \(x\).

Notation

There are four common ways to write the derivative of \(y = f(x)\). They all mean the same thing:

\[f'(x), \qquad \frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)\big], \qquad D_x\big[f(x)\big].\]

The symbol \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\) is an instruction that says "take the derivative with respect to \(x\) of whatever comes next." The symbol \(D_x\) is the same instruction written differently. The form \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) is used when the function is named \(y\). For example, if \(y = x^2\), then \(f'(x) = 2x\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2x\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[x^2\big] = 2x\), and \(D_x\big[x^2\big] = 2x\) are four ways of writing one fact. On the exam you may see any of them.

The constant rule

The graph of a constant function \(f(x) = k\) is a horizontal line. At every point, the tangent line is that same horizontal line, and a horizontal line has slope 0. In the language of rates, a constant never changes, so its rate of change is 0.

Constant rule

If \(f(x) = k\), where \(k\) is any real number, then \(f'(x) = 0\). In short form, \(k' = 0\).

In plain words: a plain number with no variable in it has derivative zero, because it never changes.

For example, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[7] = 0\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[-12] = 0\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[\pi^2\big] = 0\). The last one catches people: \(\pi^2\) is about 9.87, a fixed number, so its derivative is 0. It is not \(2\pi\), because \(\pi\) is not the variable.

The power rule

From the limit definition you already found that the derivative of \(x\) is 1, the derivative of \(x^2\) is \(2x\), the derivative of \(x^3\) is \(3x^2\), the derivative of \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) is \(-\dfrac{1}{x^2}\), and the derivative of \(\sqrt{x}\) is \(\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\). All of these are cases of one formula. Bring the exponent down in front as a multiplier, then lower the exponent by 1.

Power rule

If \(r\) is any real number, then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big[x^r\big] = r\,x^{r-1}.\]

This holds for positive, negative, and fractional exponents, and even for exponents like \(\pi\).

In plain words: copy the exponent down in front as a multiplier, then make the exponent one smaller. This only works when the variable is the base and a fixed number is the exponent.

The power rule only applies to something written in the form \(x^r\). A fraction with \(x\) in the denominator or a root of \(x\) has to be rewritten as a power first. These are the rewriting facts you need:

\[\frac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n}, \qquad \sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}, \qquad \sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}, \qquad \sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{m/n}, \qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{-1/2}.\]

After you differentiate, it is good practice (and what the textbook does) to rewrite negative and fractional exponents back as fractions and roots in the final answer. Either form is correct.

Worked example 1

Find the derivative of each function: (a) \(y = x^8\), (b) \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^3}\), (c) \(g(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\), (d) \(h(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\), (e) \(k(x) = x^{\pi}\).

How to spot this type: each function is a single power of \(x\) in disguise (a fraction with \(x\) only in the bottom, or a root), so the move is to rewrite it as \(x^r\) and use the power rule.

  1. Part (a) is already a power of \(x\) with \(r = 8\). Apply the power rule: bring down the 8 and lower the exponent to \(8 - 1 = 7\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 8x^{8-1} = 8x^7\]
  2. For part (b), rewrite the fraction as a power. A power in the denominator moves to the numerator with the sign of the exponent flipped. \[f(x) = \frac{1}{x^3} = x^{-3}\]
  3. Apply the power rule with \(r = -3\). The new exponent is \(-3 - 1 = -4\), which is more negative, not \(-2\). \[f'(x) = -3x^{-3-1} = -3x^{-4} = -\frac{3}{x^4}\]
  4. For part (c), rewrite the cube root as a power: the cube root of \(x\) is \(x\) to the one third. \[g(x) = \sqrt[3]{x} = x^{1/3}\]
  5. Apply the power rule with \(r = \tfrac{1}{3}\). The new exponent is \(\tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{3}{3} = -\tfrac{2}{3}\). Then move the negative power to the denominator and turn it back into a root, using \(x^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{x^2}\). \[g'(x) = \frac{1}{3}x^{1/3 - 1} = \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} = \frac{1}{3x^{2/3}} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\]
  6. For part (d), rewrite the square root as the power \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), and then move it up from the denominator. \[h(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{x^{1/2}} = x^{-1/2}\]
  7. Apply the power rule with \(r = -\tfrac{1}{2}\). The new exponent is \(-\tfrac{1}{2} - 1 = -\tfrac{3}{2}\). Since \(x^{3/2} = x^1 \cdot x^{1/2} = x\sqrt{x}\), the answer can be written with a root. \[h'(x) = -\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2 - 1} = -\frac{1}{2}x^{-3/2} = -\frac{1}{2x^{3/2}} = -\frac{1}{2x\sqrt{x}}\]
  8. For part (e), the exponent \(\pi\) is just a real number, so the power rule applies with \(r = \pi\). \[k'(x) = \pi x^{\pi - 1}\]

Answer: (a) \(8x^7\); (b) \(-3x^{-4} = -\dfrac{3}{x^4}\); (c) \(\dfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\); (d) \(-\dfrac{1}{2}x^{-3/2} = -\dfrac{1}{2x\sqrt{x}}\); (e) \(\pi x^{\pi-1}\).

Common mistake: differentiating a root or a fraction without rewriting it. The power rule needs the form \(x^r\). Writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{1}{x^3}\right] = \dfrac{1}{3x^2}\) is wrong: that treats the denominator as if it could be differentiated by itself. Rewrite \(\dfrac{1}{x^3} = x^{-3}\) first. The same goes for roots: \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) must become \(x^{1/3}\) before anything else happens.
Common mistake: subtracting 1 from a negative exponent in the wrong direction. For \(x^{-3}\), the new exponent is \(-3 - 1 = -4\). Students often write \(-2\) because they move toward zero. Subtracting 1 always moves the exponent one step to the left on the number line.
Common mistake: a coefficient in the denominator. \(\dfrac{5}{x^2} = 5x^{-2}\), but \(\dfrac{1}{5x^2} = \dfrac{1}{5}x^{-2}\). Only the power of \(x\) moves up. The 5 in \(\dfrac{1}{5x^2}\) stays in the denominator as \(\tfrac{1}{5}\).

The constant multiple rule and the sum and difference rule

If you stretch a graph vertically by a factor of \(k\), every slope gets multiplied by \(k\) too. That is the constant multiple rule: a constant factor just waits outside while you differentiate the rest. Also, the rate of change of a sum is the sum of the rates of change. So you can differentiate a sum or difference one term at a time.

Constant multiple rule

If \(k\) is a constant and \(g\) is differentiable, then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big[k\,g(x)\big] = k\,g'(x). \qquad \text{In short form, } (kg)' = k\,g'.\]

In plain words: a number multiplying the function stays in front untouched while you take the derivative of the rest.

Sum and difference rule

If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable, then

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x) \pm g(x)\big] = f'(x) \pm g'(x). \qquad \text{In short form, } (f \pm g)' = f' \pm g'.\]

In plain words: when terms are added or subtracted, take the derivative of each term on its own and keep the plus and minus signs between them.

For example, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[6x^5\big] = 6 \cdot 5x^4 = 30x^4\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[x^2 + x^3\big] = 2x + 3x^2\). Together with the constant rule and the power rule, these let you differentiate any polynomial and any sum of powers of \(x\) term by term.

Worked example 2

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = 5x^4 - \dfrac{8}{x^2} + 6\sqrt[3]{x} - 9\).

How to spot this type: the function is several terms joined by plus and minus signs, and each term is a number times a power, a root, or a fraction with \(x\) only in the bottom. Rewrite term by term, then differentiate term by term.

  1. Rewrite every term as a constant times a power of \(x\). The fraction becomes \(\dfrac{8}{x^2} = 8x^{-2}\) and the cube root becomes \(6\sqrt[3]{x} = 6x^{1/3}\). \[f(x) = 5x^4 - 8x^{-2} + 6x^{1/3} - 9\]
  2. By the sum and difference rule, differentiate each of the four terms separately and keep the signs between them. \[f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\big[5x^4\big] - \frac{d}{dx}\big[8x^{-2}\big] + \frac{d}{dx}\big[6x^{1/3}\big] - \frac{d}{dx}[9]\]
  3. Use the constant multiple rule and the power rule on each term. The new exponents are \(4 - 1 = 3\), \(-2 - 1 = -3\), and \(\tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{2}{3}\). The constant rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[9] = 0\). \[f'(x) = 5 \cdot 4x^{3} - 8 \cdot (-2)x^{-3} + 6 \cdot \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3} - 0\]
  4. Multiply out the coefficients. Note that \(-8 \cdot (-2) = +16\), so the middle sign changes from minus to plus. \[f'(x) = 20x^3 + 16x^{-3} + 2x^{-2/3}\]
  5. Rewrite with positive exponents, using \(x^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\). \[f'(x) = 20x^3 + \frac{16}{x^3} + \frac{2}{\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = 20x^3 + 16x^{-3} + 2x^{-2/3} = 20x^3 + \dfrac{16}{x^3} + \dfrac{2}{\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\)

How to differentiate a sum of powers of x
  1. Rewrite every root as a fractional power and every \(\dfrac{1}{x^n}\) as \(x^{-n}\). Keep each coefficient with its term.
  2. Differentiate term by term (sum and difference rule), keeping each constant multiplier out front (constant multiple rule).
  3. On each power, bring the exponent down and subtract 1 from it (power rule). A constant term becomes 0 (constant rule).
  4. Multiply coefficients carefully, watching for a negative times a negative.
  5. Rewrite the answer with positive exponents and roots if you like.

Marginal cost, marginal revenue, and marginal profit

In economics, three functions of \(x\), the number of units produced and sold, come up constantly. The cost function \(C(x)\) is the total cost of producing \(x\) units. The revenue function \(R(x)\) is the total money taken in from selling \(x\) units. If each unit sells for price \(p\), then \(R(x) = x \cdot p\). The profit function is \(P(x) = R(x) - C(x)\).

In plain words: cost is what you spend, revenue is what customers pay you, and profit is what is left over after you subtract the cost from the revenue. Revenue is always the number sold times the price of each one.

The word marginal means derivative. The marginal cost is \(C'(x)\), the marginal revenue is \(R'(x)\), and the marginal profit is \(P'(x)\). Because a derivative is a rate of change, \(C'(x)\) measures how fast cost grows per additional unit. When \(x\) increases by 1, the cost changes by approximately \(C'(x)\). So \(C'(100)\) is approximately \(C(101) - C(100)\), the cost of making the 101st unit.

In plain words: a marginal number answers the question "about how much does one more unit add?" The marginal cost at 100 units is roughly the cost of making unit number 101.

What to write on the exam. To interpret \(C'(100) = 40\), write: "When 100 units have been produced, the cost of producing one more unit (the 101st) is approximately $40." Use the same sentence for revenue and profit with the words changed: "selling one more unit increases revenue by about ..." or "increases profit by about ...". A negative marginal profit means one more unit decreases profit.
Worked example 3

A company's cost of producing \(x\) units is \(C(x) = 1500 + 20x + 0.1x^2\) dollars, and the demand function is \(p = 100 - 0.2x\), where \(p\) is the price in dollars at which \(x\) units can be sold. (a) Find the marginal cost function and \(C'(100)\), interpret it, and compare with the exact cost of the 101st unit. (b) Find the revenue function and the marginal revenue at \(x = 100\). (c) Find the marginal profit function and \(P'(100)\), and interpret it.

How to spot this type: the words "marginal cost," "marginal revenue," or "marginal profit," together with a cost function and a demand (price) equation. Build \(R = x \cdot p\) and \(P = R - C\) first, then differentiate.

  1. (a) Differentiate \(C\) term by term with the constant, constant multiple, and power rules. \[C'(x) = 0 + 20 + 0.1 \cdot 2x = 20 + 0.2x\]
  2. (a) Substitute \(x = 100\). \[C'(100) = 20 + 0.2(100) = 20 + 20 = 40\] When 100 units have been produced, the cost of producing the 101st unit is approximately $40.
  3. (a) Compute the exact cost of the 101st unit, \(C(101) - C(100)\), to see how good the approximation is. \[\begin{aligned} C(101) &= 1500 + 20(101) + 0.1(101)^2 = 1500 + 2020 + 0.1(10201) = 1500 + 2020 + 1020.1 = 4540.1 \\ C(100) &= 1500 + 20(100) + 0.1(100)^2 = 1500 + 2000 + 1000 = 4500 \\ C(101) - C(100) &= 4540.1 - 4500 = 40.1 \end{aligned}\] The exact cost is $40.10, very close to the marginal cost of $40.
  4. (b) Revenue is the number of units times the price per unit. Substitute the demand function for \(p\) and distribute. \[R(x) = x \cdot p = x(100 - 0.2x) = 100x - 0.2x^2\]
  5. (b) Differentiate with the power rule and substitute \(x = 100\). \[\begin{aligned} R'(x) &= 100 - 0.4x \\ R'(100) &= 100 - 0.4(100) = 100 - 40 = 60 \end{aligned}\] Selling the 101st unit increases revenue by approximately $60.
  6. (c) Profit is revenue minus cost. Put the whole cost function in parentheses so the minus sign reaches every term, then combine like terms. \[\begin{aligned} P(x) &= (100x - 0.2x^2) - (1500 + 20x + 0.1x^2) \\ &= 100x - 0.2x^2 - 1500 - 20x - 0.1x^2 \\ &= -0.3x^2 + 80x - 1500 \end{aligned}\]
  7. (c) Differentiate and substitute \(x = 100\). \[\begin{aligned} P'(x) &= -0.6x + 80 \\ P'(100) &= -0.6(100) + 80 = -60 + 80 = 20 \end{aligned}\] As a check, \(P'(100) = R'(100) - C'(100) = 60 - 40 = 20\), the same number. When 100 units have been sold, selling one more unit increases profit by approximately $20.

Answer: (a) \(C'(x) = 20 + 0.2x\), \(C'(100) = 40\): the 101st unit costs about $40 (exactly $40.10). (b) \(R(x) = 100x - 0.2x^2\), \(R'(100) = 60\). (c) \(P'(x) = 80 - 0.6x\), \(P'(100) = 20\): the 101st unit adds about $20 of profit.

Tangent lines and horizontal tangents

The derivative \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at the point \((a, f(a))\). A line through the point \((x_1, y_1)\) with slope \(m\) has point-slope form \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\). Putting these together gives the tangent line.

Tangent line at x = a
\[y - f(a) = f'(a)\,(x - a)\]

In plain words: a tangent line needs two things, a point and a slope. The point comes from plugging \(a\) into the original function, and the slope comes from plugging \(a\) into the derivative.

A tangent line is horizontal exactly when its slope is 0. So the graph of \(f\) has a horizontal tangent at every \(x\) where \(f'(x) = 0\).

The same idea works for any required slope. Two lines are parallel when they have the same slope, so the tangent line is parallel to \(y = mx + b\) wherever \(f'(x) = m\). Two lines are perpendicular (they meet at a right angle) when their slopes multiply to \(-1\), so the tangent line is perpendicular to \(y = mx + b\) wherever \(f'(x) = -\dfrac{1}{m}\). In every case you set \(f'(x)\) equal to the slope you want and solve for \(x\).

How to find the tangent line at x = a
  1. Find the point: compute \(y_1 = f(a)\) with the original function \(f\).
  2. Find the slope: compute \(f'(x)\), then \(m = f'(a)\).
  3. Write \(y - y_1 = m(x - a)\) and, if asked, solve for \(y\) to get \(y = mx + b\).
  4. Check: substitute \(x = a\) into your line. You should get \(f(a)\).
How to find where the tangent line is horizontal
  1. Compute \(f'(x)\) and simplify it as far as possible (factor it if you can).
  2. Solve \(f'(x) = 0\). For a fraction, set the numerator equal to 0 and make sure the denominator is not 0 there.
  3. For each solution, compute the \(y\)-coordinate with \(f\), not with \(f'\), and report the points.
Worked example 4

Let \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 2\). (a) Find an equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at \(x = 1\). (b) Find all points on the graph where the tangent line is horizontal.

How to spot this type: the words "tangent line at \(x = a\)" mean point plus slope, and the word "horizontal" means slope 0, so you solve \(f'(x) = 0\).

  1. (a) Find the point of tangency by substituting \(x = 1\) into \(f\). \[f(1) = 1^3 - 3(1)^2 - 9(1) + 2 = 1 - 3 - 9 + 2 = -9\] The point is \((1, -9)\).
  2. (a) Differentiate with the power rule term by term. \[f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9\]
  3. (a) The slope is \(f'(1)\). \[f'(1) = 3(1)^2 - 6(1) - 9 = 3 - 6 - 9 = -12\]
  4. (a) Write the point-slope form with \((x_1, y_1) = (1, -9)\) and \(m = -12\), then solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - (-9) &= -12(x - 1) \\ y + 9 &= -12x + 12 \\ y &= -12x + 3 \end{aligned}\] Check: at \(x = 1\) the line gives \(-12 + 3 = -9 = f(1)\).
  5. (b) Set \(f'(x) = 0\) and factor. First factor out the common 3, then factor the quadratic. \[\begin{aligned} 3x^2 - 6x - 9 &= 0 \\ 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) &= 0 \\ 3(x - 3)(x + 1) &= 0 \end{aligned}\] So \(x = 3\) or \(x = -1\).
  6. (b) Find the \(y\)-coordinates with the original function \(f\). \[\begin{aligned} f(3) &= 27 - 3(9) - 9(3) + 2 = 27 - 27 - 27 + 2 = -25 \\ f(-1) &= -1 - 3(1) - 9(-1) + 2 = -1 - 3 + 9 + 2 = 7 \end{aligned}\]

Answer: (a) \(y = -12x + 3\). (b) The tangent line is horizontal at \((-1, 7)\) and \((3, -25)\).

Common mistake: using f' for the y-coordinate. The slope comes from \(f'\), but the point comes from \(f\). In Worked example 4, \(f'(1) = -12\) is the slope. The point is \((1, f(1)) = (1, -9)\), not \((1, -12)\).

Section 4.2: Derivatives of products and quotients

The product rule

The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives. So it is tempting to guess that the derivative of a product is the product of the derivatives. That guess is false, and one tiny example proves it. Take \(u = x\) and \(v = x\). Their product is \(x \cdot x = x^2\), whose derivative is \(2x\). But \(u' \cdot v' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\), and \(1\) is not \(2x\). The correct rule has two terms.

Product rule

If \(u\) and \(v\) are differentiable functions of \(x\), then

\[(uv)' = u'v + uv'. \qquad \text{Equivalently, } \frac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)g(x)\big] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x).\]

In words: the derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second.

In plain words: for two things multiplied together, take turns. Differentiate the first one and leave the second alone, then leave the first alone and differentiate the second, and add the two results.

Check it on \(x \cdot x\): \(u'v + uv' = 1 \cdot x + x \cdot 1 = 2x\), which is correct. The professor's habit, and the clean way to avoid errors, is to write down the four pieces \(u\), \(v\), \(u'\), and \(v'\) before you assemble anything.

Worked example 5

Use the product rule to find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = (x^2 + 5)(\sqrt{x} - 2)\). Then check by multiplying out first.

How to spot this type: two sets of parentheses written next to each other with \(x\) in both, or the words "use the product rule."

  1. Name the two factors and rewrite the root as a power: \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\). Then differentiate each factor with the power rule. \[u = x^2 + 5, \qquad v = x^{1/2} - 2, \qquad u' = 2x, \qquad v' = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  2. Assemble the product rule \(f' = u'v + uv'\). \[f'(x) = 2x\left(x^{1/2} - 2\right) + \left(x^2 + 5\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  3. Distribute both products. Add exponents when multiplying powers: \(x \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{3/2}\) and \(x^2 \cdot x^{-1/2} = x^{3/2}\). \[f'(x) = 2x^{3/2} - 4x + \frac{1}{2}x^{3/2} + \frac{5}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  4. Combine the two \(x^{3/2}\) terms: \(2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{5}{2}\). \[f'(x) = \frac{5}{2}x^{3/2} - 4x + \frac{5}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  5. Check by multiplying out first. Expand \(f\), using \(x^2 \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{5/2}\). \[f(x) = x^2 \cdot x^{1/2} - 2x^2 + 5x^{1/2} - 10 = x^{5/2} - 2x^2 + 5x^{1/2} - 10\]
  6. Differentiate term by term with the power rule. The result matches. \[f'(x) = \frac{5}{2}x^{3/2} - 4x + \frac{5}{2}x^{-1/2}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{5}{2}x^{3/2} - 4x + \dfrac{5}{2}x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{5}{2}x\sqrt{x} - 4x + \dfrac{5}{2\sqrt{x}}\)

Common mistake: \((uv)' = u'v'\). This is wrong. For \(f(x) = (x^2 + 5)(\sqrt{x} - 2)\) it would give \(2x \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = x^{1/2}\), which is not the derivative. Always use two terms, \(u'v + uv'\).

The quotient rule

The same warning applies to quotients: the derivative of \(\dfrac{u}{v}\) is not \(\dfrac{u'}{v'}\). Take \(u = x\) and \(v = x\). Then \(\dfrac{u}{v} = 1\), whose derivative is 0, but \(\dfrac{u'}{v'} = \dfrac{1}{1} = 1\).

Quotient rule

If \(u\) and \(v\) are differentiable functions of \(x\) and \(v \neq 0\), then

\[\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}. \qquad \text{Equivalently, } \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2}.\]

Memory line, with "high" meaning the numerator and "low" meaning the denominator: low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared. Here "d-high" means the derivative of the top. So the numerator is (bottom)(derivative of top) minus (top)(derivative of bottom).

In plain words: for a fraction, multiply the bottom by the derivative of the top, then subtract the top times the derivative of the bottom, and divide everything by the bottom squared. The order matters because of the minus sign.

The order of subtraction matters. In the product rule the two terms are added, so their order does not matter. In the quotient rule they are subtracted, and \(a - b = -(b - a)\). If you write \(uv' - u'v\) instead of \(u'v - uv'\), every term of your answer has the wrong sign. The numerator must start with the derivative of the top.

Worked example 6

Use the quotient rule to find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x + 3}{x^2 + 1}\).

How to spot this type: a fraction whose bottom has two or more terms joined by plus or minus (here \(x^2 + 1\)). You cannot split that fraction, so the quotient rule is the method.

  1. Name the top and bottom and differentiate each. \[u = 2x + 3, \qquad v = x^2 + 1, \qquad u' = 2, \qquad v' = 2x\]
  2. Assemble the quotient rule \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\): low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared. \[f'(x) = \frac{2\left(x^2 + 1\right) - (2x + 3)(2x)}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)^2}\]
  3. Expand each product in the numerator separately. Keep the second product in parentheses, because the minus sign in front applies to all of it. \[2\left(x^2 + 1\right) = 2x^2 + 2, \qquad (2x + 3)(2x) = 4x^2 + 6x\]
  4. Subtract. The minus sign changes the sign of both \(4x^2\) and \(6x\). \[\begin{aligned} 2x^2 + 2 - \left(4x^2 + 6x\right) &= 2x^2 + 2 - 4x^2 - 6x \\ &= -2x^2 - 6x + 2 \end{aligned}\]
  5. Put the simplified numerator over the denominator. Leave the denominator in factored form; expanding it only makes the answer longer. \[f'(x) = \frac{-2x^2 - 6x + 2}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)^2}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{-2x^2 - 6x + 2}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)^2}\)

Common mistake: dropping the minus sign or flipping the order. Writing \(\dfrac{(2x + 3)(2x) - 2(x^2 + 1)}{(x^2 + 1)^2}\) gives \(\dfrac{2x^2 + 6x - 2}{(x^2+1)^2}\), the exact negative of the right answer. Writing a plus sign instead of a minus gives something unrelated. Say "low d-high minus high d-low" in your head every time.
Common mistake: simplifying signs wrong. In \(2x^2 + 2 - (4x^2 + 6x)\), the minus sign must reach every term inside the parentheses. Writing \(2x^2 + 2 - 4x^2 + 6x\) is the most common sign error on this topic. Always expand the second product inside parentheses first, then distribute the minus sign as its own step.
What to write on the exam. Name the rule and show its pieces: "By the quotient rule with \(u = 2x + 3\), \(v = x^2 + 1\), \(u' = 2\), \(v' = 2x\), ..." The written work is graded, and listing the pieces earns credit even if an arithmetic slip happens later.

When to simplify instead of using the quotient rule

If the denominator is a single term (a monomial, such as \(x^2\), \(5x\), or \(\sqrt{x}\)), you can divide each term of the numerator by it and use the power rule. This is faster and gives fewer chances for sign errors. For example,

\[f(x) = \frac{6x^3 - 4x + 2}{x^2} = \frac{6x^3}{x^2} - \frac{4x}{x^2} + \frac{2}{x^2} = 6x - 4x^{-1} + 2x^{-2},\]

so by the power rule

\[f'(x) = 6 - 4(-1)x^{-2} + 2(-2)x^{-3} = 6 + 4x^{-2} - 4x^{-3} = 6 + \frac{4}{x^2} - \frac{4}{x^3}.\]

The quotient rule would give the same answer after more work. When the denominator has two or more terms, like \(x^2 + 1\), you cannot split the fraction this way, because \(\dfrac{a}{b + c}\) is not \(\dfrac{a}{b} + \dfrac{a}{c}\). Then you use the quotient rule.

Which rule to use
  1. A sum of terms that are constants times powers of \(x\): rewrite roots and fractions as powers, then use the power rule term by term.
  2. A fraction whose denominator is one term: divide each numerator term by the denominator, then use the power rule.
  3. A product of two expressions that you do not want to multiply out: product rule.
  4. A fraction whose denominator has two or more terms: quotient rule.

Average cost and marginal average cost

If \(C(x)\) is the cost of producing \(x\) units, the average cost function is the cost per unit,

\[\overline{C}(x) = \frac{C(x)}{x}.\]

In plain words: average cost is the total bill divided by the number of units, so it is the cost of each unit if every unit cost the same.

Its derivative \(\overline{C}'(x)\) is called the marginal average cost. It tells you how the average cost per unit changes when production goes up by one unit. Companies want the average cost to be as small as possible, and in Chapter 5 you will see that this happens where \(\overline{C}'(x) = 0\). The same words apply to revenue and profit: \(\overline{R}(x) = \dfrac{R(x)}{x}\) is the average revenue and \(\overline{P}(x) = \dfrac{P(x)}{x}\) is the average profit, and their derivatives are the marginal average revenue and the marginal average profit.

The quotient rule with \(u = C(x)\) and \(v = x\) gives a general formula:

\[\overline{C}'(x) = \frac{C'(x)\cdot x - C(x)\cdot 1}{x^2} = \frac{x\,C'(x) - C(x)}{x^2}.\]

In plain words: the marginal average cost says whether the cost per unit goes up or down when you make one more unit, and by about how much. It is negative exactly when the next unit costs less than the current average, because a cheap extra unit pulls the average down.

Since the denominator \(x\) is a monomial, you can also simplify \(\overline{C}(x)\) first and use the power rule. Both ways appear below.

Worked example 7

For the cost function \(C(x) = 1500 + 20x + 0.1x^2\) from Worked example 3, find the average cost function and the marginal average cost function. Compute \(\overline{C}(100)\) and \(\overline{C}'(100)\) and interpret both numbers.

How to spot this type: the words "average cost" or "cost per unit" mean divide by \(x\) first, and "marginal average cost" means then take the derivative of that quotient.

  1. Divide the cost by \(x\). Because \(x\) is a monomial, split the fraction term by term. \[\overline{C}(x) = \frac{1500 + 20x + 0.1x^2}{x} = \frac{1500}{x} + \frac{20x}{x} + \frac{0.1x^2}{x} = 1500x^{-1} + 20 + 0.1x\]
  2. Differentiate with the power rule. The derivative of \(1500x^{-1}\) is \(1500(-1)x^{-2}\), the constant 20 gives 0, and \(0.1x\) gives 0.1. \[\overline{C}'(x) = -1500x^{-2} + 0 + 0.1 = -\frac{1500}{x^2} + 0.1\]
  3. Check with the quotient rule formula \(\overline{C}'(x) = \dfrac{x\,C'(x) - C(x)}{x^2}\), using \(C'(x) = 20 + 0.2x\) from Worked example 3. Expand, keep the cost in parentheses, and distribute the minus sign. \[\begin{aligned} \overline{C}'(x) &= \frac{x(20 + 0.2x) - (1500 + 20x + 0.1x^2)}{x^2} \\ &= \frac{20x + 0.2x^2 - 1500 - 20x - 0.1x^2}{x^2} \\ &= \frac{0.1x^2 - 1500}{x^2} = 0.1 - \frac{1500}{x^2} \end{aligned}\] Both methods agree.
  4. Evaluate the average cost at \(x = 100\). \[\overline{C}(100) = \frac{1500}{100} + 20 + 0.1(100) = 15 + 20 + 10 = 45\] At a production level of 100 units, the average cost is $45 per unit.
  5. Evaluate the marginal average cost at \(x = 100\). \[\overline{C}'(100) = -\frac{1500}{100^2} + 0.1 = -\frac{1500}{10000} + 0.1 = -0.15 + 0.1 = -0.05\] The value is negative, so the average cost is decreasing. When production goes from 100 to 101 units, the average cost per unit drops by approximately $0.05, that is, about 5 cents.

Answer: \(\overline{C}(x) = \dfrac{1500}{x} + 20 + 0.1x\), \(\overline{C}'(x) = 0.1 - \dfrac{1500}{x^2}\). \(\overline{C}(100) = 45\): the average cost is $45 per unit. \(\overline{C}'(100) = -0.05\): one more unit lowers the average cost by about 5 cents per unit.

Common mistake: mixing up marginal cost and marginal average cost. \(C'(100) = 40\) is the approximate cost of the next unit. \(\overline{C}'(100) = -0.05\) is the approximate change in the cost per unit. They answer different questions and have different values.
If you get stuck
  • Rewrite first. Turn every root into a fractional power and every \(\dfrac{1}{x^n}\) into \(x^{-n}\) before you take any derivative.
  • Look at the bottom of a fraction. If it is one term, split the fraction into separate terms and use the power rule. If it has two or more terms, use the quotient rule.
  • For a product or a quotient, write the four pieces \(u\), \(v\), \(u'\), \(v'\) on their own line before you assemble anything.
  • In a quotient rule numerator, expand each product inside its own parentheses, and only then subtract. The minus sign must reach every term of the second product.
  • For a tangent line, make a two-line list: "point: \((a, f(a))\)" from \(f\), and "slope: \(f'(a)\)" from \(f'\). Then write \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\).
  • For a business question, build the function before you differentiate: \(R = x \cdot p\), \(P = R - C\), \(\overline{C} = \dfrac{C}{x}\). To check a derivative, plug \(x = 1\) into your answer and into a second method (such as multiplying out first).
Quick check
Is the derivative of \(\pi^3\) equal to \(3\pi^2\)?

No. \(\pi^3\) is a fixed number (about 31), so by the constant rule its derivative is 0. The power rule only applies when the variable \(x\) is the base.

Give an example showing that \((uv)' = u'v'\) is false.

Take \(u = x\) and \(v = x\). Then \(uv = x^2\) has derivative \(2x\), but \(u'v' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\), and \(1 \neq 2x\) (for example at \(x = 1\), \(2 \neq 1\)).

Which one can you differentiate without the quotient rule: \(\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x}\) or \(\dfrac{x}{x^2 + 1}\)?

The first one. Its bottom is a single term, so \(\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x} = x + x^{-1}\), with derivative \(1 - \dfrac{1}{x^2}\). The second one has a two-term bottom, which cannot be split, so it needs the quotient rule.

At 100 units, \(C'(100) = 40\) and \(\overline{C}(100) = 45\). Will one more unit raise or lower the average cost?

Lower it. The next unit costs about $40, which is less than the current average of $45, so it pulls the average down. In numbers, \(\overline{C}'(100) = \dfrac{100(40) - 4500}{100^2} = \dfrac{-500}{10000} = -0.05\), which is negative.

A company finds \(P'(200) = -3\). Should it make and sell the 201st unit if it wants more profit?

No. A negative marginal profit means the 201st unit would lower profit by about $3, so to raise profit the company should sell fewer units, not more.

What to have memorized
  • The four notations \(f'(x)\), \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(x)]\), and \(D_x[f(x)]\) all mean the derivative.
  • Constant rule: \(k' = 0\). Power rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[x^r\big] = r\,x^{r-1}\) for every real \(r\).
  • Rewriting: \(\dfrac{1}{x^n} = x^{-n}\), \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \(\sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{m/n}\). Rewrite before you differentiate.
  • Constant multiple rule \((kg)' = k\,g'\) and sum and difference rule \((f \pm g)' = f' \pm g'\).
  • Product rule: \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\). Write \(u\), \(v\), \(u'\), \(v'\) first. Never \(u'v'\).
  • Quotient rule: \(\left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\), "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared."
  • If the denominator is a single term, divide it into each numerator term and use the power rule instead.
  • Marginal means derivative. \(C'(a)\) is approximately the cost of the \((a+1)\)st unit, and the same for \(R'\) and \(P'\). Profit is \(P = R - C\), and revenue is \(R = x \cdot p\).
  • Average cost \(\overline{C}(x) = \dfrac{C(x)}{x}\), marginal average cost \(\overline{C}'(x) = \dfrac{x\,C'(x) - C(x)}{x^2}\).
  • Tangent line at \(x = a\): \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)\). Horizontal tangents are where \(f'(x) = 0\); get the \(y\)-values from \(f\).
  • Tangent parallel to a line of slope \(m\): solve \(f'(x) = m\). Perpendicular: solve \(f'(x) = -\dfrac{1}{m}\).

One idea at a time.
Problem 1
Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^5}\).
Show full solution
  1. Rewrite the fraction as a power of \(x\) so the power rule applies. Moving \(x^5\) from the denominator to the numerator flips the sign of the exponent. \[f(x) = \frac{1}{x^5} = x^{-5}\]
  2. Apply the power rule with \(r = -5\). The new exponent is \(-5 - 1 = -6\). \[f'(x) = -5x^{-5-1} = -5x^{-6}\]
  3. Rewrite with a positive exponent. \[f'(x) = -\frac{5}{x^6}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = -5x^{-6} = -\dfrac{5}{x^6}\)

Problem 2
Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = \sqrt[4]{x^3}\).
Show full solution
  1. Rewrite the root as a fractional power using \(\sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{m/n}\). \[y = \sqrt[4]{x^3} = x^{3/4}\]
  2. Apply the power rule with \(r = \tfrac{3}{4}\). The new exponent is \(\tfrac{3}{4} - 1 = \tfrac{3}{4} - \tfrac{4}{4} = -\tfrac{1}{4}\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3}{4}x^{3/4 - 1} = \frac{3}{4}x^{-1/4}\]
  3. Move the negative power to the denominator and write it as a root, using \(x^{1/4} = \sqrt[4]{x}\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3}{4x^{1/4}} = \frac{3}{4\sqrt[4]{x}}\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{3}{4}x^{-1/4} = \dfrac{3}{4\sqrt[4]{x}}\)

Problem 3
Find \(D_x\big[7x^3 - 4x^2 + 9x - 11\big]\).
Show full solution
  1. The symbol \(D_x\) means the derivative with respect to \(x\). By the sum and difference rule, differentiate each term separately. \[D_x\big[7x^3\big] - D_x\big[4x^2\big] + D_x[9x] - D_x[11]\]
  2. Use the constant multiple rule and the power rule on each power, and the constant rule on 11. \[7 \cdot 3x^2 - 4 \cdot 2x + 9 \cdot 1 - 0\]
  3. Multiply out the coefficients. \[21x^2 - 8x + 9\]

Answer: \(21x^2 - 8x + 9\)

Problem 4
The cost of producing \(x\) bicycle helmets is \(C(x) = 300 + 8x + 0.02x^2\) dollars. Find the marginal cost when 50 helmets have been produced, and say what it means.
Show full solution
  1. Marginal cost is the derivative of cost. Differentiate term by term with the constant, constant multiple, and power rules. \[C'(x) = 0 + 8 + 0.02 \cdot 2x = 8 + 0.04x\]
  2. Substitute \(x = 50\). \[C'(50) = 8 + 0.04(50) = 8 + 2 = 10\] When 50 helmets have been produced, the cost of producing the 51st helmet is approximately $10.

Answer: \(C'(50) = 10\). Producing the 51st helmet costs about $10.

Problem 5
Use the product rule to find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = (x^2 + 3)(x - 4)\).
Show full solution
  1. Name the two factors and differentiate each. \[u = x^2 + 3, \qquad v = x - 4, \qquad u' = 2x, \qquad v' = 1\]
  2. Apply the product rule \(y' = u'v + uv'\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 2x(x - 4) + (x^2 + 3)(1)\]
  3. Distribute and combine like terms. \[\begin{aligned} \frac{dy}{dx} &= 2x^2 - 8x + x^2 + 3 \\ &= 3x^2 - 8x + 3 \end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 - 8x + 3\)

Problem 6
Use the quotient rule to find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x + 5}\).
Show full solution
  1. Name the top and bottom and differentiate each. \[u = x, \qquad v = x + 5, \qquad u' = 1, \qquad v' = 1\]
  2. Apply the quotient rule \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\): low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared. \[f'(x) = \frac{1 \cdot (x + 5) - x \cdot 1}{(x + 5)^2}\]
  3. Simplify the numerator: \(x + 5 - x = 5\). \[f'(x) = \frac{5}{(x + 5)^2}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{5}{(x + 5)^2}\)

Problem 7
Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = 6\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{10}{x^2} + \dfrac{2}{\sqrt[3]{x}}\).
Show full solution
  1. Rewrite every term as a constant times a power of \(x\). Use \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \(\dfrac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}\), and \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x}} = \dfrac{1}{x^{1/3}} = x^{-1/3}\). \[f(x) = 6x^{1/2} - 10x^{-2} + 2x^{-1/3}\]
  2. Differentiate term by term with the constant multiple rule and the power rule. The new exponents are \(\tfrac{1}{2} - 1 = -\tfrac{1}{2}\), \(-2 - 1 = -3\), and \(-\tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{4}{3}\). \[f'(x) = 6 \cdot \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} - 10 \cdot (-2)x^{-3} + 2 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)x^{-4/3}\]
  3. Multiply out the coefficients. The middle term becomes positive because \(-10 \cdot (-2) = 20\). \[f'(x) = 3x^{-1/2} + 20x^{-3} - \frac{2}{3}x^{-4/3}\]
  4. Rewrite with positive exponents. Since \(x^{4/3} = x \cdot x^{1/3} = x\sqrt[3]{x}\), the last term is \(\dfrac{2}{3x\sqrt[3]{x}}\). \[f'(x) = \frac{3}{\sqrt{x}} + \frac{20}{x^3} - \frac{2}{3x\sqrt[3]{x}}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = 3x^{-1/2} + 20x^{-3} - \dfrac{2}{3}x^{-4/3} = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{x}} + \dfrac{20}{x^3} - \dfrac{2}{3x\sqrt[3]{x}}\)

Problem 8
Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 - 5x + 3}{\sqrt{x}}\) without using the quotient rule.
Show full solution
  1. The denominator \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) is a single term, so divide it into each term of the numerator. Subtract exponents when dividing powers: \(x^2 / x^{1/2} = x^{3/2}\), \(x / x^{1/2} = x^{1/2}\), and \(1 / x^{1/2} = x^{-1/2}\). \[f(x) = \frac{2x^2}{x^{1/2}} - \frac{5x}{x^{1/2}} + \frac{3}{x^{1/2}} = 2x^{3/2} - 5x^{1/2} + 3x^{-1/2}\]
  2. Differentiate term by term with the power rule. The new exponents are \(\tfrac{3}{2} - 1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\), \(\tfrac{1}{2} - 1 = -\tfrac{1}{2}\), and \(-\tfrac{1}{2} - 1 = -\tfrac{3}{2}\). \[f'(x) = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2} - 5 \cdot \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} + 3 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{-3/2}\]
  3. Multiply out the coefficients. \[f'(x) = 3x^{1/2} - \frac{5}{2}x^{-1/2} - \frac{3}{2}x^{-3/2}\]
  4. Rewrite with roots, using \(x^{3/2} = x\sqrt{x}\). \[f'(x) = 3\sqrt{x} - \frac{5}{2\sqrt{x}} - \frac{3}{2x\sqrt{x}}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = 3x^{1/2} - \dfrac{5}{2}x^{-1/2} - \dfrac{3}{2}x^{-3/2} = 3\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{5}{2\sqrt{x}} - \dfrac{3}{2x\sqrt{x}}\)

Problem 9
Use the product rule to find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = (4x^2 - 1)(\sqrt{x} + 2)\), and simplify.
Show full solution
  1. Name the factors, rewrite \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), and differentiate each factor with the power rule. \[u = 4x^2 - 1, \qquad v = x^{1/2} + 2, \qquad u' = 8x, \qquad v' = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  2. Apply the product rule \(y' = u'v + uv'\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 8x\left(x^{1/2} + 2\right) + \left(4x^2 - 1\right)\cdot\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  3. Distribute both products, adding exponents: \(x \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{3/2}\) and \(x^2 \cdot x^{-1/2} = x^{3/2}\). Also \(4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 2\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 8x^{3/2} + 16x + 2x^{3/2} - \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  4. Combine the two \(x^{3/2}\) terms: \(8 + 2 = 10\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 10x^{3/2} + 16x - \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 10x^{3/2} + 16x - \dfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = 10x\sqrt{x} + 16x - \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\)

Problem 10
Use the quotient rule to find \(f'(t)\) for \(f(t) = \dfrac{3t^2 - 1}{t^2 + 2}\), and simplify.
Show full solution
  1. Name the top and bottom and differentiate each with respect to \(t\). \[u = 3t^2 - 1, \qquad v = t^2 + 2, \qquad u' = 6t, \qquad v' = 2t\]
  2. Apply the quotient rule \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). \[f'(t) = \frac{6t\left(t^2 + 2\right) - \left(3t^2 - 1\right)(2t)}{\left(t^2 + 2\right)^2}\]
  3. Expand each product in the numerator. \[6t\left(t^2 + 2\right) = 6t^3 + 12t, \qquad \left(3t^2 - 1\right)(2t) = 6t^3 - 2t\]
  4. Subtract, distributing the minus sign to both terms of the second product. Note that \(-(-2t) = +2t\). \[\begin{aligned} 6t^3 + 12t - \left(6t^3 - 2t\right) &= 6t^3 + 12t - 6t^3 + 2t \\ &= 14t \end{aligned}\]
  5. Put the numerator over the denominator. \[f'(t) = \frac{14t}{\left(t^2 + 2\right)^2}\]

Answer: \(f'(t) = \dfrac{14t}{\left(t^2 + 2\right)^2}\)

Problem 11
Find an equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x) = \dfrac{x + 3}{x - 1}\) at the point where \(x = 3\).
Show full solution
  1. Find the point of tangency by substituting \(x = 3\) into \(f\). \[f(3) = \frac{3 + 3}{3 - 1} = \frac{6}{2} = 3\] The point is \((3, 3)\).
  2. Differentiate with the quotient rule. The pieces are \(u = x + 3\), \(v = x - 1\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = 1\). \[f'(x) = \frac{1 \cdot (x - 1) - (x + 3) \cdot 1}{(x - 1)^2}\]
  3. Simplify the numerator, distributing the minus sign. \[(x - 1) - (x + 3) = x - 1 - x - 3 = -4, \qquad \text{so} \qquad f'(x) = \frac{-4}{(x - 1)^2}\]
  4. The slope of the tangent line is \(f'(3)\). \[f'(3) = \frac{-4}{(3 - 1)^2} = \frac{-4}{4} = -1\]
  5. Write the point-slope form with \((x_1, y_1) = (3, 3)\) and \(m = -1\), then solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - 3 &= -1(x - 3) \\ y - 3 &= -x + 3 \\ y &= -x + 6 \end{aligned}\] Check: at \(x = 3\) the line gives \(-3 + 6 = 3 = f(3)\).

Answer: \(y = -x + 6\)

Problem 12
A company sells \(x\) speakers at a price of \(p = 80 - 0.02x\) dollars each, and its cost of producing \(x\) speakers is \(C(x) = 4000 + 20x + 0.01x^2\) dollars. (a) Find the marginal revenue \(R'(500)\). (b) Find the marginal profit function. (c) Find \(P'(500)\) and \(P'(1500)\) and interpret both.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Revenue is the number of units times the price. Substitute the demand function and distribute. \[R(x) = x \cdot p = x(80 - 0.02x) = 80x - 0.02x^2\]
  2. (a) Differentiate with the power rule and substitute \(x = 500\). \[\begin{aligned} R'(x) &= 80 - 0.04x \\ R'(500) &= 80 - 0.04(500) = 80 - 20 = 60 \end{aligned}\] Selling the 501st speaker increases revenue by approximately $60.
  3. (b) Profit is revenue minus cost. Put the cost in parentheses, distribute the minus sign, and combine like terms. \[\begin{aligned} P(x) &= (80x - 0.02x^2) - (4000 + 20x + 0.01x^2) \\ &= 80x - 0.02x^2 - 4000 - 20x - 0.01x^2 \\ &= -0.03x^2 + 60x - 4000 \end{aligned}\]
  4. (b) Differentiate with the power rule to get the marginal profit. \[P'(x) = -0.06x + 60\]
  5. (c) Substitute \(x = 500\) and \(x = 1500\). \[\begin{aligned} P'(500) &= -0.06(500) + 60 = -30 + 60 = 30 \\ P'(1500) &= -0.06(1500) + 60 = -90 + 60 = -30 \end{aligned}\] When 500 speakers have been sold, selling one more increases profit by approximately $30. When 1500 speakers have been sold, selling one more decreases profit by approximately $30.

Answer: (a) \(R'(500) = 60\). (b) \(P'(x) = 60 - 0.06x\). (c) \(P'(500) = 30\): the 501st speaker adds about $30 of profit. \(P'(1500) = -30\): the 1501st speaker lowers profit by about $30.

Problem 13
Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{x + 4}\) for \(x \gt 0\). Find \(f'(x)\), write it as a single simplified fraction, and find the point on the graph where the tangent line is horizontal.
Hint
The bottom \(x + 4\) has two terms, so this is a quotient rule problem. After you assemble it, clear the small fraction \(\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\) by multiplying the top and bottom by \(2\sqrt{x}\).
Show full solution
  1. The denominator \(x + 4\) has two terms, so use the quotient rule. Rewrite \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) and name the pieces. \[u = x^{1/2}, \qquad v = x + 4, \qquad u' = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}, \qquad v' = 1\]
  2. Apply the quotient rule \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). \[f'(x) = \frac{\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}(x + 4) - x^{1/2}\cdot 1}{(x + 4)^2}\]
  3. The numerator contains the fraction \(\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\). Clear it by multiplying the numerator and the denominator by \(2x^{1/2}\). In the numerator, \(2x^{1/2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}(x + 4) = x + 4\) and \(2x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/2} = 2x\). \[f'(x) = \frac{(x + 4) - 2x}{2x^{1/2}(x + 4)^2}\]
  4. Simplify the numerator: \(x + 4 - 2x = 4 - x\). \[f'(x) = \frac{4 - x}{2\sqrt{x}\,(x + 4)^2}\]
  5. The tangent line is horizontal where \(f'(x) = 0\). A fraction is zero when its numerator is zero and its denominator is not. The denominator is positive for every \(x \gt 0\). \[4 - x = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 4\]
  6. Find the \(y\)-coordinate with the original function. \[f(4) = \frac{\sqrt{4}}{4 + 4} = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{4 - x}{2\sqrt{x}\,(x + 4)^2}\). The tangent line is horizontal at \(\left(4, \tfrac{1}{4}\right)\).

Where students lose points: Students often leave a fraction inside the numerator and then cannot see where \(f'(x) = 0\). The other common slip is computing the \(y\)-value with \(f'\) instead of \(f\); the point is \(\left(4, f(4)\right)\).

Problem 14
The cost of producing \(x\) units of a product is \(C(x) = 0.1x^2 + 6x + 1000\) dollars. (a) Find the average cost function \(\overline{C}(x)\) and the marginal average cost function \(\overline{C}'(x)\). (b) Compute \(\overline{C}'(50)\) and \(\overline{C}'(200)\) and say what each sign means. (c) Find the production level where the marginal average cost is 0, and show that at that level the marginal cost equals the average cost.
Hint
Divide each term of \(C(x)\) by \(x\) before you differentiate. For part (c), set \(\overline{C}'(x) = 0\) and multiply both sides by \(x^2\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Average cost is cost divided by \(x\). The denominator \(x\) is a single term, so split the fraction. \[\overline{C}(x) = \frac{0.1x^2 + 6x + 1000}{x} = \frac{0.1x^2}{x} + \frac{6x}{x} + \frac{1000}{x} = 0.1x + 6 + 1000x^{-1}\]
  2. (a) Differentiate with the power rule. The derivative of \(1000x^{-1}\) is \(1000(-1)x^{-2} = -1000x^{-2}\). \[\overline{C}'(x) = 0.1 + 0 - 1000x^{-2} = 0.1 - \frac{1000}{x^2}\]
  3. (b) Substitute \(x = 50\) and \(x = 200\). \[\begin{aligned} \overline{C}'(50) &= 0.1 - \frac{1000}{2500} = 0.1 - 0.4 = -0.3 \\ \overline{C}'(200) &= 0.1 - \frac{1000}{40000} = 0.1 - 0.025 = 0.075 \end{aligned}\] At 50 units the marginal average cost is negative, so producing one more unit lowers the average cost by about $0.30 per unit. At 200 units it is positive, so one more unit raises the average cost by about $0.075 per unit.
  4. (c) Set the marginal average cost equal to 0 and solve. Multiply both sides by \(x^2\), which is allowed because \(x \gt 0\). \[\begin{aligned} 0.1 - \frac{1000}{x^2} &= 0 \\ 0.1 &= \frac{1000}{x^2} \\ 0.1x^2 &= 1000 \\ x^2 &= 10000 \end{aligned}\] Since a production level is positive, \(x = 100\).
  5. (c) Compute the average cost at \(x = 100\). \[\overline{C}(100) = 0.1(100) + 6 + \frac{1000}{100} = 10 + 6 + 10 = 26\]
  6. (c) Compute the marginal cost. Differentiate \(C\), then substitute \(x = 100\). \[\begin{aligned} C'(x) &= 0.2x + 6 \\ C'(100) &= 0.2(100) + 6 = 20 + 6 = 26 \end{aligned}\] Both equal 26, so at the production level where the marginal average cost is 0, the marginal cost equals the average cost.

Answer: (a) \(\overline{C}(x) = 0.1x + 6 + \dfrac{1000}{x}\), \(\overline{C}'(x) = 0.1 - \dfrac{1000}{x^2}\). (b) \(\overline{C}'(50) = -0.3\) (average cost falling), \(\overline{C}'(200) = 0.075\) (average cost rising). (c) \(x = 100\) units, where \(C'(100) = \overline{C}(100) = 26\) dollars.

Where students lose points: Using the quotient rule without care gives sign errors; splitting first is safer. In part (c), writing \(x = \pm 100\) and keeping \(-100\) loses a point, since a production level cannot be negative.

Problem 15
A small theater's monthly revenue is \(R(t) = p(t)\,q(t)\), where \(p(t)\) is the ticket price in dollars and \(q(t)\) is the number of tickets sold per month, \(t\) months from now. Right now the price is $12 and is rising at $0.50 per month, and the theater sells 800 tickets per month, a number that is falling by 20 tickets per month. How fast is the monthly revenue changing right now? Is it increasing or decreasing?
Hint
Revenue is price times quantity, and both change with time, so the product rule gives \(R' = p'q + pq'\). A quantity that is falling has a negative rate.
Show full solution
  1. Write down what is known at the present time \(t = 0\). A rate that is falling is a negative derivative. \[p = 12, \qquad p' = 0.50 \text{ dollars per month}, \qquad q = 800, \qquad q' = -20 \text{ tickets per month}\]
  2. Revenue is a product of two functions of \(t\), so use the product rule with \(u = p(t)\) and \(v = q(t)\). \[R'(t) = p'(t)\,q(t) + p(t)\,q'(t)\]
  3. Substitute the known values. \[R'(0) = (0.50)(800) + (12)(-20) = 400 - 240 = 160\] The first term, $400, is the gain from charging more for the same 800 tickets. The second term, \(-\$240\), is the loss from selling 20 fewer tickets at $12 each.

Answer: The revenue is increasing at $160 per month.

Where students lose points: Writing \(q' = +20\) because the problem says "20 tickets" gives \(400 + 240 = 640\), which is wrong. Falling means \(q' = -20\). Also, \(R' = p'q'\) (here \(-10\)) is the wrong rule.

Problem 16
Let \(g(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x} + 1}{\sqrt{x} - 1}\). (a) Find \(g'(x)\) and simplify. (b) Find \(g'(4)\). (c) Find an equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(g\) at \(x = 9\).
Hint
The top and bottom have the same derivative. After the quotient rule, factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\) out of the whole numerator.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Use the quotient rule. Rewrite \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\); the top and bottom have the same derivative. \[u = x^{1/2} + 1, \qquad v = x^{1/2} - 1, \qquad u' = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}, \qquad v' = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\]
  2. (a) Apply \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). \[g'(x) = \frac{\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\left(x^{1/2} - 1\right) - \left(x^{1/2} + 1\right)\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}}{\left(x^{1/2} - 1\right)^2}\]
  3. (a) Both terms of the numerator contain the factor \(\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\). Factor it out, then simplify the bracket, distributing the minus sign. \[\begin{aligned} \text{numerator} &= \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\Big[\left(x^{1/2} - 1\right) - \left(x^{1/2} + 1\right)\Big] \\ &= \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}\Big[x^{1/2} - 1 - x^{1/2} - 1\Big] \\ &= \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}(-2) = -x^{-1/2} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} \end{aligned}\]
  4. (a) Put the numerator over the denominator. Dividing by \(\sqrt{x}\) inside the numerator moves \(\sqrt{x}\) into the denominator. \[g'(x) = \frac{-\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}}{\left(\sqrt{x} - 1\right)^2} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}\left(\sqrt{x} - 1\right)^2}\]
  5. (b) Substitute \(x = 4\), using \(\sqrt{4} = 2\). \[g'(4) = -\frac{1}{2(2 - 1)^2} = -\frac{1}{2 \cdot 1} = -\frac{1}{2}\]
  6. (c) Find the point of tangency, using \(\sqrt{9} = 3\). \[g(9) = \frac{3 + 1}{3 - 1} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\] The point is \((9, 2)\).
  7. (c) Find the slope. \[g'(9) = -\frac{1}{3(3 - 1)^2} = -\frac{1}{3 \cdot 4} = -\frac{1}{12}\]
  8. (c) Write the point-slope form and solve for \(y\). Note \(\tfrac{9}{12} = \tfrac{3}{4}\) and \(2 = \tfrac{8}{4}\). \[\begin{aligned} y - 2 &= -\frac{1}{12}(x - 9) \\ y - 2 &= -\frac{1}{12}x + \frac{3}{4} \\ y &= -\frac{1}{12}x + \frac{3}{4} + \frac{8}{4} = -\frac{1}{12}x + \frac{11}{4} \end{aligned}\] Check: at \(x = 9\) the line gives \(-\tfrac{9}{12} + \tfrac{11}{4} = -\tfrac{3}{4} + \tfrac{11}{4} = \tfrac{8}{4} = 2 = g(9)\).

Answer: (a) \(g'(x) = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}\left(\sqrt{x} - 1\right)^2}\). (b) \(g'(4) = -\dfrac{1}{2}\). (c) \(y = -\dfrac{1}{12}x + \dfrac{11}{4}\).

Where students lose points: The minus sign in front of the second product must reach the \(+1\) inside \(x^{1/2} + 1\); missing it gives a numerator of 0. In part (c), students sometimes use \(g'(4)\) from part (b) as the slope at \(x = 9\).

Problem 17
The demand function for a craft kit is \(p = 50 - \sqrt{x}\), where \(p\) is the price in dollars at which \(x\) kits can be sold. The cost of producing \(x\) kits is \(C(x) = 100 + 8x\) dollars. (a) Find the marginal profit function. (b) Find \(P'(100)\) and \(P'(900)\) and interpret them. (c) At what sales level is the marginal profit zero?
Hint
Build \(R(x) = x \cdot p\) and then \(P = R - C\) before differentiating. Write \(x\sqrt{x}\) as \(x^{3/2}\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Revenue is units times price. Distribute and write \(x\sqrt{x} = x^1 \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{3/2}\). \[R(x) = x\left(50 - x^{1/2}\right) = 50x - x^{3/2}\]
  2. (a) Profit is revenue minus cost. Distribute the minus sign and combine \(50x - 8x = 42x\). \[\begin{aligned} P(x) &= \left(50x - x^{3/2}\right) - (100 + 8x) \\ &= 50x - x^{3/2} - 100 - 8x \\ &= 42x - x^{3/2} - 100 \end{aligned}\]
  3. (a) Differentiate with the power rule. The derivative of \(x^{3/2}\) is \(\tfrac{3}{2}x^{1/2}\). \[P'(x) = 42 - \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2} = 42 - \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\]
  4. (b) Substitute \(x = 100\) and \(x = 900\), using \(\sqrt{100} = 10\) and \(\sqrt{900} = 30\). \[\begin{aligned} P'(100) &= 42 - \frac{3}{2}(10) = 42 - 15 = 27 \\ P'(900) &= 42 - \frac{3}{2}(30) = 42 - 45 = -3 \end{aligned}\] When 100 kits have been sold, selling one more increases profit by approximately $27. When 900 kits have been sold, selling one more decreases profit by approximately $3.
  5. (c) Set the marginal profit equal to 0 and solve for \(\sqrt{x}\), then square. \[\begin{aligned} 42 - \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x} &= 0 \\ \frac{3}{2}\sqrt{x} &= 42 \\ \sqrt{x} &= 42 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = 28 \\ x &= 28^2 = 784 \end{aligned}\]

Answer: (a) \(P'(x) = 42 - \dfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{x}\). (b) \(P'(100) = 27\): the 101st kit adds about $27 of profit. \(P'(900) = -3\): the 901st kit lowers profit by about $3. (c) The marginal profit is zero at \(x = 784\) kits.

Problem 18
Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 8}{x - 1}\). (a) Find \(f'(x)\) and write its numerator in factored form. (b) Find all points on the graph of \(f\) where the tangent line is horizontal. (c) Find an equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at \(x = 2\).
Hint
Quotient rule with \(u = x^2 + 8\) and \(v = x - 1\). Simplify the numerator to a quadratic and factor it; the factored form answers part (b) and makes part (c) quick.
Show full solution
  1. (a) The denominator has two terms, so use the quotient rule. Name the pieces. \[u = x^2 + 8, \qquad v = x - 1, \qquad u' = 2x, \qquad v' = 1\]
  2. (a) Apply \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\): low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared. \[f'(x) = \frac{2x(x - 1) - \left(x^2 + 8\right)(1)}{(x - 1)^2}\]
  3. (a) Expand the numerator and distribute the minus sign over both terms of \(x^2 + 8\). \[\begin{aligned} 2x(x - 1) - \left(x^2 + 8\right) &= 2x^2 - 2x - x^2 - 8 \\ &= x^2 - 2x - 8 \end{aligned}\]
  4. (a) Factor the quadratic. Two numbers that multiply to \(-8\) and add to \(-2\) are \(-4\) and \(2\). \[x^2 - 2x - 8 = (x - 4)(x + 2), \qquad \text{so} \qquad f'(x) = \frac{(x - 4)(x + 2)}{(x - 1)^2}\]
  5. (b) The tangent line is horizontal where \(f'(x) = 0\). Set the numerator equal to 0. Neither solution makes the denominator zero, since the denominator is zero only at \(x = 1\). \[(x - 4)(x + 2) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = 4 \ \text{ or } \ x = -2\]
  6. (b) Find the \(y\)-coordinates with the original function \(f\). \[f(4) = \frac{16 + 8}{4 - 1} = \frac{24}{3} = 8, \qquad f(-2) = \frac{4 + 8}{-2 - 1} = \frac{12}{-3} = -4\]
  7. (c) Find the point of tangency at \(x = 2\). \[f(2) = \frac{4 + 8}{2 - 1} = \frac{12}{1} = 12\] The point is \((2, 12)\).
  8. (c) Find the slope, using the factored derivative. \[f'(2) = \frac{(2 - 4)(2 + 2)}{(2 - 1)^2} = \frac{(-2)(4)}{1} = -8\]
  9. (c) Write the point-slope form and solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - 12 &= -8(x - 2) \\ y - 12 &= -8x + 16 \\ y &= -8x + 28 \end{aligned}\] Check: at \(x = 2\) the line gives \(-16 + 28 = 12 = f(2)\).

Answer: (a) \(f'(x) = \dfrac{(x - 4)(x + 2)}{(x - 1)^2}\). (b) Horizontal tangents at \((4, 8)\) and \((-2, -4)\). (c) \(y = -8x + 28\).

Where students lose points: The usual error is expanding \(2x(x - 1) - (x^2 + 8)\) as \(2x^2 - 2x - x^2 + 8\), which gives an unfactorable numerator. Also, the \(y\)-values in part (b) must come from \(f\), not from \(f'\).

Problem 19
Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{(2x + 1)(x - 3)}{x^2}\). (a) Find \(f'(x)\) using the quotient rule, with the product rule for the derivative of the numerator, and simplify. (b) Find \(f'(x)\) again by simplifying \(f\) first, and confirm the two answers agree. (c) Find the point on the graph where the tangent line is horizontal.
Hint
For part (a), the top is itself a product, so find \(u'\) with the product rule first. For part (b), multiply out the top and divide every term by \(x^2\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Let \(u = (2x + 1)(x - 3)\) be the numerator. Its derivative needs the product rule, with first factor \(2x + 1\) (derivative 2) and second factor \(x - 3\) (derivative 1). \[u' = 2(x - 3) + (2x + 1)(1) = 2x - 6 + 2x + 1 = 4x - 5\]
  2. (a) Expand \(u\) as well, since it will be needed in the quotient rule. \[u = (2x + 1)(x - 3) = 2x^2 - 6x + x - 3 = 2x^2 - 5x - 3\]
  3. (a) The denominator is \(v = x^2\) with \(v' = 2x\). Apply the quotient rule \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\), noting \(v^2 = \left(x^2\right)^2 = x^4\). \[f'(x) = \frac{(4x - 5)x^2 - \left(2x^2 - 5x - 3\right)(2x)}{x^4}\]
  4. (a) Expand each product in the numerator. \[(4x - 5)x^2 = 4x^3 - 5x^2, \qquad \left(2x^2 - 5x - 3\right)(2x) = 4x^3 - 10x^2 - 6x\]
  5. (a) Subtract, distributing the minus sign to all three terms of the second product. \[\begin{aligned} 4x^3 - 5x^2 - \left(4x^3 - 10x^2 - 6x\right) &= 4x^3 - 5x^2 - 4x^3 + 10x^2 + 6x \\ &= 5x^2 + 6x \end{aligned}\]
  6. (a) Put it over \(x^4\), factor \(x\) out of the numerator, and cancel one factor of \(x\) (valid since \(x \neq 0\) in the domain). \[f'(x) = \frac{5x^2 + 6x}{x^4} = \frac{x(5x + 6)}{x^4} = \frac{5x + 6}{x^3}\]
  7. (b) Simplify first. The denominator \(x^2\) is a single term, so divide it into each term of the expanded numerator. \[f(x) = \frac{2x^2 - 5x - 3}{x^2} = \frac{2x^2}{x^2} - \frac{5x}{x^2} - \frac{3}{x^2} = 2 - 5x^{-1} - 3x^{-2}\]
  8. (b) Differentiate with the power rule. Note \(-5 \cdot (-1) = 5\) and \(-3 \cdot (-2) = 6\). \[f'(x) = 0 - 5(-1)x^{-2} - 3(-2)x^{-3} = 5x^{-2} + 6x^{-3} = \frac{5}{x^2} + \frac{6}{x^3}\]
  9. (b) Combine over the common denominator \(x^3\) to compare with part (a). \[\frac{5}{x^2} + \frac{6}{x^3} = \frac{5x}{x^3} + \frac{6}{x^3} = \frac{5x + 6}{x^3}\] This matches part (a).
  10. (c) Set \(f'(x) = 0\). The numerator must be zero, and \(x = -\tfrac{6}{5}\) does not make the denominator zero. \[5x + 6 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = -\frac{6}{5}\]
  11. (c) Find the \(y\)-coordinate with the simplified form \(f(x) = 2 - \dfrac{5}{x} - \dfrac{3}{x^2}\). Here \(\dfrac{5}{-6/5} = -\dfrac{25}{6}\) and \(\left(-\tfrac{6}{5}\right)^2 = \tfrac{36}{25}\), so \(\dfrac{3}{36/25} = \dfrac{75}{36} = \dfrac{25}{12}\). \[\begin{aligned} f\left(-\tfrac{6}{5}\right) &= 2 - \left(-\frac{25}{6}\right) - \frac{25}{12} = 2 + \frac{25}{6} - \frac{25}{12} \\ &= \frac{24}{12} + \frac{50}{12} - \frac{25}{12} = \frac{49}{12} \end{aligned}\]

Answer: (a) and (b) \(f'(x) = \dfrac{5x + 6}{x^3} = \dfrac{5}{x^2} + \dfrac{6}{x^3}\). (c) The tangent line is horizontal at \(\left(-\tfrac{6}{5}, \tfrac{49}{12}\right)\).

Where students lose points: Students write the derivative of \((2x + 1)(x - 3)\) as \(2 \cdot 1 = 2\), which is the false rule \((uv)' = u'v'\). In part (c), the \(y\)-coordinate needs careful fraction work: \(\left(-\tfrac{6}{5}\right)^2 = \tfrac{36}{25}\) is positive.

Problem 20
The total cost, in dollars, of producing \(x\) custom chairs is \(C(x) = \dfrac{50x^2 + 1000x}{x + 10}\). (a) Find the marginal cost \(C'(10)\) and interpret it. (b) Find the average cost function in simplest form, and the marginal average cost function. (c) Compute \(\overline{C}(10)\) and \(\overline{C}'(10)\) and interpret both.
Hint
For part (a), use the quotient rule on \(C(x)\). For part (b), divide by \(x\) and cancel the common factor \(x\) from the top before you differentiate.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Use the quotient rule. Name the pieces. \[u = 50x^2 + 1000x, \qquad v = x + 10, \qquad u' = 100x + 1000, \qquad v' = 1\]
  2. (a) Apply \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). \[C'(x) = \frac{(100x + 1000)(x + 10) - \left(50x^2 + 1000x\right)(1)}{(x + 10)^2}\]
  3. (a) Expand the first product. \[(100x + 1000)(x + 10) = 100x^2 + 1000x + 1000x + 10000 = 100x^2 + 2000x + 10000\]
  4. (a) Subtract the second part, distributing the minus sign, and combine like terms. \[\begin{aligned} 100x^2 + 2000x + 10000 - 50x^2 - 1000x &= 50x^2 + 1000x + 10000 \end{aligned}\] So \[C'(x) = \frac{50x^2 + 1000x + 10000}{(x + 10)^2}\]
  5. (a) Substitute \(x = 10\). \[C'(10) = \frac{50(100) + 1000(10) + 10000}{(20)^2} = \frac{5000 + 10000 + 10000}{400} = \frac{25000}{400} = 62.5\] When 10 chairs have been produced, the cost of producing the 11th chair is approximately $62.50.
  6. (b) Average cost is cost divided by \(x\). Factor \(x\) out of the numerator of \(C(x)\) and cancel it. \[\overline{C}(x) = \frac{C(x)}{x} = \frac{50x^2 + 1000x}{x(x + 10)} = \frac{x(50x + 1000)}{x(x + 10)} = \frac{50x + 1000}{x + 10}\]
  7. (b) Differentiate with the quotient rule. The pieces are \(u = 50x + 1000\), \(v = x + 10\), \(u' = 50\), \(v' = 1\). \[\overline{C}'(x) = \frac{50(x + 10) - (50x + 1000)(1)}{(x + 10)^2}\]
  8. (b) Simplify the numerator, distributing the minus sign. \[50x + 500 - 50x - 1000 = -500, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \overline{C}'(x) = \frac{-500}{(x + 10)^2}\]
  9. (c) Evaluate both at \(x = 10\). \[\overline{C}(10) = \frac{500 + 1000}{20} = \frac{1500}{20} = 75, \qquad \overline{C}'(10) = \frac{-500}{20^2} = \frac{-500}{400} = -1.25\] As a check, \(C(10) = \dfrac{5000 + 10000}{20} = \dfrac{15000}{20} = 750\), and \(\dfrac{750}{10} = 75\).
  10. (c) Interpret. At a production level of 10 chairs, the average cost is $75 per chair. Because \(\overline{C}'(10) = -1.25\) is negative, producing one more chair (going from 10 to 11) lowers the average cost by approximately $1.25 per chair.

Answer: (a) \(C'(10) = 62.5\): the 11th chair costs about $62.50. (b) \(\overline{C}(x) = \dfrac{50x + 1000}{x + 10}\), \(\overline{C}'(x) = \dfrac{-500}{(x + 10)^2}\). (c) \(\overline{C}(10) = 75\) dollars per chair; \(\overline{C}'(10) = -1.25\), so one more chair lowers the average cost by about $1.25 per chair.

Where students lose points: Mixing up the two questions: \(C'(10)\) is the cost of the next chair, while \(\overline{C}'(10)\) is the change in the cost per chair. Students also forget to divide by \(x\) and report \(C'\) when asked for \(\overline{C}'\).

Problem 21
The concentration of a medicine in a patient's blood \(t\) hours after an injection is \(c(t) = \dfrac{20t}{t^2 + 4}\) milligrams per liter. (a) Find \(c'(t)\) and simplify. (b) Find \(c'(1)\) and \(c'(4)\), and explain what each one says about the concentration. (c) At what time is the tangent line to the graph of \(c\) horizontal, and what is the concentration then?
Hint
Quotient rule with \(u = 20t\) and \(v = t^2 + 4\). Factor the simplified numerator; the sign of \(4 - t^2\) tells you rising or falling.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Use the quotient rule. Name the pieces. \[u = 20t, \qquad v = t^2 + 4, \qquad u' = 20, \qquad v' = 2t\]
  2. (a) Apply \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). \[c'(t) = \frac{20\left(t^2 + 4\right) - 20t(2t)}{\left(t^2 + 4\right)^2}\]
  3. (a) Expand each product in the numerator, then subtract and factor out 20. \[20\left(t^2 + 4\right) = 20t^2 + 80, \qquad 20t(2t) = 40t^2\] \[\begin{aligned} 20t^2 + 80 - 40t^2 &= -20t^2 + 80 = 20\left(4 - t^2\right) \end{aligned}\] So \[c'(t) = \frac{20\left(4 - t^2\right)}{\left(t^2 + 4\right)^2}\]
  4. (b) Substitute \(t = 1\). \[c'(1) = \frac{20(4 - 1)}{(1 + 4)^2} = \frac{60}{25} = \frac{12}{5}\] One hour after the injection, the concentration is increasing at \(\tfrac{12}{5} = 2.4\) milligrams per liter per hour.
  5. (b) Substitute \(t = 4\). \[c'(4) = \frac{20(4 - 16)}{(16 + 4)^2} = \frac{20(-12)}{400} = \frac{-240}{400} = -\frac{3}{5}\] Four hours after the injection, the concentration is decreasing at \(\tfrac{3}{5} = 0.6\) milligrams per liter per hour.
  6. (c) The tangent line is horizontal where \(c'(t) = 0\). The denominator is never zero, so set the numerator equal to 0. Time is not negative, so reject \(t = -2\). \[\begin{aligned} 20\left(4 - t^2\right) &= 0 \\ t^2 &= 4 \\ t &= 2 \quad (t \geq 0) \end{aligned}\]
  7. (c) Find the concentration at \(t = 2\) with the original function. \[c(2) = \frac{20(2)}{4 + 4} = \frac{40}{8} = 5\]

Answer: (a) \(c'(t) = \dfrac{20\left(4 - t^2\right)}{\left(t^2 + 4\right)^2}\). (b) \(c'(1) = \dfrac{12}{5}\): rising at 2.4 mg/L per hour; \(c'(4) = -\dfrac{3}{5}\): falling at 0.6 mg/L per hour. (c) At \(t = 2\) hours, when the concentration is 5 mg/L.

Where students lose points: Interpreting \(c'(4) = -\tfrac{3}{5}\) as "the concentration is negative" is wrong; the concentration is 4 mg/L and it is falling. In part (c), keeping \(t = -2\) loses a point because time starts at 0.

Problem 22
Let \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2\). (a) Find all points on the graph of \(f\) where the tangent line is parallel to the line \(y = 9x - 4\). (b) Find all points where the tangent line is perpendicular to the line \(x + 3y = 6\).
Hint
Parallel lines have equal slopes, so set \(f'(x)\) equal to the slope of the given line. For part (b), solve the line for \(y\) to read its slope, then use the negative reciprocal.
Show full solution
  1. Differentiate \(f\) with the power rule term by term. The constant 2 has derivative 0. \[f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x\]
  2. (a) The line \(y = 9x - 4\) has slope 9. A tangent line is parallel to it exactly when its slope is also 9, so set \(f'(x) = 9\). \[\begin{aligned} 3x^2 - 6x &= 9 \\ 3x^2 - 6x - 9 &= 0 \\ 3\left(x^2 - 2x - 3\right) &= 0 \\ 3(x - 3)(x + 1) &= 0 \end{aligned}\] So \(x = 3\) or \(x = -1\).
  3. (a) Find the \(y\)-coordinates with the original function \(f\). \[\begin{aligned} f(3) &= 27 - 3(9) + 2 = 27 - 27 + 2 = 2 \\ f(-1) &= -1 - 3(1) + 2 = -1 - 3 + 2 = -2 \end{aligned}\]
  4. (b) Solve \(x + 3y = 6\) for \(y\) to read off its slope. \[3y = -x + 6 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad y = -\frac{1}{3}x + 2\] The slope is \(-\tfrac{1}{3}\). A perpendicular line has slope equal to the negative reciprocal, \(-\dfrac{1}{-1/3} = 3\).
  5. (b) Set \(f'(x) = 3\). The quadratic does not factor with whole numbers, so use the quadratic formula. \[\begin{aligned} 3x^2 - 6x &= 3 \\ 3x^2 - 6x - 3 &= 0 \\ x^2 - 2x - 1 &= 0 \\ x &= \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 4}}{2} = \frac{2 \pm 2\sqrt{2}}{2} = 1 \pm \sqrt{2} \end{aligned}\]
  6. (b) Find \(f\left(1 + \sqrt{2}\right)\). First compute the powers: \(\left(1 + \sqrt{2}\right)^2 = 1 + 2\sqrt{2} + 2 = 3 + 2\sqrt{2}\), and \(\left(1 + \sqrt{2}\right)^3 = \left(1 + \sqrt{2}\right)\left(3 + 2\sqrt{2}\right) = 3 + 2\sqrt{2} + 3\sqrt{2} + 4 = 7 + 5\sqrt{2}\). \[\begin{aligned} f\left(1 + \sqrt{2}\right) &= \left(7 + 5\sqrt{2}\right) - 3\left(3 + 2\sqrt{2}\right) + 2 \\ &= 7 + 5\sqrt{2} - 9 - 6\sqrt{2} + 2 = -\sqrt{2} \end{aligned}\]
  7. (b) Find \(f\left(1 - \sqrt{2}\right)\) the same way: \(\left(1 - \sqrt{2}\right)^2 = 3 - 2\sqrt{2}\) and \(\left(1 - \sqrt{2}\right)^3 = 7 - 5\sqrt{2}\). \[\begin{aligned} f\left(1 - \sqrt{2}\right) &= \left(7 - 5\sqrt{2}\right) - 3\left(3 - 2\sqrt{2}\right) + 2 \\ &= 7 - 5\sqrt{2} - 9 + 6\sqrt{2} + 2 = \sqrt{2} \end{aligned}\]

Answer: (a) \((3, 2)\) and \((-1, -2)\). (b) \(\left(1 + \sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2}\right)\) and \(\left(1 - \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}\right)\).

Where students lose points: In part (b), using the slope \(-\tfrac{1}{3}\) itself, or using 6 (the number on the right side of \(x + 3y = 6\)), instead of the perpendicular slope 3. Also, reporting only the \(x\)-values when the question asks for points.

Problem 23
A ball is thrown straight up from the edge of a roof. Its height above the ground after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t) = -16t^2 + 48t + 64\) feet. (a) Find the velocity function, and the velocity at \(t = 1\) and \(t = 2\). Say what each sign means. (b) When does the ball reach its maximum height, and how high is it then? (c) When does the ball hit the ground, and what is its velocity at impact?
Hint
Velocity is the derivative of position. At the highest point the ball stops rising, so the velocity is 0 there. The ball hits the ground when the height \(s(t)\) is 0.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Velocity is the derivative of the position function. Use the power rule term by term. \[v(t) = s'(t) = -16 \cdot 2t + 48 + 0 = -32t + 48\]
  2. (a) Substitute \(t = 1\) and \(t = 2\). \[\begin{aligned} v(1) &= -32(1) + 48 = 16 \\ v(2) &= -32(2) + 48 = -64 + 48 = -16 \end{aligned}\] At 1 second the velocity is positive, so the ball is moving up at 16 feet per second. At 2 seconds it is negative, so the ball is moving down at 16 feet per second.
  3. (b) The ball rises while \(v(t) \gt 0\) and falls while \(v(t) \lt 0\), so the highest point is where \(v(t) = 0\). \[-32t + 48 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 32t = 48 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad t = \frac{48}{32} = \frac{3}{2}\]
  4. (b) Find the height at \(t = \tfrac{3}{2}\), using \(\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{9}{4}\). \[s\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right) = -16 \cdot \frac{9}{4} + 48 \cdot \frac{3}{2} + 64 = -36 + 72 + 64 = 100\]
  5. (c) The ball hits the ground when its height is 0. Divide the equation by \(-16\), then factor. \[\begin{aligned} -16t^2 + 48t + 64 &= 0 \\ t^2 - 3t - 4 &= 0 \\ (t - 4)(t + 1) &= 0 \end{aligned}\] So \(t = 4\) or \(t = -1\). Time after the throw cannot be negative, so \(t = 4\).
  6. (c) Find the velocity at \(t = 4\). \[v(4) = -32(4) + 48 = -128 + 48 = -80\] The negative sign means the ball is moving downward, at 80 feet per second.

Answer: (a) \(v(t) = -32t + 48\); \(v(1) = 16\) ft/sec (rising), \(v(2) = -16\) ft/sec (falling). (b) At \(t = \tfrac{3}{2}\) seconds, at a height of 100 feet. (c) At \(t = 4\) seconds, with velocity \(-80\) ft/sec (80 ft/sec downward).

Where students lose points: Answering part (b) with the time only, when the question also asks how high. In part (c), solving \(v(t) = 0\) again instead of \(s(t) = 0\), or keeping \(t = -1\).

Problem 24
(a) A student was asked to differentiate \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 3}{2x + 1}\) and wrote \[f'(x) = \frac{(x^2 - 3)(2) - (2x)(2x + 1)}{(2x + 1)^2} = \frac{2x^2 - 6 - 4x^2 + 2x}{(2x + 1)^2} = \frac{-2x^2 + 2x - 6}{(2x + 1)^2}.\] Find both errors and give the correct derivative. (b) Another student says that the derivative of \(x^3(2x + 5)\) is \(3x^2 \cdot 2 = 6x^2\). Explain what is wrong, and find the correct derivative two ways.
Hint
Compare the student's numerator with "low d-high minus high d-low": which product should come first? Then check how the minus sign was distributed over \((2x)(2x + 1)\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Name the pieces of the quotient rule. \[u = x^2 - 3, \qquad v = 2x + 1, \qquad u' = 2x, \qquad v' = 2\] The correct numerator is \(u'v - uv' = (2x)(2x + 1) - (x^2 - 3)(2)\).
  2. (a) First error: the student wrote \(uv' - u'v\), with the two products in the wrong order. Because of the minus sign, the wrong order flips the sign of the whole answer.
  3. (a) Second error: when distributing the minus sign over \((2x)(2x + 1) = 4x^2 + 2x\), the student wrote \(-4x^2 + 2x\). The minus sign must reach both terms, giving \(-4x^2 - 2x\).
  4. (a) Now do it correctly. Expand each product separately. \[(2x)(2x + 1) = 4x^2 + 2x, \qquad \left(x^2 - 3\right)(2) = 2x^2 - 6\]
  5. (a) Subtract, distributing the minus sign to both terms of \(2x^2 - 6\). Note that \(-(-6) = +6\). \[\begin{aligned} 4x^2 + 2x - \left(2x^2 - 6\right) &= 4x^2 + 2x - 2x^2 + 6 \\ &= 2x^2 + 2x + 6 \end{aligned}\] So \[f'(x) = \frac{2x^2 + 2x + 6}{(2x + 1)^2}\]
  6. (b) The student multiplied the two derivatives, which uses the false rule \((uv)' = u'v'\). The product rule has two terms. Name the pieces. \[u = x^3, \qquad v = 2x + 5, \qquad u' = 3x^2, \qquad v' = 2\]
  7. (b) Apply the product rule \(u'v + uv'\) and simplify. \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\Big[x^3(2x + 5)\Big] &= 3x^2(2x + 5) + x^3(2) \\ &= 6x^3 + 15x^2 + 2x^3 \\ &= 8x^3 + 15x^2 \end{aligned}\]
  8. (b) Second way: multiply out first, then use the power rule. The answers match. \[x^3(2x + 5) = 2x^4 + 5x^3, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\Big[2x^4 + 5x^3\Big] = 8x^3 + 15x^2\]

Answer: (a) The order of the products is reversed, and the minus sign was not distributed to \(2x\). The correct derivative is \(f'(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 + 2x + 6}{(2x + 1)^2}\). (b) The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives; the correct derivative is \(8x^3 + 15x^2\).

Where students lose points: Finding only one of the two errors in part (a). In part (b), saying "it is wrong" without showing the correct work; a "find the error" question wants the right answer too.

Problem 25
The graph below shows \(y = f(x)\) and its tangent line at \(x = 2\). The tangent line touches the curve at \((2, 3)\) and also passes through \((0, -1)\). A second function \(g\) satisfies \(g(2) = 4\) and \(g'(2) = -3\).
(a) Read \(f(2)\) and \(f'(2)\) from the graph. (b) Find \(h'(2)\) for \(h(x) = 3f(x) - 2g(x) + 5\). (c) Find \(p'(2)\) for \(p(x) = f(x)g(x)\), and the equation of the tangent line to \(y = p(x)\) at \(x = 2\). (d) Find \(q'(2)\) for \(q(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\).
Hint
\(f'(2)\) is the slope of the drawn tangent line: rise over run between the two marked points. Then write each rule with letters before putting in the four numbers.
Show full solution
  1. (a) The point of tangency \((2, 3)\) is on the curve, so \(f(2) = 3\). The derivative \(f'(2)\) is the slope of the tangent line. Use the two marked points on it. \[f'(2) = \frac{3 - (-1)}{2 - 0} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\]
  2. Collect the four numbers you will use. \[f(2) = 3, \qquad f'(2) = 2, \qquad g(2) = 4, \qquad g'(2) = -3\]
  3. (b) Use the sum and difference rule and the constant multiple rule. The constant 5 has derivative 0. \[\begin{aligned} h'(x) &= 3f'(x) - 2g'(x) + 0 \\ h'(2) &= 3(2) - 2(-3) = 6 + 6 = 12 \end{aligned}\]
  4. (c) Use the product rule with \(u = f\) and \(v = g\). \[\begin{aligned} p'(x) &= f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) \\ p'(2) &= (2)(4) + (3)(-3) = 8 - 9 = -1 \end{aligned}\]
  5. (c) The point of tangency on \(y = p(x)\) is \((2, p(2))\), with \(p(2) = f(2)g(2) = 3 \cdot 4 = 12\). Write the point-slope form with slope \(-1\) and solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - 12 &= -1(x - 2) \\ y - 12 &= -x + 2 \\ y &= -x + 14 \end{aligned}\]
  6. (d) Use the quotient rule with \(u = f\) and \(v = g\). \[\begin{aligned} q'(x) &= \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{[g(x)]^2} \\ q'(2) &= \frac{(2)(4) - (3)(-3)}{4^2} = \frac{8 + 9}{16} = \frac{17}{16} \end{aligned}\]

Answer: (a) \(f(2) = 3\), \(f'(2) = 2\). (b) \(h'(2) = 12\). (c) \(p'(2) = -1\), tangent line \(y = -x + 14\). (d) \(q'(2) = \dfrac{17}{16}\).

Where students lose points: Reading the slope as \(-1\) (the \(y\)-intercept of the tangent line) instead of computing rise over run. In part (d), \(-(3)(-3)\) is \(+9\); writing \(8 - 9\) is the most common slip.

Problem 26
Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{ax + b}{x + 1}\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants. (a) Find \(f'(x)\) in terms of \(a\) and \(b\). (b) Find the values of \(a\) and \(b\) for which the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at \(x = 1\) is \(y = 2x - 1\). (c) For those values, explain why the graph of \(f\) has no horizontal tangent lines.
Hint
Treat \(a\) and \(b\) as fixed numbers when you use the quotient rule. The given tangent line tells you two facts at \(x = 1\): the value of \(f\) and the value of \(f'\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Use the quotient rule. Since \(a\) and \(b\) are constants, the derivative of \(ax + b\) is \(a\). \[u = ax + b, \qquad v = x + 1, \qquad u' = a, \qquad v' = 1\]
  2. (a) Apply \(\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\) and simplify the numerator, distributing the minus sign. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \frac{a(x + 1) - (ax + b)(1)}{(x + 1)^2} \\ &= \frac{ax + a - ax - b}{(x + 1)^2} = \frac{a - b}{(x + 1)^2} \end{aligned}\]
  3. (b) The tangent line touches the graph at \(x = 1\), so \(f(1)\) equals the line's value there, which is \(2(1) - 1 = 1\). \[f(1) = \frac{a + b}{2} = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a + b = 2\]
  4. (b) The slope of the tangent line is 2, so \(f'(1) = 2\). Note that \((1 + 1)^2 = 4\). \[f'(1) = \frac{a - b}{4} = 2 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a - b = 8\]
  5. (b) Add the two equations to eliminate \(b\), then substitute back. \[\begin{aligned} (a + b) + (a - b) &= 2 + 8 \\ 2a &= 10 \\ a &= 5, \qquad b = 2 - a = 2 - 5 = -3 \end{aligned}\]
  6. (b) Check: with \(f(x) = \dfrac{5x - 3}{x + 1}\), we get \(f(1) = \dfrac{2}{2} = 1\) and \(f'(1) = \dfrac{5 - (-3)}{4} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2\). Both match the line.
  7. (c) With \(a = 5\) and \(b = -3\), the derivative is \[f'(x) = \frac{5 - (-3)}{(x + 1)^2} = \frac{8}{(x + 1)^2}.\] A fraction is zero only when its numerator is zero. The numerator is the constant 8, which is never zero, so \(f'(x) = 0\) has no solution and no tangent line is horizontal.

Answer: (a) \(f'(x) = \dfrac{a - b}{(x + 1)^2}\). (b) \(a = 5\), \(b = -3\). (c) \(f'(x) = \dfrac{8}{(x + 1)^2}\) has numerator 8, which is never 0, so there is no horizontal tangent.

Where students lose points: Using only the slope condition and getting one equation with two unknowns. The point condition \(f(1) = 1\) is the second equation. Also, treating \(a\) as a variable and writing its derivative as 1.

Problem 27
Find the equations of all lines through the point \((1, -2)\) that are tangent to the graph of \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\).
Hint
First check whether \((1, -2)\) is on the graph. It is not, so call the unknown point of tangency \(\left(a, a^2 + 1\right)\), write the tangent line there, and require it to pass through \((1, -2)\).
Show full solution
  1. Check whether the given point is on the graph. \(f(1) = 1 + 1 = 2\), not \(-2\), so \((1, -2)\) is not on the curve. The tangent lines touch the curve somewhere else, at an unknown \(x = a\).
  2. Write the tangent line at a general point \(x = a\). The point is \(\left(a, a^2 + 1\right)\), and since \(f'(x) = 2x\), the slope is \(2a\). \[y - \left(a^2 + 1\right) = 2a(x - a)\]
  3. The line must pass through \((1, -2)\). Substitute \(x = 1\) and \(y = -2\). \[-2 - \left(a^2 + 1\right) = 2a(1 - a)\]
  4. Expand both sides and collect everything on one side. \[\begin{aligned} -2 - a^2 - 1 &= 2a - 2a^2 \\ -a^2 - 3 &= 2a - 2a^2 \\ a^2 - 2a - 3 &= 0 \\ (a - 3)(a + 1) &= 0 \end{aligned}\] So \(a = 3\) or \(a = -1\). There are two tangent lines.
  5. For \(a = 3\): the point is \((3, 10)\) and the slope is \(2(3) = 6\). \[y - 10 = 6(x - 3) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad y = 6x - 18 + 10 = 6x - 8\] Check: at \(x = 1\), \(6 - 8 = -2\).
  6. For \(a = -1\): the point is \((-1, 2)\) and the slope is \(2(-1) = -2\). \[y - 2 = -2(x + 1) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad y = -2x - 2 + 2 = -2x\] Check: at \(x = 1\), \(-2(1) = -2\).

Answer: \(y = 6x - 8\) (tangent at \((3, 10)\)) and \(y = -2x\) (tangent at \((-1, 2)\)).

Where students lose points: Using \(x = 1\) as the point of tangency and writing \(y + 2 = 2(x - 1)\). That line does pass through \((1, -2)\), but it is not tangent to the curve, because \((1, -2)\) is not on the curve.

Problem 28
The demand for a gadget is \(q = 120 - 4p\), where \(q\) is the number of gadgets sold per week at a price of \(p\) dollars. The weekly cost of making \(q\) gadgets is \(C(q) = 200 + 6q\) dollars. (a) Write the revenue as a function of \(q\), and find the marginal revenue. (b) Find the marginal profit function. (c) Use the marginal profit to estimate the change in profit from selling the 41st gadget, and compare with the exact change. At a sales level of 60 gadgets, should the company sell more or fewer to increase profit? (d) Find the marginal average profit at \(q = 40\) and interpret it.
Hint
The derivative is with respect to \(q\), so the price must be written in terms of \(q\). Solve the demand equation for \(p\) before you form \(R = q \cdot p\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Solve the demand equation for the price. \[4p = 120 - q \quad \Longrightarrow \quad p = 30 - \frac{q}{4}\]
  2. (a) Revenue is the number sold times the price. Distribute, then differentiate with the power rule. \[\begin{aligned} R(q) &= q\left(30 - \frac{q}{4}\right) = 30q - \frac{q^2}{4} \\ R'(q) &= 30 - \frac{2q}{4} = 30 - \frac{q}{2} \end{aligned}\]
  3. (b) Profit is revenue minus cost. Put the cost in parentheses and combine \(30q - 6q = 24q\). \[\begin{aligned} P(q) &= \left(30q - \frac{q^2}{4}\right) - (200 + 6q) \\ &= 30q - \frac{q^2}{4} - 200 - 6q = 24q - \frac{q^2}{4} - 200 \end{aligned}\]
  4. (b) Differentiate. \[P'(q) = 24 - \frac{q}{2}\]
  5. (c) The change from 40 to 41 gadgets is estimated by \(P'(40)\). \[P'(40) = 24 - \frac{40}{2} = 24 - 20 = 4\] Selling the 41st gadget increases profit by approximately $4.
  6. (c) Compute the exact change \(P(41) - P(40)\), using \(40^2 = 1600\) and \(41^2 = 1681\). \[\begin{aligned} P(40) &= 24(40) - \frac{1600}{4} - 200 = 960 - 400 - 200 = 360 \\ P(41) &= 24(41) - \frac{1681}{4} - 200 = 984 - 420.25 - 200 = 363.75 \\ P(41) - P(40) &= 363.75 - 360 = 3.75 \end{aligned}\] The exact change is $3.75, close to the estimate of $4.
  7. (c) At 60 gadgets, check the sign of the marginal profit. \[P'(60) = 24 - \frac{60}{2} = 24 - 30 = -6\] It is negative: one more gadget would lower profit by about $6. To increase profit, the company should sell fewer gadgets.
  8. (d) The average profit is profit per gadget. The denominator \(q\) is a single term, so split the fraction. \[\overline{P}(q) = \frac{P(q)}{q} = \frac{24q}{q} - \frac{q^2}{4q} - \frac{200}{q} = 24 - \frac{q}{4} - 200q^{-1}\]
  9. (d) Differentiate with the power rule. The derivative of \(-200q^{-1}\) is \(-200(-1)q^{-2} = 200q^{-2}\). \[\overline{P}'(q) = -\frac{1}{4} + \frac{200}{q^2}\]
  10. (d) Substitute \(q = 40\), using \(40^2 = 1600\) and \(\tfrac{200}{1600} = \tfrac{1}{8}\). \[\overline{P}'(40) = -\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} = -\frac{2}{8} + \frac{1}{8} = -\frac{1}{8}\] At 40 gadgets the average profit is \(\overline{P}(40) = \tfrac{360}{40} = 9\) dollars per gadget. Selling one more gadget lowers the average profit by about \(\tfrac{1}{8}\) dollar, that is, about 12.5 cents per gadget.

Answer: (a) \(R(q) = 30q - \dfrac{q^2}{4}\), \(R'(q) = 30 - \dfrac{q}{2}\). (b) \(P'(q) = 24 - \dfrac{q}{2}\). (c) \(P'(40) = 4\), so about $4 (exactly $3.75); \(P'(60) = -6 \lt 0\), so sell fewer. (d) \(\overline{P}'(40) = -\dfrac{1}{8}\): the average profit drops by about 12.5 cents per gadget.

Where students lose points: Writing \(R = q(120 - 4p)\) or \(R = p \cdot q\) with \(p\) still in it, then differentiating with respect to \(q\) as if \(p\) were a constant. In part (c), reporting \(P'(41)\) as the estimate; the change from 40 to 41 uses \(P'(40)\).

Problem 29
Right now a bakery's monthly cost is $12,000 to bake 4000 loaves a month. The monthly cost is rising at $300 per month, and production is rising at 200 loaves per month. (a) What is the average cost per loaf right now? (b) Is the average cost per loaf rising or falling right now, and how fast? (c) If the cost keeps rising at $300 per month, how fast would production have to grow for the average cost per loaf to stay the same?
Hint
The average cost is \(A = \dfrac{C}{x}\), and both \(C\) and \(x\) change with time. Use the quotient rule with respect to time: \(A' = \dfrac{C'x - Cx'}{x^2}\).
Show full solution
  1. Define the variables at time \(t\) (in months): \(C(t)\) is the monthly cost in dollars, \(x(t)\) is the number of loaves per month, and \(A(t) = \dfrac{C(t)}{x(t)}\) is the average cost in dollars per loaf. Right now: \[C = 12000, \qquad C' = 300, \qquad x = 4000, \qquad x' = 200\]
  2. (a) Divide the cost by the number of loaves. \[A = \frac{12000}{4000} = 3\] The average cost is $3 per loaf.
  3. (b) The average cost is a quotient of two functions of \(t\). Apply the quotient rule with \(u = C\) and \(v = x\). \[A'(t) = \frac{C'(t)\,x(t) - C(t)\,x'(t)}{[x(t)]^2}\]
  4. (b) Substitute the current values. Compute each product first: \(300 \cdot 4000 = 1{,}200{,}000\), \(12000 \cdot 200 = 2{,}400{,}000\), and \(4000^2 = 16{,}000{,}000\). \[A' = \frac{1{,}200{,}000 - 2{,}400{,}000}{16{,}000{,}000} = \frac{-1{,}200{,}000}{16{,}000{,}000} = -\frac{12}{160} = -\frac{3}{40} = -0.075\] The value is negative, so the average cost per loaf is falling, at 7.5 cents per loaf per month. The reason is that production is growing by \(\tfrac{200}{4000} = 5\%\) a month while cost grows by only \(\tfrac{300}{12000} = 2.5\%\) a month.
  5. (c) The average cost stays the same when \(A' = 0\). Since the denominator \(x^2\) is not zero, the numerator must be zero. Keep \(x'\) as the unknown. \[\begin{aligned} C'x - Cx' &= 0 \\ 300(4000) - 12000\,x' &= 0 \\ 12000\,x' &= 1{,}200{,}000 \\ x' &= 100 \end{aligned}\] Production would need to grow at 100 loaves per month.

Answer: (a) $3 per loaf. (b) Falling, at \(\tfrac{3}{40}\) dollar (7.5 cents) per loaf per month. (c) Production would have to grow at 100 loaves per month.

Where students lose points: Dividing the rates, \(\tfrac{C'}{x'} = \tfrac{300}{200} = 1.5\), which is not the rate of change of the average. Also, flipping the order in the numerator, which gives \(+0.075\) and the wrong direction.
