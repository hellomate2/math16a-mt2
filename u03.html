The big idea

These two sections give you the derivative of any function built from \(e\), from a constant raised to a power that contains \(x\), or from a logarithm. The one thing to remember is this pair of rules: \(e^{g(x)}\) has derivative \(e^{g(x)}\) times \(g'(x)\), and \(\ln g(x)\) has derivative \(g'(x)\) divided by \(g(x)\). Everything else in this unit is those two rules mixed with the product, quotient, and chain rules you already know.

Section 4.4: Derivatives of exponential functions

Exponential functions and power functions

An exponential function is a function of the form \(f(x) = a^x\). The base \(a\) is a positive constant, and the variable \(x\) sits in the exponent. Examples are \(2^x\), \(10^x\), \(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^x\), and \(e^x\). The number \(e\) is a special base, \(e \approx 2.71828\). You met it earlier as the limit \(e = \lim_{n \to \infty}\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{n}\right)^n\).

A power function is the opposite arrangement: the variable is the base and the exponent is a constant, as in \(x^2\), \(x^{-1}\), or \(x^{e}\). The professor's caution is to never mix these up. The power rule \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^r) = r x^{r-1}\) is only for power functions. It does not apply to \(a^x\).

In plain words: look at where the variable sits. If it is up in the exponent, the function is exponential. If it is down in the base, the function is a power function. The two kinds have different derivative rules, so decide which kind you have before you differentiate.

Why the derivative of ex is ex

Start with any base \(a \gt 0\) and \(f(x) = a^x\). Use the definition of the derivative and the exponent rule \(a^{x+h} = a^x \cdot a^h\):

\[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^{x+h} - a^x}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^x a^h - a^x}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^x\left(a^h - 1\right)}{h} \\ &= a^x \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^h - 1}{h}. \end{aligned}\]

The factor \(a^x\) came out of the limit because it does not depend on \(h\). The limit that is left over is a plain number that depends only on the base \(a\). That number is exactly \(f'(0)\), because putting \(x = 0\) in the first line gives \(f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^h - 1}{h}\). So

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(a^x\right) = f'(0) \cdot a^x,\]

where \(f'(0)\) is the slope of the tangent line to \(y = a^x\) at the point \((0, 1)\). The number \(e\) can be defined as the one base for which this slope is exactly 1:

\[\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^h - 1}{h} = 1.\]

Put \(a = e\) into the boxed formula and the constant becomes 1. That is the whole reason \(e\) is the favorite base in calculus.

The derivative of ex

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^x\right) = e^x.\] The function \(e^x\) is its own derivative. At every point \(x_0\), the slope of the tangent line to \(y = e^x\) equals the height \(e^{x_0}\) of the graph there. The graphs of \(f\) and \(f'\) are the same curve.

In plain words: differentiating \(e^x\) does nothing to it. At every point on its graph, the height and the slope are the same number.

The derivative of ax for any base

To handle any base \(a \gt 0\), rewrite \(a\) as a power of \(e\). Since \(e^{\ln a} = a\), we have \(a^x = \left(e^{\ln a}\right)^x = e^{(\ln a)x}\). This is \(e^u\) with the inside function \(u = (\ln a)x\), whose derivative is the constant \(\ln a\). The chain rule (derivative of the outside, evaluated at the inside, times the derivative of the inside) gives

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left(a^x\right) = e^{(\ln a)x} \cdot \ln a = (\ln a) \cdot a^x.\]

Comparing this with \(\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = a^x \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^h - 1}{h}\) from above tells you the value of that mystery limit: \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^h - 1}{h} = \ln a\). It is the slope of \(y = a^x\) at \((0, 1)\). For \(a = e\) this gives \(\ln e = 1\), which agrees with the special case.

The derivative of ax

For any constant \(a \gt 0\), \[\frac{d}{dx}\left(a^x\right) = (\ln a) \cdot a^x.\] Do NOT write \(\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = x a^{x-1}\). That is the power rule used on a function that is not a power function.

In plain words: for any constant base, the derivative is the same function multiplied by the natural log of the base. Only for the base \(e\) is that extra number equal to 1, which is why it disappears.

Exponentials with the chain rule

When the exponent is a function \(g(x)\) instead of just \(x\), the function \(e^{g(x)}\) is a composition, which means one function plugged into another. The outside function is \(e^u\) and the inside function is \(u = g(x)\). The outside function is its own derivative. So the chain rule says: copy \(e^{g(x)}\) exactly as it is, then multiply by the derivative of the exponent. The same reasoning with the outside function \(a^u\) gives the second rule.

Chain rule for exponentials

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^{g(x)}\right] = e^{g(x)} \cdot g'(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[a^{g(x)}\right] = (\ln a) \cdot a^{g(x)} \cdot g'(x).\] The second formula is the most general one. The other three exponential rules are special cases: \(a = e\) gives the first (because \(\ln e = 1\)), \(g(x) = x\) gives \(\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = (\ln a)a^x\), and both together give \(\frac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x\).

In plain words: copy the exponential exactly as it is, then multiply by the derivative of its exponent. If the base is not \(e\), also multiply by the natural log of the base.

Worked example 1

Find the derivative of each function. (a) \(f(x) = e^{x^3 - 2x}\) (b) \(y = 7^{1/x}\) (c) \(y = 2^{\sqrt{x}}\) (d) \(f(x) = x^{\pi} + \pi^{x}\)

How to spot this type: a constant base (\(e\), 7, 2, \(\pi\)) with something containing \(x\) in the exponent, and no product or quotient around it, means you need only the exponential chain rule. A term with \(x\) in the base and a constant exponent, like \(x^{\pi}\), is a power function instead.

  1. (a) This is \(e^{g(x)}\) with exponent \(g(x) = x^3 - 2x\). Its derivative is \(g'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\) by the power rule. Copy \(e^{g(x)}\) and multiply by \(g'(x)\). \[f'(x) = e^{x^3 - 2x} \cdot (3x^2 - 2) = (3x^2 - 2)\,e^{x^3 - 2x}\]
  2. (b) This is \(a^{g(x)}\) with base \(a = 7\) and exponent \(g(x) = \frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}\). The power rule gives \(g'(x) = -x^{-2} = -\frac{1}{x^2}\). Use the rule for \(a^{g(x)}\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = (\ln 7) \cdot 7^{1/x} \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{x^2}\right) = -\frac{(\ln 7)\, 7^{1/x}}{x^2}\]
  3. (c) This is \(a^{g(x)}\) with \(a = 2\) and \(g(x) = \sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), so \(g'(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = (\ln 2) \cdot 2^{\sqrt{x}} \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} = \frac{(\ln 2)\, 2^{\sqrt{x}}}{2\sqrt{x}}\]
  4. (d) The first term \(x^{\pi}\) has the variable in the base and the constant \(\pi\) in the exponent, so it is a power function and the power rule applies. The second term \(\pi^x\) has the constant \(\pi\) in the base and the variable in the exponent, so it is an exponential function with \(a = \pi\). \[f'(x) = \pi x^{\pi - 1} + (\ln \pi)\, \pi^{x}\]

Answer: (a) \((3x^2 - 2)e^{x^3 - 2x}\) (b) \(-\dfrac{(\ln 7)\,7^{1/x}}{x^2}\) (c) \(\dfrac{(\ln 2)\,2^{\sqrt{x}}}{2\sqrt{x}}\) (d) \(\pi x^{\pi - 1} + (\ln \pi)\,\pi^{x}\)

Worked example 2

Find the derivative of each function and simplify. (a) \(f(x) = x^3 e^{-2x}\) (b) \(g(x) = \dfrac{6}{2 + e^{-3x}}\)

How to spot this type: the exponential is multiplied by, or divided into, another function of \(x\). A product of two pieces that both contain \(x\) calls for the product rule. A constant divided by an expression calls for rewriting it as a negative power.

  1. (a) This is a product, so use the product rule \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\). Name the pieces first. The derivative of \(e^{-2x}\) comes from the chain rule with exponent \(-2x\), whose derivative is \(-2\). \[u = x^3, \qquad v = e^{-2x}, \qquad u' = 3x^2, \qquad v' = e^{-2x} \cdot (-2) = -2e^{-2x}\]
  2. (a) Assemble the product rule. \[f'(x) = 3x^2 \cdot e^{-2x} + x^3 \cdot \left(-2e^{-2x}\right) = 3x^2 e^{-2x} - 2x^3 e^{-2x}\]
  3. (a) Factor out the common factor \(x^2 e^{-2x}\) from both terms. \[f'(x) = x^2 e^{-2x}(3 - 2x)\]
  4. (b) The numerator is a constant, so the quotient rule is more work than needed. Rewrite \(g\) as a power and use the generalized power rule, which is the chain rule for a power of a function: \(\frac{d}{dx}[u(x)]^n = n[u(x)]^{n-1}u'(x)\). Here the power is \(n = -1\). \[g(x) = 6\left(2 + e^{-3x}\right)^{-1}\]
  5. (b) The inside function is \(2 + e^{-3x}\). Its derivative is \(0 + e^{-3x} \cdot (-3) = -3e^{-3x}\), by the chain rule on \(e^{-3x}\). \[g'(x) = 6 \cdot (-1)\left(2 + e^{-3x}\right)^{-2} \cdot \left(-3e^{-3x}\right)\]
  6. (b) Multiply the constants: \(6 \cdot (-1) \cdot (-3) = 18\). Move the negative power to the denominator. \[g'(x) = 18e^{-3x}\left(2 + e^{-3x}\right)^{-2} = \frac{18e^{-3x}}{\left(2 + e^{-3x}\right)^2}\]

Answer: (a) \(f'(x) = x^2 e^{-2x}(3 - 2x)\) (b) \(g'(x) = \dfrac{18e^{-3x}}{\left(2 + e^{-3x}\right)^2}\)

Logistic functions

The function \(g\) in Worked example 2(b) is a logistic function. Logistic functions model populations or product sales that grow slowly at first, then quickly, and finally level off at a limiting value. The professor writes them as

\[P(t) = \frac{m P_0}{P_0 + (m - P_0)e^{-kmt}},\]

where \(P_0 = P(0)\) is the initial population (or sales quantity), \(m\) is the maximum possible population (the carrying capacity), and \(k\) is a positive constant. Dividing the numerator and the denominator by \(P_0\) gives

\[P(t) = \frac{m}{1 + \left(\frac{m}{P_0} - 1\right)e^{-kmt}}.\]

Since \(e^{-kmt} \to 0\) as \(t \to \infty\), the denominator goes to 1 and \(P(t) \to m\). With \(m = 3\), \(P_0 = 2\), and \(k = 1\), the first formula is \(\frac{6}{2 + e^{-3t}}\), which is the function from Worked example 2(b). Its derivative is always positive, so the function always increases, and it approaches 3.

In plain words: a logistic function starts at \(P_0\), grows slowly, then quickly, then flattens out just under the ceiling \(m\). It never goes above \(m\).

Rates of change of growth and decay

A quantity that grows or decays continuously is modeled by \(A(t) = A_0 e^{kt}\), where \(A_0 = A(0)\) is the starting amount and \(k\) is a constant (positive for growth, negative for decay). By the chain rule, \(A'(t) = A_0 e^{kt} \cdot k = k\,A(t)\). In words: the rate of change is always proportional to the current amount, with \(k\) as the constant of proportionality. A colony twice as big grows twice as fast.

In plain words: for continuous growth or decay, the rate of change right now is \(k\) times the amount you have right now. More stuff means faster change.

In business, if \(R(x)\) is the revenue from selling \(x\) units, the marginal revenue \(R'(x)\) approximates the extra revenue from selling one more unit (unit number \(x + 1\)). Revenue is price times quantity, \(R(x) = x \cdot p\). Here the demand function \(p\) gives the price at which \(x\) units can be sold.

In plain words: marginal revenue is the derivative of revenue. It estimates how many extra dollars the next unit brings in.

Worked example 3

(a) A bacteria population is \(P(t) = 800e^{0.05t}\) after \(t\) hours. How fast is the population growing after 20 hours? (b) The demand for a product is \(p = 40e^{-x/20}\), where \(p\) is the price per unit in dollars and \(x\) is the number of units sold. Find the marginal revenue and evaluate it at \(x = 10\). Interpret the answer.

How to spot this type: the words "how fast", "rate of change", or "marginal", together with a formula containing \(e^{kt}\) or a demand formula, mean: differentiate, plug in the given time or quantity, and state the units. A demand formula \(p\) always needs \(R = x \cdot p\) first.

  1. (a) Differentiate with the chain rule. The exponent is \(0.05t\), whose derivative is \(0.05\). \[P'(t) = 800e^{0.05t} \cdot 0.05 = 40e^{0.05t}\]
  2. (a) Substitute \(t = 20\). The exponent becomes \(0.05 \cdot 20 = 1\). \[P'(20) = 40e^{0.05 \cdot 20} = 40e^{1} = 40e\] The units of \(P'\) are bacteria per hour. As a check, \(P(20) = 800e\) and \(0.05 \cdot 800e = 40e\), which matches \(P'(t) = 0.05\,P(t)\).
  3. (b) Write the revenue function. Revenue is the number of units times the price per unit. \[R(x) = x \cdot p = 40x\,e^{-x/20}\]
  4. (b) Differentiate with the product rule. The pieces are below. The chain rule on \(e^{-x/20}\) multiplies by the derivative of \(-\frac{x}{20}\), which is \(-\frac{1}{20}\). \[u = 40x, \qquad v = e^{-x/20}, \qquad u' = 40, \qquad v' = -\frac{1}{20}e^{-x/20}\]
  5. (b) Assemble \(u'v + uv'\) and factor out \(e^{-x/20}\). \[\begin{aligned} R'(x) &= 40e^{-x/20} + 40x\left(-\frac{1}{20}e^{-x/20}\right) \\ &= 40e^{-x/20} - 2x\,e^{-x/20} \\ &= e^{-x/20}(40 - 2x) \end{aligned}\]
  6. (b) Substitute \(x = 10\). The exponent is \(-\frac{10}{20} = -\frac{1}{2}\), and \(e^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}\). \[R'(10) = e^{-1/2}(40 - 20) = 20e^{-1/2} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{e}}\]

Answer: (a) \(P'(20) = 40e\) bacteria per hour (a little under 109). (b) \(R'(x) = e^{-x/20}(40 - 2x)\) and \(R'(10) = \dfrac{20}{\sqrt{e}}\) dollars per unit: selling the 11th unit brings in about \(\dfrac{20}{\sqrt{e}}\) dollars (about 12 dollars) of extra revenue.

Common mistake. Writing \(\frac{d}{dx}(5^x) = x \cdot 5^{x-1}\). The power rule is only for a variable base and a constant exponent. The correct derivative is \((\ln 5)\,5^x\).
Common mistake. Forgetting the derivative of the exponent: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{-2x}\right)\) is \(-2e^{-2x}\), not \(e^{-2x}\), and \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x^2}\right)\) is \(2x\,e^{x^2}\). Also, never lower the exponent: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x^2}\right)\) is not \(x^2 e^{x^2 - 1}\).
Common mistake. Treating \(e^2\) or \(\ln 3\) as a function. They are constants, so \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^2\right) = 0\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}(\ln 3) = 0\). In \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^2 x\right)\) the answer is the constant \(e^2\).
Common mistake. Combining exponents wrongly. \(e^x \cdot e^{x} = e^{2x}\), not \(e^{x^2}\), and \(\left(e^{x}\right)^3 = e^{3x}\). Simplify with these exponent rules before differentiating whenever they help, as in \(\frac{e^{5x}}{e^{2x}} = e^{3x}\).

Section 4.5: Derivatives of logarithmic functions

What a logarithm is, and its properties

For a base \(a \gt 0\) with \(a \neq 1\), the logarithm \(\log_a x\) is the exponent you put on \(a\) to get \(x\). So \(y = \log_a x\) means exactly the same thing as \(a^y = x\). For example, \(\log_2 8 = 3\) because \(2^3 = 8\). The natural logarithm is the logarithm with base \(e\), written \(\ln x = \log_e x\). The logarithm is only defined for \(x \gt 0\).

Because \(\log_a\) undoes \(a^x\) (they are inverse functions), you have \(e^{\ln x} = x\) for \(x \gt 0\) and \(\ln\left(e^{x}\right) = x\) for every \(x\). Also \(\ln 1 = 0\) and \(\ln e = 1\).

In plain words: a logarithm answers the question "what power do I need?" The natural log \(\ln x\) asks what power of \(e\) gives \(x\). You can only take the log of a positive number. In the textbook, \(\log x\) written with no base means base 10.

Properties of logarithms

For \(x \gt 0\), \(y \gt 0\), and any real number \(r\): \[\begin{aligned} &\text{Product rule:} && \ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y \\ &\text{Quotient rule:} && \ln\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \ln x - \ln y \\ &\text{Power rule:} && \ln\left(x^r\right) = r\ln x \\ &\text{Change of base:} && \log_a x = \frac{\ln x}{\ln a} \end{aligned}\] The same properties hold for \(\log_a\) with any base. There is no rule for \(\ln(x + y)\): it does not split.

In plain words: a log turns multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, and a power into a number in front. A log of a sum stays as it is.

Why the derivative of ln x is 1/x

The professor finds this derivative from the fact that \(\ln x\) is the inverse of \(e^x\). Let \(y = \ln x\). Then \(e^y = x\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), using the chain rule on the left (the exponent \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so the derivative of \(e^y\) is \(e^y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}\)):

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{y}\right) &= \frac{d}{dx}(x) \\ e^{y}\,\frac{dy}{dx} &= 1 \\ x\,\frac{dy}{dx} &= 1 \qquad (\text{because } e^{y} = x) \\ \frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{1}{x}. \end{aligned}\]

The same steps with \(a^y = x\) give \((\ln a)\,a^y \frac{dy}{dx} = 1\), so \((\ln a)\,x\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 1\) and \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\ln a} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\). You can also see this from the change of base formula: \(\log_a x = \frac{1}{\ln a} \cdot \ln x\) is a constant times \(\ln x\).

The professor's remark is that this trick works for the inverse \(g\) of any function \(f\) whose derivative you know: differentiate \(f(g(x)) = x\) to get \(f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = 1\), so \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))}\).

Derivatives of logarithms

\[\frac{d}{dx}(\ln x) = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(\log_a x\right) = \frac{1}{\ln a} \cdot \frac{1}{x}.\]

In plain words: the derivative of \(\ln x\) is one over \(x\). For a log with any other base, also divide by the natural log of that base.

Logarithms with the chain rule, and absolute values

For \(\ln g(x)\), the outside function is \(\ln u\) with derivative \(\frac{1}{u}\), and the inside function is \(u = g(x)\). The chain rule gives \(\frac{1}{g(x)} \cdot g'(x)\): the derivative of the inside divided by the inside.

The formula \(\frac{d}{dx}(\ln x) = \frac{1}{x}\) holds for \(x \gt 0\). For \(x \lt 0\), the function \(\ln(-x)\) makes sense, and it equals \(\ln|x|\) because \(-x = |x|\) when \(x \lt 0\). The chain rule with inside function \(-x\) gives \(\frac{d}{dx}[\ln(-x)] = \frac{-1}{-x} = \frac{1}{x}\). So the same formula works on both sides of 0, and \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}\) for all \(x \neq 0\).

Chain rule for logarithms

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\ln|g(x)|\right] = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[\log_a|g(x)|\right] = \frac{1}{\ln a} \cdot \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)},\] valid wherever \(g(x) \neq 0\). The same formulas hold without the absolute value bars wherever \(g(x) \gt 0\). The last formula is the most general of the four log rules: \(a = e\) gives the first, and \(g(x) = x\) gives \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x| = \frac{1}{x}\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}\log_a|x| = \frac{1}{\ln a} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\).

In plain words: take the derivative of the inside and divide it by the inside. The absolute value bars do not change the formula. They only allow the inside to be negative.

Worked example 4

Find the derivative of each function. (a) \(y = \ln(6x)\), \(x \gt 0\) (b) \(f(x) = \log_3\left|x^2 - 5x + 2\right|\) (c) \(y = \dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}\) (d) \(f(x) = 5^x \log_5 x\)

How to spot this type: a logarithm of an expression, such as \(\ln(\ldots)\) or \(\log_3(\ldots)\), means the rule "inside derivative over inside". A logarithm multiplied by or divided by another function of \(x\) means the product or quotient rule comes first.

  1. (a) Use the chain rule for \(\ln g(x)\) with inside \(g(x) = 6x\) and \(g'(x) = 6\), then cancel the 6. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{6}{6x} = \frac{1}{x}\] This makes sense. The product property gives \(\ln(6x) = \ln 6 + \ln x\), and \(\ln 6\) is a constant with derivative 0, so the derivative is \(\frac{1}{x}\) either way.
  2. (b) Use the rule for \(\log_a|g(x)|\) with \(a = 3\) and \(g(x) = x^2 - 5x + 2\), so \(g'(x) = 2x - 5\). \[f'(x) = \frac{1}{\ln 3} \cdot \frac{2x - 5}{x^2 - 5x + 2} = \frac{2x - 5}{(\ln 3)\left(x^2 - 5x + 2\right)}\]
  3. (c) This is a quotient. For the quotient rule \(\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\), name the pieces. \[u = \ln x, \qquad v = x^2, \qquad u' = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad v' = 2x\]
  4. (c) Assemble the quotient rule. In the numerator, \(\frac{1}{x} \cdot x^2 = x\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{1}{x} \cdot x^2 - \ln x \cdot 2x}{\left(x^2\right)^2} = \frac{x - 2x\ln x}{x^4}\]
  5. (c) Factor \(x\) out of the numerator and cancel it with one factor of \(x\) in the denominator. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x(1 - 2\ln x)}{x^4} = \frac{1 - 2\ln x}{x^3}\]
  6. (d) This is a product. Name the pieces for the product rule. \[u = 5^x, \qquad v = \log_5 x, \qquad u' = (\ln 5)\,5^x, \qquad v' = \frac{1}{(\ln 5)\,x}\]
  7. (d) Assemble \(u'v + uv'\). \[f'(x) = (\ln 5)\,5^x \log_5 x + 5^x \cdot \frac{1}{(\ln 5)\,x} = (\ln 5)\,5^x \log_5 x + \frac{5^x}{(\ln 5)\,x}\]

Answer: (a) \(\dfrac{1}{x}\) (b) \(\dfrac{2x - 5}{(\ln 3)(x^2 - 5x + 2)}\) (c) \(\dfrac{1 - 2\ln x}{x^3}\) (d) \((\ln 5)\,5^x\log_5 x + \dfrac{5^x}{(\ln 5)\,x}\)

Simplify the logarithm first

When a logarithm contains products, quotients, powers, or roots, use the log properties to break it into simple pieces before you differentiate. The professor says this directly about \(\ln\sqrt{x^2 + x}\): use the rules of logarithms before computing the derivative. Each piece then needs only the rule \(\frac{g'}{g}\) on a small \(g\), and you avoid the product and quotient rules completely. The next example does one problem both ways so you can see the difference.

Worked example 5 (the same problem two ways)

Find \(f'(x)\) for \(f(x) = \ln\left[\dfrac{\left(x^2 + 1\right)^3}{(2x - 1)^2}\right]\), where \(x \neq \tfrac{1}{2}\).

How to spot this type: a logarithm with a product, quotient, power, or root inside it (a big fraction inside the \(\ln\)) is the signal to split it with the log properties before differentiating.

  1. Method 1, without simplifying. Write \(f(x) = \ln g(x)\) with \(g(x) = \frac{(x^2+1)^3}{(2x-1)^2}\), so \(f'(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\). To find \(g'\) with the quotient rule, name the pieces. Both \(u'\) and \(v'\) need the generalized power rule. \[u = \left(x^2 + 1\right)^3, \quad v = (2x - 1)^2, \quad u' = 3\left(x^2+1\right)^2 \cdot 2x = 6x\left(x^2+1\right)^2, \quad v' = 2(2x - 1) \cdot 2 = 4(2x - 1)\]
  2. Method 1, continued. Assemble \(\frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). \[g'(x) = \frac{6x\left(x^2+1\right)^2(2x - 1)^2 - \left(x^2+1\right)^3 \cdot 4(2x - 1)}{(2x - 1)^4}\]
  3. Method 1, continued. Factor \(\left(x^2+1\right)^2(2x - 1)\) out of the numerator, then cancel one factor of \(2x - 1\). \[\begin{aligned} g'(x) &= \frac{\left(x^2+1\right)^2(2x - 1)\left[6x(2x - 1) - 4\left(x^2+1\right)\right]}{(2x - 1)^4} \\ &= \frac{\left(x^2+1\right)^2\left[12x^2 - 6x - 4x^2 - 4\right]}{(2x - 1)^3} \\ &= \frac{\left(x^2+1\right)^2\left(8x^2 - 6x - 4\right)}{(2x - 1)^3} \end{aligned}\]
  4. Method 1, continued. Divide by \(g(x)\), which means multiplying by its reciprocal \(\frac{(2x-1)^2}{(x^2+1)^3}\), and cancel. \[f'(x) = \frac{\left(x^2+1\right)^2\left(8x^2 - 6x - 4\right)}{(2x - 1)^3} \cdot \frac{(2x - 1)^2}{\left(x^2+1\right)^3} = \frac{8x^2 - 6x - 4}{\left(x^2+1\right)(2x - 1)}\]
  5. Method 2, simplifying first. Use the quotient property, then the power property. For the power on \(2x - 1\), use \(\ln\left(u^2\right) = 2\ln|u|\): the square is positive even when \(2x - 1\) is negative, so the absolute value keeps the logarithm defined. The power on \(x^2 + 1\) needs no bars because \(x^2 + 1 \gt 0\). \[f(x) = \ln\left(x^2 + 1\right)^3 - \ln(2x - 1)^2 = 3\ln\left(x^2 + 1\right) - 2\ln|2x - 1|\]
  6. Method 2, continued. Differentiate each piece with \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln|g| = \frac{g'}{g}\). The inside derivatives are \(2x\) and \(2\). \[f'(x) = 3 \cdot \frac{2x}{x^2 + 1} - 2 \cdot \frac{2}{2x - 1} = \frac{6x}{x^2 + 1} - \frac{4}{2x - 1}\] This is already a correct final answer.
  7. Check that the two methods agree by combining the Method 2 answer over the common denominator \(\left(x^2 + 1\right)(2x - 1)\). \[\frac{6x(2x - 1) - 4\left(x^2 + 1\right)}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)(2x - 1)} = \frac{12x^2 - 6x - 4x^2 - 4}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)(2x - 1)} = \frac{8x^2 - 6x - 4}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)(2x - 1)}\] This is the Method 1 answer.

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{6x}{x^2 + 1} - \dfrac{4}{2x - 1} = \dfrac{8x^2 - 6x - 4}{\left(x^2 + 1\right)(2x - 1)}\)

Logarithmic differentiation

A function like \(h(x) = x^x\) or \(h(x) = (x^2 + 1)^{3x}\) has the variable in the base and in the exponent at the same time. It is not a power function (the exponent is not constant), and it is not an exponential function (the base is not constant), so neither rule applies. The method for \(h(x) = u(x)^{v(x)}\) is called logarithmic differentiation. Taking \(\ln\) of both sides turns the exponent into a factor, by the power property:

\[\ln h(x) = \ln\left[u(x)^{v(x)}\right] = v(x)\ln u(x).\]

Differentiate both sides. The left side is \(\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)}\) by the chain rule, and the right side needs the product rule:

\[\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)} = v'(x)\ln u(x) + \frac{v(x)\,u'(x)}{u(x)}.\]

Multiply both sides by \(h(x) = u(x)^{v(x)}\) to get \(h'(x)\). The professor's advice is to remember these steps rather than memorize the final formula.

How to do logarithmic differentiation of h(x) = u(x)v(x)
  1. Take the natural logarithm of both sides and use the power property: \(\ln h(x) = v(x)\ln u(x)\).
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). The left side becomes \(\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)}\). Use the product rule on the right side.
  3. Multiply both sides by \(h(x)\), and replace \(h(x)\) by its original formula \(u(x)^{v(x)}\).

In plain words: taking the log brings the hard exponent down to the ground as a factor. Then it is an ordinary product rule problem, and at the end you multiply the original function back in.

Worked example 6

Let \(h(x) = \left(x^2 + 4\right)^{x}\). Find \(h'(x)\) and \(h'(0)\).

How to spot this type: the variable appears in the base and in the exponent at the same time, as in \(x^x\) or \(\left(x^2 + 4\right)^x\). Neither the power rule nor the exponential rule fits, so take \(\ln\) of both sides.

  1. Take the natural logarithm of both sides, then use the power property to bring the exponent \(x\) down in front. \[\ln h(x) = \ln\left[\left(x^2 + 4\right)^{x}\right] = x\ln\left(x^2 + 4\right)\]
  2. Differentiate the left side with the chain rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}[\ln h(x)] = \frac{h'(x)}{h(x)}\). The right side is a product. Name its pieces. The derivative of \(\ln(x^2+4)\) is \(\frac{2x}{x^2 + 4}\) by the rule \(\frac{g'}{g}\). \[u = x, \qquad v = \ln\left(x^2 + 4\right), \qquad u' = 1, \qquad v' = \frac{2x}{x^2 + 4}\]
  3. Assemble the product rule on the right side and set the two sides equal. \[\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)} = 1 \cdot \ln\left(x^2 + 4\right) + x \cdot \frac{2x}{x^2 + 4} = \ln\left(x^2 + 4\right) + \frac{2x^2}{x^2 + 4}\]
  4. Multiply both sides by \(h(x) = \left(x^2 + 4\right)^{x}\). \[h'(x) = \left(x^2 + 4\right)^{x}\left[\ln\left(x^2 + 4\right) + \frac{2x^2}{x^2 + 4}\right]\]
  5. Substitute \(x = 0\). The base is \(0^2 + 4 = 4\), and \(4^0 = 1\). The fraction is \(\frac{0}{4} = 0\). Finally \(\ln 4 = \ln\left(2^2\right) = 2\ln 2\) by the power property. \[h'(0) = 4^{0}\left[\ln 4 + \frac{0}{4}\right] = 1 \cdot \ln 4 = 2\ln 2\]

Answer: \(h'(x) = \left(x^2 + 4\right)^{x}\left[\ln\left(x^2 + 4\right) + \dfrac{2x^2}{x^2 + 4}\right]\) and \(h'(0) = \ln 4 = 2\ln 2\).

A second way to do the same problem: write \(u^{v} = e^{v\ln u}\) (because \(e^{\ln u} = u\)) and use the chain rule for \(e^{g(x)}\). For \(x^x = e^{x\ln x}\), the exponent \(x\ln x\) has derivative \(\ln x + 1\) by the product rule, so \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^x\right) = e^{x\ln x}(\ln x + 1) = x^x(\ln x + 1)\). Both methods always give the same answer.

How to differentiate a function with exponentials and logarithms
  1. Simplify first. Split logarithms of products, quotients, powers, and roots with the log properties. Combine exponentials with the exponent rules (for example \(\frac{e^{5x}}{e^{2x}} = e^{3x}\)). Rewrite roots as fractional powers.
  2. If the variable is in both the base and the exponent, use logarithmic differentiation.
  3. Find the outermost operation. If the function is a sum, differentiate term by term. If it is a product or quotient, write down \(u\), \(v\), \(u'\), \(v'\) and use the product or quotient rule.
  4. For each \(e^{g(x)}\), \(a^{g(x)}\), \(\ln g(x)\), or \(\log_a g(x)\), use the chain rule version of the rule, and multiply by \(g'(x)\).
  5. Factor out common factors (especially exponentials) to simplify the answer.

Tangent lines

The tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = x_0\) is the line through the point \((x_0, f(x_0))\) with slope \(m = f'(x_0)\). Point-slope form is the way of writing a line from one point and its slope. Here it is \(y - f(x_0) = f'(x_0)(x - x_0)\). Remember that \(e^0 = 1\) and \(\ln 1 = 0\). Problem writers like to pick an \(x_0\) that makes the exponent 0 or the inside of the logarithm 1, because then the numbers come out clean without a calculator.

In plain words: a tangent line needs two things, a point on the curve and the slope there. The slope is the derivative at that \(x\).

How to find the tangent line at x = x0
  1. Find the point: compute \(y_0 = f(x_0)\).
  2. Find \(f'(x)\) with the rules, then the slope \(m = f'(x_0)\).
  3. Write \(y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)\) and solve for \(y\).
Worked example 7

(a) Find an equation of the tangent line to \(y = (x + 2)e^{-x}\) at \(x = 0\). (b) Find an equation of the tangent line to \(y = \ln\left(x^2 - 8\right)\) at \(x = 3\).

How to spot this type: the words "tangent line at \(x = \ldots\)" mean you need a point and a slope. Expect the given \(x\) to make an exponent equal to 0 or the inside of a log equal to 1.

  1. (a) Find the point. Substitute \(x = 0\) and use \(e^0 = 1\). \[y_0 = (0 + 2)e^{0} = 2 \cdot 1 = 2\]
  2. (a) Differentiate with the product rule. The pieces are below, with the chain rule giving \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{-x}\right) = -e^{-x}\). \[u = x + 2, \qquad v = e^{-x}, \qquad u' = 1, \qquad v' = -e^{-x}\] Assemble \(u'v + uv'\), factor out \(e^{-x}\), and simplify the bracket. \[\begin{aligned} y' &= 1 \cdot e^{-x} + (x + 2)\left(-e^{-x}\right) \\ &= e^{-x}\left[1 - (x + 2)\right] \\ &= e^{-x}(1 - x - 2) \\ &= e^{-x}(-x - 1) = -(x + 1)e^{-x} \end{aligned}\]
  3. (a) Find the slope at \(x = 0\). \[m = -(0 + 1)e^{0} = -1\]
  4. (a) Write the line in point-slope form and solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - 2 &= -1(x - 0) \\ y &= -x + 2 \end{aligned}\]
  5. (b) Find the point. The inside of the logarithm is \(3^2 - 8 = 9 - 8 = 1\), and \(\ln 1 = 0\). \[y_0 = \ln\left(3^2 - 8\right) = \ln 1 = 0\]
  6. (b) Differentiate with the rule \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln g(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\), where \(g(x) = x^2 - 8\) and \(g'(x) = 2x\). Then substitute \(x = 3\). \[y' = \frac{2x}{x^2 - 8}, \qquad m = \frac{2 \cdot 3}{9 - 8} = \frac{6}{1} = 6\]
  7. (b) Write the line in point-slope form and solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - 0 &= 6(x - 3) \\ y &= 6x - 18 \end{aligned}\]

Answer: (a) \(y = -x + 2\) (b) \(y = 6x - 18\)

Common mistake. Splitting a logarithm of a sum. \(\ln\left(x^2 + 5\right)\) is not \(\ln x^2 + \ln 5\). The product property is for \(\ln(xy)\), a logarithm of a product.
Common mistake. Confusing \((\ln x)^4\) with \(\ln\left(x^4\right)\). The first is a power of a logarithm: by the generalized power rule its derivative is \(4(\ln x)^3 \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \frac{4(\ln x)^3}{x}\). The second simplifies to \(4\ln x\) for \(x \gt 0\), with derivative \(\frac{4}{x}\).
Common mistake. Writing \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln(5x) = \frac{1}{5x}\). The chain rule puts the inside derivative 5 on top: \(\frac{5}{5x} = \frac{1}{x}\). In general, forgetting \(g'(x)\) in \(\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\) is the most common error in this section.
Common mistake. Dropping the \(\ln a\) in the base-\(a\) rules. \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(\log_2 x\right) = \frac{1}{(\ln 2)x}\), not \(\frac{1}{x}\), and \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(2^x\right) = (\ln 2)2^x\), not \(2^x\). Notice where \(\ln a\) goes: it multiplies for \(a^x\) and divides for \(\log_a x\).
Common mistake. Using the power rule or the exponential rule on \(x^x\). Neither \(x \cdot x^{x-1}\) nor \(x^x\ln x\) is correct. Use logarithmic differentiation, which gives \(x^x(\ln x + 1)\).

If you get stuck
  • Ask where the variable is. If it is only in the base, use the power rule. If it is only in the exponent, use the exponential rule. If it is in both, take \(\ln\) of both sides (logarithmic differentiation).
  • Before differentiating any logarithm, rewrite roots as powers (\(\sqrt{u} = u^{1/2}\)) and split the log with the product, quotient, and power properties.
  • Write the inside function \(g(x)\) and its derivative \(g'(x)\) on their own line before you use \(e^{g}g'\) or \(\frac{g'}{g}\). Most lost points in this unit are a missing \(g'\).
  • For a product or quotient, write \(u\), \(v\), \(u'\), \(v'\) first, then assemble the rule.
  • To solve \(f'(x) = 0\), factor out the exponential. It is never zero, so only the other factors can give solutions.
  • When you have to plug in a number, look for \(e^0 = 1\), \(\ln 1 = 0\), \(\ln e = 1\), \(e^{\ln a} = a\), and \(\ln e^{a} = a\). Those turn the arithmetic into whole numbers.
Quick check
Is \(x^{e}\) a power function or an exponential function, and what is its derivative?

It is a power function, because the variable is in the base and the exponent \(e\) is a constant. The power rule gives \(e\,x^{e - 1}\).

Why do \(\ln(7x)\) and \(\ln x\) have the same derivative, and are they the same function?

By the product property, \(\ln(7x) = \ln 7 + \ln x\), and the constant \(\ln 7\) has derivative 0, so both derivatives are \(\frac{1}{x}\). They are different functions: they differ by the constant \(\ln 7\).

Can \(e^{g(x)}\) ever equal zero? Why does that matter when you solve \(f'(x) = 0\)?

No. Every power of \(e\) is positive. So in \(f'(x) = xe^{-x}(2 - x) = 0\), the factor \(e^{-x}\) gives no solutions, and only \(x = 0\) and \(x = 2\) remain.

What is the slope of \(y = 2^x\) at \(x = 0\), and what is the slope of \(y = \log_2 x\) at \(x = 1\)?

The first is \((\ln 2)2^0 = \ln 2\). The second is \(\frac{1}{(\ln 2) \cdot 1} = \frac{1}{\ln 2}\). The factor \(\ln a\) multiplies for \(a^x\) and divides for \(\log_a x\).

Why can you not use the power rule on \(x^x\), and what is its derivative at \(x = 1\)?

The exponent is not a constant, so \(x^x\) is not a power function. Logarithmic differentiation gives \(x^x(\ln x + 1)\), which at \(x = 1\) is \(1 \cdot (0 + 1) = 1\).

Problem 2
Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = e^{4x - 7}\).
Show full solution

Problem 7

Problem 9
Find the derivative of \(f(x) = \dfrac{e^{2x}}{x^2 + 3}\) and simplify.
Show full solution
  1. This is a quotient, so use the quotient rule \(\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\). Name the pieces. The chain rule gives \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{2x}\right) = 2e^{2x}\). \[u = e^{2x}, \qquad v = x^2 + 3, \qquad u' = 2e^{2x}, \qquad v' = 2x\]
  2. Assemble the quotient rule. \[f'(x) = \frac{2e^{2x}\left(x^2 + 3\right) - e^{2x} \cdot 2x}{\left(x^2 + 3\right)^2}\]
  3. Factor \(2e^{2x}\) out of the numerator. Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{2e^{2x}\left(x^2 - x + 3\right)}{\left(x^2 + 3\right)^2}\)

Find the derivative of \(f(x) = \log_5\left(2x^2 + 1\right)\).
Show full solution
  1. This is \(\log_a g(x)\) with base \(a = 5\) and inside function \(g(x) = 2x^2 + 1\), so \(g'(x) = 4x\). The inside is always positive, so no absolute value is needed.
  2. Use the rule \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\log_a g(x)\right] = \frac{1}{\ln a} \cdot \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\). \[f'(x) = \frac{1}{\ln 5} \cdot \frac{4x}{2x^2 + 1} = \frac{4x}{(\ln 5)\left(2x^2 + 1\right)}\]

Problem 12
Find an equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(y = x\ln x\) at the point where \(x = e\).
Show full solution
  1. Find the point. Substitute \(x = e\) and use \(\ln e = 1\). \[y_0 = e\ln e = e \cdot 1 = e\] So the point is \((e, e)\).
  2. Differentiate with the product rule. Name the pieces. \[u = x, \qquad v = \ln x, \qquad u' = 1, \qquad v' = \frac{1}{x}\] \[y' = 1 \cdot \ln x + x \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1\]
  3. Find the slope at \(x = e\). \[m = \ln e + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2\]
  4. Write the line in point-slope form and solve for \(y\). \[\begin{aligned} y - e &= 2(x - e) \\ y - e &= 2x - 2e \\ y &= 2x - e \end{aligned}\]

Problem 13
Let \(f(x) = \ln\left[\dfrac{\left(x^2 + 4\right)^3\sqrt{2x + 1}}{x^5}\right]\) for \(x \gt 0\). Simplify \(f\) with the properties of logarithms, find \(f'(x)\), and evaluate \(f'(2)\).

Show full solution

  3. Differentiate each piece with \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln g(x) = \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\). The inside derivatives are \(2x\), \(2\), and \(1\). \[f'(x) = 3 \cdot \frac{2x}{x^2 + 4} + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{2x + 1} - 5 \cdot \frac{1}{x} = \frac{6x}{x^2 + 4} + \frac{1}{2x + 1} - \frac{5}{x}\]

  5. Combine. First \(\frac{3}{2} - \frac{5}{2} = -1\), then \(-1 + \frac{1}{5} = -\frac{5}{5} + \frac{1}{5}\). \[f'(2) = -1 + \frac{1}{5} = -\frac{4}{5}\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{6x}{x^2 + 4} + \dfrac{1}{2x + 1} - \dfrac{5}{x}\) and \(f'(2) = -\dfrac{4}{5}\)

Problem 14
Let \(h(x) = \left(1 + \dfrac{1}{x}\right)^{x}\) for \(x \gt 0\). Use logarithmic differentiation to find \(h'(x)\), and then find \(h'(1)\).
Hint
The variable is in the base and in the exponent, so take \(\ln\) of both sides. Writing \(1 + \tfrac{1}{x} = \tfrac{x + 1}{x}\) makes that log easy to split.
Show full solution
  1. The variable is in both the base and the exponent, so use logarithmic differentiation. Take the natural logarithm of both sides and use the power property. \[\ln h(x) = x\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)\]
  2. Simplify the logarithm before differentiating. Write \(1 + \frac{1}{x} = \frac{x + 1}{x}\) and use the quotient property. \[\ln h(x) = x\ln\left(\frac{x + 1}{x}\right) = x\left[\ln(x + 1) - \ln x\right]\]
  3. Differentiate both sides. The left side is \(\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)}\) by the chain rule. The right side is a product with the pieces below. \[u = x, \qquad v = \ln(x + 1) - \ln x, \qquad u' = 1, \qquad v' = \frac{1}{x + 1} - \frac{1}{x}\] \[\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)} = 1 \cdot \left[\ln(x + 1) - \ln x\right] + x\left[\frac{1}{x + 1} - \frac{1}{x}\right]\]
  4. Simplify the second term: \(x \cdot \frac{1}{x + 1} = \frac{x}{x + 1}\) and \(x \cdot \frac{1}{x} = 1\). Then \(\frac{x}{x + 1} - 1 = \frac{x - (x + 1)}{x + 1} = -\frac{1}{x + 1}\). Also put the first term back together as \(\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)\). \[\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)} = \ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right) + \frac{x}{x + 1} - 1 = \ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right) - \frac{1}{x + 1}\]
  5. Multiply both sides by \(h(x) = \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{x}\). \[h'(x) = \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^{x}\left[\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right) - \frac{1}{x + 1}\right]\]
  6. Substitute \(x = 1\). The base is \(1 + 1 = 2\), so \(h(1) = 2^1 = 2\), and \(\frac{1}{x + 1} = \frac{1}{2}\). \[h'(1) = 2\left[\ln 2 - \frac{1}{2}\right] = 2\ln 2 - 1\]

Answer: \(h'(x) = \left(1 + \dfrac{1}{x}\right)^{x}\left[\ln\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{x}\right) - \dfrac{1}{x + 1}\right]\) and \(h'(1) = 2\ln 2 - 1\)

Where students lose points: using the power rule on \(\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^x\), and forgetting to multiply by \(h(x)\) at the end. If you do not split the log, the derivative of \(\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)\) needs the inside derivative \(-\frac{1}{x^2}\), which is easy to drop.

Problem 15
A patient receives a 200 mg dose of a drug. The amount left in the bloodstream after \(t\) hours is \(A(t) = 200e^{-kt}\) milligrams, and the drug has a half-life of 4 hours (after 4 hours, half of the dose is left). Find \(k\) exactly, and find the rate at which the amount of drug is changing after 8 hours.
Hint
Use the half-life to write \(200e^{-4k} = 100\), and solve for \(k\) with \(\ln\). Then use \(A'(t) = -k\,A(t)\).
Show full solution
  1. Use the half-life to find \(k\). After 4 hours the amount is half of 200, which is 100. \[200e^{-4k} = 100\]
  2. Divide both sides by 200, then take the natural logarithm of both sides. Use \(\ln\left(e^{u}\right) = u\) on the left and \(\ln\frac{1}{2} = \ln 1 - \ln 2 = -\ln 2\) on the right. \[\begin{aligned} e^{-4k} &= \frac{1}{2} \\ -4k &= \ln\frac{1}{2} = -\ln 2 \\ k &= \frac{\ln 2}{4} \end{aligned}\]
  3. Differentiate \(A(t) = 200e^{-kt}\) with the chain rule. The exponent \(-kt\) has derivative \(-k\). \[A'(t) = 200e^{-kt} \cdot (-k) = -k \cdot 200e^{-kt} = -k\,A(t)\]
  4. Find the amount at \(t = 8\). With \(k = \frac{\ln 2}{4}\), the exponent is \(-8k = -8 \cdot \frac{\ln 2}{4} = -2\ln 2\). By the power property, \(-2\ln 2 = \ln\left(2^{-2}\right) = \ln\frac{1}{4}\), so \(e^{-8k} = e^{\ln(1/4)} = \frac{1}{4}\). This agrees with the half-life: two half-lives leave one quarter. \[A(8) = 200 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 50\]
  5. Find the rate at \(t = 8\) using \(A'(t) = -k\,A(t)\). \[A'(8) = -\frac{\ln 2}{4} \cdot 50 = -\frac{50\ln 2}{4} = -\frac{25\ln 2}{2}\]
  6. Interpret. The derivative is negative, so the amount is decreasing. The units are milligrams per hour.

Answer: \(k = \dfrac{\ln 2}{4}\) per hour, and \(A'(8) = -\dfrac{25\ln 2}{2}\) mg per hour: after 8 hours the amount of drug is decreasing at \(\dfrac{25\ln 2}{2}\) milligrams per hour (about 8.7 mg per hour).

Where students lose points: reporting the amount \(A(8) = 50\) when the question asks for the rate \(A'(8)\), and getting the sign of \(k\) wrong so the drug appears to grow. Give units: milligrams per hour.

Problem 16
Sales of a new phone (in thousands of units) \(t\) years after launch are modeled by the logistic function \(S(t) = \dfrac{12}{1 + 3e^{-t}}\). (a) Find \(S'(t)\). (b) Find the sales and the rate of change of sales at \(t = \ln 3\). (c) What happens to \(S(t)\) in the long run?
Hint
Rewrite \(S\) as \(12\left(1 + 3e^{-t}\right)^{-1}\) and use the generalized power rule. For part (b), \(e^{-\ln 3} = \tfrac{1}{3}\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Rewrite \(S\) as a power so the generalized power rule applies. \[S(t) = 12\left(1 + 3e^{-t}\right)^{-1}\]
  2. (a) The inside function is \(1 + 3e^{-t}\). By the chain rule on \(e^{-t}\), its derivative is \(0 + 3e^{-t} \cdot (-1) = -3e^{-t}\). \[S'(t) = 12 \cdot (-1)\left(1 + 3e^{-t}\right)^{-2} \cdot \left(-3e^{-t}\right)\]
  3. (a) Multiply the constants, \(12 \cdot (-1) \cdot (-3) = 36\), and move the negative power to the denominator. \[S'(t) = \frac{36e^{-t}}{\left(1 + 3e^{-t}\right)^2}\]
  4. (b) Evaluate the exponential at \(t = \ln 3\). By the power property, \(-\ln 3 = \ln\left(3^{-1}\right)\), so \(e^{-\ln 3} = 3^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\). Then \(3e^{-\ln 3} = 1\). \[1 + 3e^{-\ln 3} = 1 + 1 = 2\]

Problem 17
Find every point on the graph of \(f(x) = x^2e^{-x}\) where the tangent line is horizontal.

Problem 20
Let \(y = x^{\ln x}\) for \(x \gt 0\). Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\), and then find an equation of the tangent line to the curve at \(x = e\).

Take \(\ln\) of both sides: \(\ln\left(x^{\ln x}\right) = (\ln x)(\ln x)\). At \(x = e\), use \(\ln e = 1\).
Show full solution
  1. The variable is in both the base and the exponent, so use logarithmic differentiation. Take the natural logarithm of both sides and use the power property. \[\ln y = \ln\left(x^{\ln x}\right) = (\ln x)(\ln x) = (\ln x)^2\]
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\). The left side is \(\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}\) by the chain rule. The right side needs the generalized power rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}(\ln x)^2 = 2(\ln x) \cdot \frac{1}{x}\). \[\frac{1}{y}\,\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2\ln x}{x}\]
  3. Multiply both sides by \(y = x^{\ln x}\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = x^{\ln x} \cdot \frac{2\ln x}{x} = \frac{2(\ln x)\,x^{\ln x}}{x}\]
  4. Find the point at \(x = e\). Since \(\ln e = 1\), the exponent is 1. \[y_0 = e^{\ln e} = e^{1} = e\]

Show full solution

Problem 24

A company models its sales \(S\) (in thousands of units) as a function of the amount \(A\) it spends on local advertising (in thousands of dollars) by \(S(A) = c - de^{-\theta A}\), where \(c\), \(d\), and \(\theta\) are positive constants with \(c \gt d\). (a) Find \(S'(A)\). Explain why sales always increase as advertising increases, and why each extra thousand dollars adds fewer sales than the one before. (b) Take \(c = 50\), \(d = 40\), and \(\theta = \frac{1}{10}\). Find the sales with no advertising and the rate \(S'(0)\). (c) With the same constants, find the exact advertising level at which sales are increasing at a rate of 1 thousand units per thousand dollars, and the sales at that level. (d) What happens to sales in the long run as \(A\) grows? Explain what this means for the company.
Hint
Treat \(c\), \(d\), and \(\theta\) as fixed numbers. The chain rule on \(e^{-\theta A}\) brings down \(-\theta\). In (c), set \(S'(A) = 1\), not \(S(A) = 1\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Define the variables. \(A \ge 0\) is the advertising spending in thousands of dollars, and \(S(A)\) is the sales in thousands of units. Differentiate. The constant \(c\) has derivative 0. The chain rule on \(e^{-\theta A}\) multiplies by the derivative of the exponent, which is \(-\theta\). \[S'(A) = 0 - d \cdot e^{-\theta A} \cdot (-\theta) = d\theta\,e^{-\theta A}\]
  2. (a) Find the sign of \(S'\). The constants \(d\) and \(\theta\) are positive, and an exponential is always positive. So \(S'(A) \gt 0\) for every \(A\), and sales always increase as advertising increases.
  3. (a) Explain why the increase slows. As \(A\) gets larger, the exponent \(-\theta A\) gets more negative, so \(e^{-\theta A}\) gets smaller. So \(S'(A) = d\theta\,e^{-\theta A}\) gets smaller as \(A\) grows. Each extra thousand dollars of advertising raises sales by less than the thousand dollars before it.
  4. (b) Substitute the constants. Note \(d\theta = 40 \cdot \frac{1}{10} = 4\). \[S(A) = 50 - 40e^{-A/10}, \qquad S'(A) = 4e^{-A/10}\]
  5. (b) Substitute \(A = 0\), using \(e^0 = 1\). \[S(0) = 50 - 40 \cdot 1 = 10, \qquad S'(0) = 4 \cdot 1 = 4\] With no advertising, sales are 10 thousand units. At that point, each extra thousand dollars of advertising adds about 4 thousand units.
  6. (c) Set the rate equal to 1 and isolate the exponential by dividing by 4. \[4e^{-A/10} = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad e^{-A/10} = \frac{1}{4}\]
  7. (c) Take \(\ln\) of both sides. Use \(\ln e^{u} = u\) on the left and \(\ln\frac{1}{4} = -\ln 4\) on the right. Then multiply by \(-10\), and write \(\ln 4 = \ln 2^2 = 2\ln 2\). \[\begin{aligned} -\frac{A}{10} &= -\ln 4 \\ A &= 10\ln 4 = 20\ln 2 \end{aligned}\]
  8. (c) Find the sales at that level. We already know \(e^{-A/10} = \frac{1}{4}\) there. \[S(20\ln 2) = 50 - 40 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 50 - 10 = 40\] Since \(\ln 2 \approx 0.69\), the level \(20\ln 2\) is about 13.9 thousand dollars.
  9. (d) As \(A \to \infty\), \(e^{-A/10} \to 0\). \[\lim_{A \to \infty} S(A) = 50 - 40 \cdot 0 = 50\] Sales approach 50 thousand units but never reach it. Past a certain point, more advertising buys almost no extra sales, so the company should not keep raising its advertising budget without limit.

Answer: (a) \(S'(A) = d\theta\,e^{-\theta A}\), which is always positive, so sales always increase; it shrinks as \(A\) grows, so each extra thousand dollars adds fewer sales. (b) \(S(0) = 10\) thousand units and \(S'(0) = 4\) thousand units per thousand dollars. (c) \(A = 20\ln 2\) thousand dollars (about 13.9 thousand dollars), where sales are 40 thousand units. (d) Sales approach 50 thousand units, so extra advertising eventually adds almost nothing.

Where students lose points: dropping one of the two minus signs, which makes \(S'\) negative; and in (c), solving \(S(A) = 1\) instead of \(S'(A) = 1\).
