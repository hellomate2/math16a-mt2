Trig derivatives and L'Hospital's rule

Derivatives of sin, cos, tan, sec, csc, cot with the chain rule. Indeterminate forms 0/0 and infinity/infinity.

The big idea

Sine, cosine, and the other four trig functions have their own derivative formulas, and you combine them with the chain, product, and quotient rules exactly as you did for \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\). L'Hospital's rule is a tool for limits that come out as \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\): you replace the top and the bottom by their derivatives and try the limit again. The one thing to remember: check the form before every use of L'Hospital's rule, and multiply by the derivative of the inside every time a trig function has something other than plain \(x\) inside it.

Section 13.2: Derivatives of trigonometric functions

Radians and the unit circle

In calculus every angle is measured in radians, never in degrees. One full turn is \(2\pi\) radians. So half a turn is \(\pi\) radians, and

\[180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians}, \qquad 1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180} \text{ radians}.\]

To turn degrees into radians, multiply by \(\tfrac{\pi}{180}\). For example, \(60^\circ = 60 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{180} = \tfrac{60\pi}{180} = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\), and \(135^\circ = 135 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{180} = \tfrac{135\pi}{180} = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\). Every derivative formula in this section is true only when \(x\) is in radians.

In plain words: radians are just another unit for measuring angles, the way centimeters are another unit for length. The calculus formulas in this unit only work when the angle is measured in radians.

The unit circle is the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. Start at the point \((1, 0)\). Walk counterclockwise along the circle through an angle \(\theta\). The point where you stop has coordinates \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). So \(\cos\theta\) is the \(x\)-coordinate of that point and \(\sin\theta\) is its \(y\)-coordinate. The ratio \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\) is the slope of the line from the origin to that point. Because the point is on a circle of radius 1, both \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) are always between \(-1\) and \(1\).

In plain words: cosine tells you how far right (or left) the point on the circle is, and sine tells you how far up (or down) it is. Neither one can ever be bigger than 1 or smaller than \(-1\).

You need the values at \(0, \tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) cold. A pattern helps. The sines of \(0, \tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) are \(\tfrac{\sqrt{0}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{1}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{4}}{2}\). The cosines are the same list in reverse order.

Every other angle you will see is a multiple of one of these. To evaluate it, use its reference angle. The reference angle is the acute angle (less than \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)) between the radius to your stopping point and the \(x\)-axis. Take the value of the reference angle from the first quadrant, then attach the sign from the quadrant you are in:

  • Quadrant I (between \(0\) and \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\)): everything is positive.
  • Quadrant II (between \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\pi\)): only sine is positive.
  • Quadrant III (between \(\pi\) and \(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\)): only tangent is positive.
  • Quadrant IV (between \(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) and \(2\pi\)): only cosine is positive.

For example, \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\) is in quadrant II. Its reference angle is \(\pi - \tfrac{5\pi}{6} = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\). Sine is positive in quadrant II and cosine is negative, so \(\sin\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\cos\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\).

In plain words: every angle on the table has the same size of sine and cosine as one of the five first-quadrant angles. Only the plus or minus sign changes, and the quadrant tells you which sign.

Adding \(2\pi\) to an angle brings you back to the same point on the circle. So sine and cosine repeat every \(2\pi\). For example, \(\sin\tfrac{8\pi}{3} = \sin\left(\tfrac{8\pi}{3} - 2\pi\right) = \sin\left(\tfrac{8\pi}{3} - \tfrac{6\pi}{3}\right) = \sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\). A function that repeats like this is called periodic.

In plain words: if an angle is bigger than \(2\pi\), subtract \(2\pi\) (one full lap) until it lands between \(0\) and \(2\pi\). The sine and cosine do not change.

The other four functions and the identities from lecture

Definitions and identities
\[\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, \qquad \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}, \qquad \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}, \qquad \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}.\] \[\sin(-x) = -\sin x, \qquad \cos(-x) = \cos x, \qquad \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1, \qquad 1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x, \qquad 1 + \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x.\] \[\sin(2x) = 2\sin x\cos x, \qquad \cos(2x) = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 x.\]

The notation \(\sin^2 x\) means \((\sin x)^2\). It does not mean \(\sin(\sin x)\), and it does not mean \(\sin(x^2)\).

In plain words: tangent is sine divided by cosine, and the other three are flips (cosecant is one over sine, secant is one over cosine, cotangent is one over tangent). Sine squared plus cosine squared is always exactly 1, because the point \((\cos x, \sin x)\) is on a circle of radius 1. The other two squared identities come from dividing that one by \(\cos^2 x\) or by \(\sin^2 x\).

The professor also gave two special limits. You should simply know them (you do not need to derive them):

\[\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} = 1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0.\]

In plain words: for a very small angle \(h\) (in radians), \(\sin h\) is almost exactly equal to \(h\), so their ratio is almost 1. Also \(\cos h\) is so close to 1 that \(\cos h - 1\) shrinks faster than \(h\) does.

Here is how these limits give the derivative of sine. Start with the definition of the derivative, \(f'(x) = \lim\limits_{h\to 0}\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\), with \(f(x) = \sin x\). Use the addition formula \(\sin(x+h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x \sin h\):

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h} &= \frac{\sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h - \sin x}{h}\\ &= \sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h}.\end{aligned}\]

As \(h\to 0\), the two special limits turn this into \(\sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1 = \cos x\). So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x\). The other five derivatives follow from that one. For instance, \(\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\), so by the quotient rule with \(u = \sin x\), \(v = \cos x\), \(u' = \cos x\), \(v' = -\sin x\),

\[\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \frac{(\cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x.\]

The six derivatives

Derivatives of the trigonometric functions (x in radians)
\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\sin x &= \cos x, &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x &= -\sin x, &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tan x &= \sec^2 x,\\ \frac{d}{dx}\csc x &= -\csc x\cot x, &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sec x &= \sec x\tan x, &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cot x &= -\csc^2 x. \end{aligned}\]

In plain words: the derivative of sine is cosine, and the derivative of cosine is minus sine. Tangent gives secant squared, and secant gives secant times tangent. The three functions whose names start with "co" are the only ones whose derivatives have a minus sign.

How to remember them:

  • The three functions whose names start with "co" (cosine, cotangent, cosecant) are exactly the three whose derivatives have a minus sign.
  • Each "co" derivative is the matching plain derivative with every function swapped for its "co" partner and a minus sign added. Sine goes to cosine, so cosine goes to minus sine. Tangent goes to secant squared, so cotangent goes to minus cosecant squared. Secant goes to secant tangent, so cosecant goes to minus cosecant cotangent.
  • Sign check with a graph: \(\cos x\) starts at its top value 1 at \(x = 0\) and then goes down. So its derivative \(-\sin x\) must be negative just after 0. It is, because \(\sin x \gt 0\) there.

The chain rule versions

Most exam problems have something other than plain \(x\) inside the trig function. The chain rule says: differentiate the outside function, leave the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. If \(u = g(x)\) is the inside, then

Trig derivatives with the chain rule
\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\sin u &= \cos u \cdot u', &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos u &= -\sin u \cdot u', &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tan u &= \sec^2 u \cdot u',\\ \frac{d}{dx}\csc u &= -\csc u\cot u \cdot u', &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sec u &= \sec u\tan u \cdot u', &\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cot u &= -\csc^2 u \cdot u'. \end{aligned}\]

A power of a trig function uses the generalized power rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^n x = \dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x)^n = n(\sin x)^{n-1}\cdot\cos x\).

In plain words: use the plain formula with the inside copied exactly as it is, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. For a power such as \(\sin^3 x\), bring the power down, lower it by one, and multiply by the derivative of \(\sin x\).

Compare the two functions \(\sin^2 x\) and \(\sin(x^2)\). In \(\sin^2 x = (\sin x)^2\), the outside function is squaring and the inside is \(\sin x\). So its derivative is \(2\sin x \cdot \cos x\). In \(\sin(x^2)\), the outside function is sine and the inside is \(x^2\). So its derivative is \(\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\). Before you differentiate, always say to yourself which function is on the outside.

How to differentiate a function with trig in it
  1. Rewrite powers so the outside function is visible: \(\cos^3(2x) = [\cos(2x)]^3\).
  2. Decide the last operation you would do if you computed the function at a number: a sum, a product, a quotient, or a composition (one function inside another). That tells you which rule to use first: the sum rule, product rule, quotient rule, or chain rule.
  3. For a product or quotient, write \(u\), \(v\), \(u'\), \(v'\) before assembling.
  4. For a composition, peel one layer at a time from the outside in, multiplying by the derivative of each inside.
  5. Simplify with the identities only if it makes the answer shorter. A correct unsimplified derivative is still correct, unless the problem says "simplify."
Worked example 1

Find the derivatives of (a) \(f(x) = \sin^2(3x)\) and (b) \(g(x) = \sin(3x^2)\).

How to spot this type: the problem says "find the derivative," and the trig function has something other than plain \(x\) inside it or is raised to a power. That means the chain rule, and you must decide which layer is on the outside.

  1. (a) Rewrite \(f\) so the outside function is visible: \(f(x) = [\sin(3x)]^2\). The outside function is squaring and the inside is \(\sin(3x)\). By the generalized power rule, \[f'(x) = 2[\sin(3x)]^1 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x).\]
  2. Differentiate the inside \(\sin(3x)\) with the chain rule. Its outside is sine and its inside is \(3x\), whose derivative is 3. \[\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x) = \cos(3x)\cdot 3 = 3\cos(3x).\]
  3. Put the pieces together. \[f'(x) = 2\sin(3x)\cdot 3\cos(3x) = 6\sin(3x)\cos(3x).\] If you like, you can shorten this with the double-angle formula \(2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin(2\theta)\), using \(\theta = 3x\): \[6\sin(3x)\cos(3x) = 3\cdot 2\sin(3x)\cos(3x) = 3\sin(2\cdot 3x) = 3\sin(6x).\]
  4. (b) Here the outside function is sine and the inside is \(3x^2\), whose derivative is \(6x\). By the chain rule, \[g'(x) = \cos(3x^2)\cdot 6x = 6x\cos(3x^2).\]

Answer: (a) \(f'(x) = 6\sin(3x)\cos(3x) = 3\sin(6x)\). (b) \(g'(x) = 6x\cos(3x^2)\).

Worked example 2

Find the derivatives of (a) \(y = x^3\cos x\) and (b) \(y = \dfrac{\cos x}{1 - \sin x}\). Simplify (b) as far as you can.

How to spot this type: two pieces are multiplied (product rule) or divided (quotient rule), and at least one piece is a trig function. The word "simplify" means you should finish with an identity such as \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\).

  1. (a) This is a product, so use the product rule \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\) with \[u = x^3,\quad v = \cos x,\quad u' = 3x^2,\quad v' = -\sin x.\]
  2. Assemble. \[y' = 3x^2\cos x + x^3(-\sin x) = 3x^2\cos x - x^3\sin x.\]
  3. (b) This is a quotient, so use the quotient rule \(\left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\) with \[u = \cos x,\quad v = 1 - \sin x,\quad u' = -\sin x,\quad v' = -\cos x.\]
  4. Assemble. \[y' = \frac{(-\sin x)(1 - \sin x) - (\cos x)(-\cos x)}{(1 - \sin x)^2}.\]
  5. Multiply out the numerator one piece at a time. The first piece is \((-\sin x)(1 - \sin x) = -\sin x + \sin^2 x\). The second piece is \(-(\cos x)(-\cos x) = +\cos^2 x\). So the numerator is \[-\sin x + \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x.\]
  6. Use the identity \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\). \[-\sin x + \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = -\sin x + 1 = 1 - \sin x.\]
  7. Cancel one factor of \(1 - \sin x\) from the top and bottom. \[y' = \frac{1 - \sin x}{(1 - \sin x)^2} = \frac{1}{1 - \sin x}.\]

Answer: (a) \(y' = 3x^2\cos x - x^3\sin x\). (b) \(y' = \dfrac{1}{1 - \sin x}\).

Tangent lines at special angles

The tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = a\) passes through the point \((a, f(a))\) and has slope \(f'(a)\). Its equation in point-slope form is \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)\). With trig functions the number \(a\) is usually one of the table angles. Then both \(f(a)\) and \(f'(a)\) come out exact with no calculator.

In plain words: the tangent line touches the curve at one point and has the same steepness as the curve there. You need two things: the point (plug \(a\) into \(f\)) and the slope (plug \(a\) into \(f'\)).

Worked example 3

Find the equation of the tangent line to \(f(x) = x\tan x\) at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).

How to spot this type: the words "tangent line" (or "slope of the tangent") together with a specific \(x\)-value. You need a point and a slope, and the slope comes from \(f'\), not from \(f\).

  1. Find the point. Since \(\tan\tfrac{\pi}{4} = 1\), \[f\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot 1 = \tfrac{\pi}{4}.\] The point is \(\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\).
  2. Differentiate with the product rule, using \[u = x,\quad v = \tan x,\quad u' = 1,\quad v' = \sec^2 x.\] \[f'(x) = 1\cdot\tan x + x\sec^2 x = \tan x + x\sec^2 x.\]
  3. Find the slope at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\). Since \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\), we get \(\sec\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}\), so \(\sec^2\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \left(\sqrt{2}\right)^2 = 2\). \[f'\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = 1 + \tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot 2 = 1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}.\]
  4. Write the line in point-slope form. \[y - \tfrac{\pi}{4} = \left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right).\]
  5. Solve for \(y\) to get slope-intercept form. First multiply out the constant part: \(\left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\tfrac{\pi}{4} - \tfrac{\pi^2}{8}\). Then add \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) to both sides. \[\begin{aligned} y &= \left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)x - \tfrac{\pi}{4} - \tfrac{\pi^2}{8} + \tfrac{\pi}{4}\\ &= \left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)x - \tfrac{\pi^2}{8}.\end{aligned}\]

Answer: \(y = \left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)x - \tfrac{\pi^2}{8}\), which is the same line as \(y - \tfrac{\pi}{4} = \left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\).

Periodic models

Some quantities repeat on a cycle: temperature over a day, the depth of water as tides come in and out, seasonal sales. These are modeled with sine or cosine. Take a model such as \(T(t) = A + B\cos(kt)\).

  • The number \(A\) is the average level.
  • The number \(B\) is how far above and below the average the quantity swings.
  • The period (the time for one full cycle) is \(\tfrac{2\pi}{k}\).

The derivative \(T'(t)\) is the rate of change. A positive value means the quantity is rising at that time. A negative value means it is falling. Always give units, such as "degrees per hour."

In plain words: the formula describes a wave. The derivative at a time tells you how fast the wave is moving at that moment and whether it is going up or down.

Worked example 4

The temperature in a town \(t\) hours after midnight is modeled by \(T(t) = 60 - 10\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\right)\) degrees Fahrenheit, for \(0 \le t \le 24\). (a) Find \(T'(t)\). (b) How fast is the temperature changing at 8 am? (c) How fast is it changing at 6 pm?

How to spot this type: a real quantity that repeats (temperature, tide, sales) with a sine or cosine in its formula, and a question that says "how fast," "rate of change," or "rising or falling." First turn each clock time into a value of \(t\).

  1. (a) Differentiate with the chain rule. The outside function is cosine and the inside is \(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\), whose derivative is \(\tfrac{\pi}{12}\). The constant 60 has derivative 0. \[\begin{aligned} T'(t) &= 0 - 10\left(-\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\right)\right)\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{12}\\ &= \tfrac{10\pi}{12}\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\right) = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\right).\end{aligned}\]
  2. (b) 8 am is 8 hours after midnight, so \(t = 8\). The angle is \(\tfrac{8\pi}{12} = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\), and \(\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\). \[T'(8) = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \tfrac{5\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{12}.\] This is positive, so the temperature is rising.
  3. (c) 6 pm is 18 hours after midnight, so \(t = 18\). The angle is \(\tfrac{18\pi}{12} = \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\), and \(\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2} = -1\). \[T'(18) = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\cdot(-1) = -\tfrac{5\pi}{6}.\] This is negative, so the temperature is falling.
  4. Check against the graph. The temperature is lowest at midnight, where \(T(0) = 60 - 10\cos 0 = 60 - 10 = 50\). It is highest at noon, where \(T(12) = 60 - 10\cos\pi = 60 + 10 = 70\). So it should be rising at 8 am and falling at 6 pm, which matches the signs. The labeled values on the graph are \(T(8) = 60 - 10\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 65\) and \(T(18) = 60 - 10(0) = 60\).

Answer: (a) \(T'(t) = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\right)\). (b) At 8 am the temperature is rising at \(\tfrac{5\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{12}\) degrees per hour. (c) At 6 pm it is falling at \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\) degrees per hour.

Common mistake. Getting the sign of \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x\) wrong. It is \(-\sin x\). The same minus sign appears for \(\cot\) and \(\csc\), and nowhere else among the six.
Common mistake. Forgetting the derivative of the inside. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(5x)\) is \(5\cos(5x)\), not \(\cos(5x)\). \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan(x^2)\) is \(2x\sec^2(x^2)\), not \(\sec^2(x^2)\).
Common mistake. Mixing up \(\sin^2 x\) and \(\sin(x^2)\). The derivative of \(\sin^2 x\) is \(2\sin x\cos x\). The derivative of \(\sin(x^2)\) is \(2x\cos(x^2)\).
Common mistake. Writing \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec^2 x\) or \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec x\tan x\). The squared one belongs to tangent. The product belongs to secant.
Common mistake. Plugging degrees into a derivative formula. In \(T(t) = 60 - 10\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\right)\) the angle \(\tfrac{\pi t}{12}\) is in radians. At \(t = 8\) the angle is \(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\) radians (the same as \(120^\circ\)), not 8 degrees. The formula \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x\) is false when \(x\) is measured in degrees.
Common mistake. Treating \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) (or any trig value at a fixed number) as something to differentiate. It is a constant, so its derivative is 0.
What to write on the exam. Name each rule as you use it: "By the chain rule with outside function \(\ln u\) and inside \(u = \sin(\dots)\)," or "By the product rule with \(u = x^3\), \(v = \cos x\)." For a rate problem, finish with a sentence that includes the sign in words and the units: "At 8 am the temperature is rising at \(\tfrac{5\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{12}\) degrees per hour."

Section 12.7: L'Hospital's rule

Indeterminate forms

Suppose you want \(\lim\limits_{x\to a}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\), and both \(f(x)\to 0\) and \(g(x)\to 0\) as \(x\to a\). Substituting gives the symbol \(\tfrac{0}{0}\). That symbol tells you nothing: the limit might be 5, or 0, or infinite, or might not exist. Such a limit is called an indeterminate form of type \(\tfrac{0}{0}\). Likewise, if \(f(x)\to\pm\infty\) and \(g(x)\to\pm\infty\), the limit is an indeterminate form of type \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\).

In plain words: "\(\tfrac{0}{0}\)" means the top and the bottom are both shrinking to zero. The answer depends on which one shrinks faster, and plugging in cannot tell you that. "Indeterminate" just means "not decided yet."

Sometimes algebra handles it. For instance, \[\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2 - 3x}{x^2 - 9} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x(x - 3)}{(x - 3)(x + 3)} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x}{x + 3} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}.\] But no algebra trick cancels anything in \(\lim\limits_{x\to 1}\dfrac{\ln x}{x - 1}\) or \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{e^x}{x^3}\). L'Hospital's rule handles these. It compares how fast the top and bottom are changing, which is exactly what their derivatives measure.

Forms that are not indeterminate:

  • \(\tfrac{5}{2}\) is just \(\tfrac{5}{2}\), and \(\tfrac{0}{3}\) is 0.
  • \(\tfrac{\infty}{3}\) means the limit is infinite.
  • A nonzero number over 0 (such as \(\tfrac{4}{0}\)) means the quotient blows up. The limit is \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or does not exist. Check the sign of the bottom from each side. For example, \(\tfrac{3}{2x}\to\infty\) as \(x\to 0^+\) but \(\tfrac{3}{2x}\to-\infty\) as \(x\to 0^-\), so \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\tfrac{3}{2x}\) does not exist.

None of these allow L'Hospital's rule.

L'Hospital's rule

Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable on an open interval containing \(a\), and \(g'(x) \ne 0\) near \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\)). Suppose that either

  • \(\lim\limits_{x\to a}f(x) = 0\) and \(\lim\limits_{x\to a}g(x) = 0\) (type \(\tfrac{0}{0}\)), or
  • \(\lim\limits_{x\to a}f(x) = \pm\infty\) and \(\lim\limits_{x\to a}g(x) = \pm\infty\) (type \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\)).

Then \[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\] if the limit on the right exists or is \(\pm\infty\). The rule also holds for one-sided limits and limits at infinity: you may replace \(x\to a\) with \(x\to a^+\), \(x\to a^-\), \(x\to\infty\), or \(x\to-\infty\).

In plain words: when the top and bottom both go to 0, or both blow up, the limit of the fraction is the same as the limit of (derivative of the top) divided by (derivative of the bottom). You take two separate derivatives. You do not use the quotient rule.

Why it works, for type \(\tfrac{0}{0}\): if \(f(a) = 0\) and \(g(a) = 0\), then near \(a\) each function is close to its tangent line. So \(f(x) \approx f'(a)(x - a)\) and \(g(x) \approx g'(a)(x - a)\). Divide, and the \((x - a)\) factors cancel: \[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} \approx \frac{f'(a)(x - a)}{g'(a)(x - a)} = \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}.\] The derivatives measure how fast each one is heading to zero, and the ratio of those speeds is the answer.

How to use L'Hospital's rule
  1. Check the form first. Substitute \(x = a\) (or let \(x\to\pm\infty\)) in the top and in the bottom separately. Write down what each one approaches.
  2. If the form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\), say so in words. If it is anything else, stop: the rule does not apply, and the limit comes from substitution (or from the sign check for a nonzero number over 0).
  3. Differentiate the top and the bottom separately. Do not use the quotient rule. The new limit is \(\lim \dfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\).
  4. Simplify the new quotient if any algebra is available. Then try the limit again by substituting.
  5. If the new limit is still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\), go back to step 2 and apply the rule again. Check the form every time.
Worked example 5

Find (a) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 1}\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2 - 1}\) and (b) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{2x} - 1}{\sin(3x)}\).

How to spot this type: a limit as \(x\) goes to a number, written as one fraction, where plugging in gives 0 on top and 0 on the bottom. Common signs are \(\ln 1\), \(e^0 - 1\), \(\sin 0\), and \(1 - \cos 0\), which are all 0.

  1. (a) Check the form. At \(x = 1\) the top is \(\ln 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(1^2 - 1 = 0\). The limit has the form \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2 - 1) = 2x\). Then simplify the fraction: dividing \(\tfrac{1}{x}\) by \(2x\) gives \(\tfrac{1}{x}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2x} = \tfrac{1}{2x^2}\). \[\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{\ln x}{x^2 - 1} = \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{1/x}{2x} = \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{1}{2x^2}.\]
  3. Substitute \(x = 1\). \[\frac{1}{2(1)^2} = \frac{1}{2}.\]
  4. (b) Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(e^0 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(\sin 0 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so the rule applies.
  5. Differentiate the top and bottom separately, using the chain rule on each: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(e^{2x} - 1) = 2e^{2x}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(3x) = 3\cos(3x)\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^{2x} - 1}{\sin(3x)} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2e^{2x}}{3\cos(3x)}.\]
  6. Substitute \(x = 0\). \[\frac{2e^0}{3\cos 0} = \frac{2\cdot 1}{3\cdot 1} = \frac{2}{3}.\]

Answer: (a) \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). (b) \(\tfrac{2}{3}\).

Common mistake: using the rule on a form that is not indeterminate. Consider \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{3 + \sin x}{1 + x}\). Substituting gives \(\dfrac{3 + 0}{1 + 0} = 3\), and that is the answer. If you skip the form check and differentiate anyway, you get \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{1} = \cos 0 = 1,\] which is wrong. The rule is only a theorem when the form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). The same thing happens in the professor's example \(\lim\limits_{x\to 1}\dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x + 2}\). Substitution gives \(\tfrac{0}{3} = 0\), while the illegal derivative step gives \(\lim\limits_{x\to 1}\dfrac{2x}{1} = 2\).
Common mistake. Using the quotient rule on \(\dfrac{f}{g}\). L'Hospital's rule says to replace the top by \(f'\) and the bottom by \(g'\) as two separate derivatives. There is no \(g^2\) anywhere.
Common mistake. Writing "\(= \tfrac{0}{0}\)" as if it were a value, or writing an equals sign between the original limit and the derivative limit before checking the form. Write the form check in words first.

Limits at infinity and the growth ranking

For limits as \(x\to\infty\), L'Hospital's rule settles a race between the top and the bottom. Three kinds of functions show up, and they always finish in the same order.

Growth ranking as x goes to infinity

\(\ln x\) grows much more slowly than any positive power \(x^p\) (including \(\sqrt{x}\) and \(x^{1/3}\)). Every power \(x^n\) grows much more slowly than \(e^x\). In limits, for every \(p \gt 0\) and every \(n\),

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^p} = 0, \qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^n}{e^x} = 0, \qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{x^n} = \infty.\]

The same holds with a polynomial \(p(x)\) with positive leading coefficient in place of \(x^n\): \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{e^x}{p(x)} = \infty\). Each use of L'Hospital's rule lowers the power of \(x\) by one, while \(e^x\) stays \(e^x\). After enough steps the power is gone and \(e^x\) wins.

In plain words: when \(x\) gets huge, \(e^x\) beats every power of \(x\), and every power of \(x\) (even \(\sqrt{x}\)) beats \(\ln x\). Whichever one is on the bottom and wins makes the fraction go to 0. Whichever one is on top and wins makes the fraction go to \(\infty\).

You still have to show the work with L'Hospital's rule on the exam. The ranking tells you what answer to expect, so you can spot an algebra slip.

Worked example 6

Find (a) \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^2 + 3x}{e^x}\) and (b) \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln x}{x^{1/3}}\).

How to spot this type: \(x\to\infty\), and the top and bottom both blow up. Usually it is a power of \(x\) against \(e^x\), or \(\ln x\) against a power of \(x\). Expect to use the rule more than once when the power is bigger than 1.

  1. (a) Check the form. As \(x\to\infty\), the top \(x^2 + 3x\to\infty\) and the bottom \(e^x\to\infty\). The form is \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((x^2 + 3x)' = 2x + 3\) and \((e^x)' = e^x\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2 + 3x}{e^x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x + 3}{e^x}.\]
  3. Check the form again. The top \(2x + 3\to\infty\) and the bottom \(e^x\to\infty\), so the form is still \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). Apply the rule a second time: \((2x + 3)' = 2\) and \((e^x)' = e^x\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x + 3}{e^x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{e^x}.\]
  4. Now the top is the constant 2 and the bottom goes to \(\infty\). A fixed number divided by something growing without bound goes to 0. \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2}{e^x} = 0.\]
  5. (b) Check the form. As \(x\to\infty\), \(\ln x\to\infty\) and \(x^{1/3}\to\infty\). The form is \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\).
  6. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{1/3} = \tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^{1/3}} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{\tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}}.\]
  7. Simplify before taking the limit. Dividing by \(\tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\) is the same as multiplying by \(3x^{2/3}\). \[\frac{1/x}{\tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}} = \frac{1}{x}\cdot 3x^{2/3} = 3x^{2/3 - 1} = 3x^{-1/3} = \frac{3}{x^{1/3}}.\]
  8. Take the limit. The bottom \(x^{1/3}\to\infty\), so \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3}{x^{1/3}} = 0.\] This agrees with the ranking: \(\ln x\) loses to every positive power of \(x\).

Answer: (a) \(0\). (b) \(0\).

Other indeterminate forms: products and differences

L'Hospital's rule only works on quotients. Two other forms covered in lecture must first be rewritten as a quotient.

Type \(0\cdot\infty\). Suppose \(f(x)\to 0\) and \(g(x)\to\pm\infty\). Then the product \(f(x)g(x)\) is indeterminate. Rewrite it as \[fg = \frac{f}{1/g} \qquad\text{or}\qquad fg = \frac{g}{1/f}.\] This turns it into type \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). The professor's advice: pick the version that gives an easier expression after differentiating. Usually that means keeping \(\ln x\) on top, because its derivative \(\tfrac{1}{x}\) is simple. The other choice puts \(\tfrac{1}{\ln x}\) on the bottom, and its derivative is messy.

In plain words: one factor is pulling the product toward 0 and the other is pulling it toward infinity. To use the rule, move one factor into the bottom of a fraction by flipping it. Keep the logarithm on top.

Type \(\infty - \infty\). Suppose \(f(x)\to\infty\) and \(g(x)\to\infty\). Then the difference \(f(x) - g(x)\) is indeterminate. If it is a difference of fractions, combine it into one fraction over a common denominator. If there is a square root, multiply and divide by the conjugate (the same expression with a plus sign in the middle). Then check the new form. You may or may not need L'Hospital's rule after that.

In plain words: "infinity minus infinity" is not 0, because one of the two might be much bigger than the other. Turn the difference into a single fraction, then look again.

Here is the professor's square root example, which needs no L'Hospital's rule. To find \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\left(x - \sqrt{x^2 - 3x}\right)\), multiply and divide by the conjugate \(x + \sqrt{x^2 - 3x}\). The top becomes \(x^2 - (x^2 - 3x) = 3x\). Then divide the top and bottom by \(x\), using \(\tfrac{\sqrt{x^2 - 3x}}{x} = \sqrt{\tfrac{x^2 - 3x}{x^2}} = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{3}{x}}\) for \(x \gt 0\): \[x - \sqrt{x^2 - 3x} = \frac{3x}{x + \sqrt{x^2 - 3x}} = \frac{3}{1 + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{3}{x}}} \longrightarrow \frac{3}{1 + 1} = \frac{3}{2}.\]

Worked example 7

Find (a) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0^+}x^2\ln x\) and (b) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0^+}\left(\dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{1}{\sin x}\right)\).

How to spot this type: the expression is not a single fraction. It is a product where one factor goes to 0 and the other blows up (like \(x^2\ln x\)), or a difference of two things that both blow up (like \(\tfrac{1}{x} - \tfrac{1}{\sin x}\)). Rewrite it as one fraction before anything else.

  1. (a) Identify the form. As \(x\to 0^+\), \(x^2\to 0\) and \(\ln x\to-\infty\). The form is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\), which is indeterminate but not a quotient yet.
  2. Rewrite as a quotient, keeping \(\ln x\) on top. Since \(x^2 = \dfrac{1}{1/x^2}\), \[x^2\ln x = \frac{\ln x}{1/x^2} = \frac{\ln x}{x^{-2}}.\] Now the top goes to \(-\infty\) and the bottom \(x^{-2}\) goes to \(\infty\). The form is \(\tfrac{-\infty}{\infty}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  3. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac{1}{x}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{-2} = -2x^{-3}\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{x^{-2}} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1/x}{-2x^{-3}}.\]
  4. Simplify. Dividing by \(-2x^{-3}\) is the same as multiplying by \(-\tfrac{x^3}{2}\). \[\frac{1/x}{-2x^{-3}} = \frac{1}{x}\cdot\left(-\frac{x^3}{2}\right) = -\frac{x^2}{2}.\]
  5. Take the limit. \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right) = -\frac{0}{2} = 0.\]
  6. (b) Identify the form. As \(x\to 0^+\), \(\tfrac{1}{x}\to\infty\). Also \(\sin x\to 0\) through positive values, so \(\tfrac{1}{\sin x}\to\infty\). The form is \(\infty - \infty\).
  7. Combine over the common denominator \(x\sin x\). \[\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin x}{x\sin x} - \frac{x}{x\sin x} = \frac{\sin x - x}{x\sin x}.\] At \(x = 0\) the top is \(0 - 0 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\cdot 0 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\).
  8. Apply L'Hospital's rule. The top has derivative \(\cos x - 1\). The bottom is a product, so use the product rule with \(u = x\), \(v = \sin x\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = \cos x\). Its derivative is \(1\cdot\sin x + x\cos x = \sin x + x\cos x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\sin x - x}{x\sin x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\cos x - 1}{\sin x + x\cos x}.\]
  9. Check the form again. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(1 - 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0 + 0\cdot 1 = 0\). It is still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule again. The top has derivative \(-\sin x\). For the bottom, \(\sin x\) has derivative \(\cos x\), and \(x\cos x\) has derivative \(1\cdot\cos x + x(-\sin x)\) by the product rule. So the bottom has derivative \(\cos x + \cos x - x\sin x = 2\cos x - x\sin x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\cos x - 1}{\sin x + x\cos x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{-\sin x}{2\cos x - x\sin x}.\]
  10. Substitute \(x = 0\). The bottom is no longer 0. \[\frac{-\sin 0}{2\cos 0 - 0\cdot\sin 0} = \frac{0}{2 - 0} = 0.\]

Answer: (a) \(0\). (b) \(0\).

Common mistake. Applying the rule directly to a product like \(x^2\ln x\) or a difference like \(\tfrac{1}{x} - \tfrac{1}{\sin x}\). Differentiate only after the expression is one fraction whose form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\).
Common mistake. Applying the rule a second time without rechecking. After each step, substitute again. If the new quotient gives a number, you are done. Applying the rule once more at that point can give a wrong answer, exactly like the trap example above.
Common mistake. Deciding that \(\infty - \infty = 0\) or that \(0\cdot\infty = 0\). Both are indeterminate. The answer can be any number, or infinite, until you rewrite and compute.
What to write on the exam. Before each use of the rule, write one sentence such as "As \(x\to 0\), the numerator \(\to 0\) and the denominator \(\to 0\), so the limit has the form \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) and L'Hospital's rule applies." For a product or difference, write "This has the form \(0\cdot\infty\), so I rewrite it as a quotient" before rewriting. The grader is looking for the form check every time the rule is used.
If you get stuck
  • Rewrite every power so the outside function is visible (\(\sin^3(2x) = [\sin(2x)]^3\)), then peel the layers from the outside in, one derivative per layer.
  • If a trig expression looks messy, rewrite everything in terms of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) before you simplify. Then look for \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\).
  • For an angle outside the first quadrant, subtract \(2\pi\) until it is between \(0\) and \(2\pi\), find the reference angle, then attach the sign from the quadrant.
  • For any limit, substitute first and write down what the top and the bottom each approach. That one line decides everything else.
  • If L'Hospital's rule makes the fraction messier, you picked the wrong factor for the bottom in a \(0\cdot\infty\) problem. Flip the other factor instead and keep \(\ln x\) on top.
  • If you get a nonzero number over 0, stop using the rule. Check the sign of the bottom just to the right and just to the left of the point.
Quick check
Do \(\sin^2 x\) and \(\sin(x^2)\) have the same derivative?

No. The derivative of \(\sin^2 x = (\sin x)^2\) is \(2\sin x\cos x\), while the derivative of \(\sin(x^2)\) is \(2x\cos(x^2)\), because the outside functions are different.

Why is \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x\) false when \(x\) is in degrees?

An angle of \(x\) degrees is \(\tfrac{\pi x}{180}\) radians, so the chain rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi x}{180}\right) = \tfrac{\pi}{180}\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi x}{180}\right)\), which has an extra factor \(\tfrac{\pi}{180}\).

Can you use L'Hospital's rule on \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{x + 2}{x + 1}\)?

No. Substituting gives \(\tfrac{2}{1}\), which is not indeterminate, so the limit is simply \(2\). Using the rule anyway would give the wrong value \(\tfrac{1}{1} = 1\).

After one use of L'Hospital's rule you get a limit of the form \(\tfrac{5}{0}\). What do you do?

Stop using the rule, because \(\tfrac{5}{0}\) is not indeterminate. The limit is \(\infty\), \(-\infty\), or does not exist, and you decide which by checking the sign of the bottom on each side of the point.

Which is bigger for very large \(x\): \(x^{100}\) or \(e^{x}\)? What is \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^{100}}{e^{x}}\)?

\(e^x\) wins in the end, so the limit is \(0\). One hundred uses of L'Hospital's rule turn the top into the constant \(100!\) while the bottom stays \(e^x\).

What to have memorized
  • \(\pi\) radians \(= 180^\circ\), and the sine, cosine, and tangent of \(0, \tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), plus the quadrant signs for their multiples.
  • \(\tan x = \tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\), \(\sec x = \tfrac{1}{\cos x}\), \(\csc x = \tfrac{1}{\sin x}\), \(\cot x = \tfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\), \(1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\), \(\sin(2x) = 2\sin x\cos x\).
  • \(\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\tfrac{\sin h}{h} = 1\) and \(\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\tfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} = 0\).
  • \((\sin x)' = \cos x\), \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), \((\tan x)' = \sec^2 x\), \((\csc x)' = -\csc x\cot x\), \((\sec x)' = \sec x\tan x\), \((\cot x)' = -\csc^2 x\). The "co" functions get the minus signs.
  • Chain rule: \((\sin u)' = \cos u\cdot u'\), and so on for all six. \((\sin^n x)' = n\sin^{n-1}x\cos x\).
  • L'Hospital's rule: for type \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}\) only, \(\lim\tfrac{f}{g} = \lim\tfrac{f'}{g'}\). Derivatives of top and bottom separately, no quotient rule. Check the form before every application.
  • \(0\cdot\infty\): rewrite as \(\tfrac{f}{1/g}\) or \(\tfrac{g}{1/f}\). \(\infty - \infty\): combine into one fraction, or use the conjugate if there is a square root.
  • Growth ranking as \(x\to\infty\): \(\ln x\) is much slower than \(x^p\) (\(p \gt 0\)), which is much slower than \(e^x\).

Words that change the method. "Exact value" or "without a calculator" means use the unit-circle table and leave \(\pi\) and square roots in the answer. "Find \(f'(x)\)" asks for a formula, "find the slope at \(x = a\)" asks for one number, and "find the tangent line" asks for an equation. "How fast," "rate of change," and "at what rate" mean the derivative, with units; "rising or falling" means its sign; "fastest" means the extreme value of the derivative, not of the function. "Absolute" maximum or minimum on a closed interval means compare \(f\) at the critical numbers inside the interval and at the endpoints, while "relative" or "local" means use a sign chart of \(f'\). "Evaluate the limit, if it exists" warns that the answer may be "does not exist," and "use L'Hospital's rule where applicable" warns that it may not be applicable. "\(x\to a^+\)" means look only from the right, which matters whenever a \(\ln x\) or a sign check is involved. "Verify," "justify," and "explain" mean the grader wants the reason in words: the form check before every use of the rule, or the name of the rule you used. "Simplify" means finish with an identity.

Problem 1
Without a calculator, find the exact values of \(\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\), \(\cos\tfrac{7\pi}{4}\), \(\tan\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\), and \(\sec\tfrac{\pi}{3}\).
Show full solution
  1. \(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\) is in quadrant II (between \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\pi\)) with reference angle \(\pi - \tfrac{2\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\). Sine is positive in quadrant II. \[\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = \sin\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.\]
  2. \(\tfrac{7\pi}{4}\) is in quadrant IV (between \(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) and \(2\pi\)) with reference angle \(2\pi - \tfrac{7\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\). Cosine is positive in quadrant IV. \[\cos\tfrac{7\pi}{4} = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.\]
  3. \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\) is in quadrant II with reference angle \(\pi - \tfrac{5\pi}{6} = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\), so \(\sin\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(\cos\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\). Divide. \[\tan\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{1/2}{-\sqrt{3}/2} = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}.\]
  4. Secant is one over cosine, and \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{1}{2}\). \[\sec\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{1/2} = 2.\]

Answer: \(\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), \(\cos\tfrac{7\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\), \(\tan\tfrac{5\pi}{6} = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\), \(\sec\tfrac{\pi}{3} = 2\).

Problem 2
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(4x)}{x}\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(\sin 0 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\). The limit has the form \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately. By the chain rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(4x) = 4\cos(4x)\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}x = 1\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin(4x)}{x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{4\cos(4x)}{1}.\]
  3. Substitute \(x = 0\). \[\frac{4\cos 0}{1} = \frac{4\cdot 1}{1} = 4.\]

Answer: \(4\).

Problem 3
Find the derivatives of \(f(x) = \sin^2 x\) and \(g(x) = \sin(x^2)\).
Show full solution
  1. Rewrite \(f(x) = (\sin x)^2\). The outside function is squaring and the inside is \(\sin x\). By the generalized power rule (the chain rule for powers), \[f'(x) = 2(\sin x)^1\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = 2\sin x\cos x.\]
  2. For \(g(x) = \sin(x^2)\) the outside function is sine and the inside is \(x^2\), whose derivative is \(2x\). By the chain rule, \[g'(x) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2).\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = 2\sin x\cos x\) (which also equals \(\sin(2x)\)) and \(g'(x) = 2x\cos(x^2)\).

Problem 4
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{x + 1}{\cos x}\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form by substituting \(x = 0\). The top is \(0 + 1 = 1\) and the bottom is \(\cos 0 = 1\). The form is \(\tfrac{1}{1}\), which is not indeterminate, so L'Hospital's rule does not apply.
  2. Because the bottom is not 0, the quotient is continuous at \(x = 0\) and the limit is the value from substitution. \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x + 1}{\cos x} = \frac{1}{1} = 1.\]
  3. For comparison, differentiating anyway would give \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{1}{-\sin x}\), which does not even exist. This is why the form check comes first.

Answer: \(1\) (by substitution; L'Hospital's rule does not apply).

Problem 5
Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = 4\sin x - 3\cos x + \cot x\).
Show full solution
  1. Differentiate term by term with the sum rule, using \((\sin x)' = \cos x\), \((\cos x)' = -\sin x\), and \((\cot x)' = -\csc^2 x\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 4\cos x - 3(-\sin x) + (-\csc^2 x).\]
  2. Clean up the signs. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 4\cos x + 3\sin x - \csc^2 x.\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 4\cos x + 3\sin x - \csc^2 x\).

Problem 6
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{e^x}{2x}\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. As \(x\to\infty\), the top \(e^x\to\infty\) and the bottom \(2x\to\infty\). The form is \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((e^x)' = e^x\) and \((2x)' = 2\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2}.\]
  3. Take the limit. Since \(e^x\to\infty\), half of it also goes to \(\infty\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2} = \infty.\] This matches the growth ranking: \(e^x\) beats any power of \(x\).

Answer: \(\infty\) (the limit does not exist as a number; the quotient grows without bound).

Problem 7
Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = x^3\csc x\).
Show full solution
  1. This is a product, so use the product rule \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\) with \[u = x^3,\quad v = \csc x,\quad u' = 3x^2,\quad v' = -\csc x\cot x.\]
  2. Assemble. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2\csc x + x^3(-\csc x\cot x) = 3x^2\csc x - x^3\csc x\cot x.\]
  3. Factor out the common factor \(x^2\csc x\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2\csc x\,(3 - x\cot x).\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2\csc x - x^3\csc x\cot x = x^2\csc x\,(3 - x\cot x)\).

Problem 8
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{3x} - 1 - 3x}{x^2}\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(e^0 - 1 - 0 = 1 - 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0^2 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately. By the chain rule \((e^{3x})' = 3e^{3x}\), so the top has derivative \(3e^{3x} - 3\). The bottom has derivative \(2x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^{3x} - 1 - 3x}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{3e^{3x} - 3}{2x}.\]
  3. Check the form again. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(3\cdot 1 - 3 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\). Still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule a second time. The top has derivative \(9e^{3x}\) and the bottom has derivative \(2\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{3e^{3x} - 3}{2x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{9e^{3x}}{2}.\]
  4. Substitute \(x = 0\). \[\frac{9e^0}{2} = \frac{9}{2}.\]

Answer: \(\tfrac{9}{2}\).

Problem 9
Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = \ln\lvert\cos(3x)\rvert\) and simplify.
Show full solution
  1. Use the rule \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln\lvert u\rvert = \dfrac{u'}{u}\) with the inside \(u = \cos(3x)\).
  2. Find \(u'\) with the chain rule. The outside of \(\cos(3x)\) is cosine and the inside is \(3x\), with derivative 3. \[u' = -\sin(3x)\cdot 3 = -3\sin(3x).\]
  3. Assemble. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-3\sin(3x)}{\cos(3x)}.\]
  4. Simplify with \(\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta\) for \(\theta = 3x\). \[\frac{dy}{dx} = -3\tan(3x).\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -3\tan(3x)\).

Problem 10
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{e^{x/2}}\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. As \(x\to\infty\), the top \(x^2 + 1\to\infty\) and the bottom \(e^{x/2}\to\infty\). The form is \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately. The top has derivative \(2x\). By the chain rule the bottom has derivative \(e^{x/2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}e^{x/2}\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2 + 1}{e^{x/2}} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x}{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x/2}} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x}{e^{x/2}}.\]
  3. Check the form again. The top \(4x\to\infty\) and the bottom \(e^{x/2}\to\infty\), still \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). Apply the rule a second time. \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4x}{e^{x/2}} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{4}{\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x/2}} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{8}{e^{x/2}}.\]
  4. The top is the constant 8 and the bottom goes to \(\infty\), so the quotient goes to 0. \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{8}{e^{x/2}} = 0.\]

Answer: \(0\).

Problem 11
Find \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) for \(y = \sin^4(2x)\).
Show full solution
  1. Rewrite so the outside function is visible: \(y = [\sin(2x)]^4\). The outside function is the fourth power and the inside is \(\sin(2x)\).
  2. Apply the generalized power rule. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 4[\sin(2x)]^3\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\sin(2x).\]
  3. Differentiate \(\sin(2x)\) with the chain rule: its inside \(2x\) has derivative 2. \[\frac{d}{dx}\sin(2x) = \cos(2x)\cdot 2 = 2\cos(2x).\]
  4. Put the pieces together. \[\frac{dy}{dx} = 4\sin^3(2x)\cdot 2\cos(2x) = 8\sin^3(2x)\cos(2x).\]

Answer: \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 8\sin^3(2x)\cos(2x)\).

Problem 12
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0^+}\sqrt{x}\,\ln x\).
Show full solution
  1. Identify the form. As \(x\to 0^+\), \(\sqrt{x}\to 0\) and \(\ln x\to-\infty\). The form is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\), which must be rewritten as a quotient first.
  2. Keep \(\ln x\) on top and move \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) to the bottom as \(x^{-1/2}\). \[\sqrt{x}\,\ln x = \frac{\ln x}{x^{-1/2}}.\] As \(x\to 0^+\), the top goes to \(-\infty\) and the bottom \(x^{-1/2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\) goes to \(\infty\). The form is \(\tfrac{-\infty}{\infty}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  3. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((\ln x)' = \tfrac{1}{x}\) and \((x^{-1/2})' = -\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{x^{-1/2}} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1/x}{-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}}.\]
  4. Simplify. Dividing by \(-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-3/2}\) is multiplying by \(-2x^{3/2}\). \[\frac{1}{x}\cdot\left(-2x^{3/2}\right) = -2x^{3/2 - 1} = -2x^{1/2} = -2\sqrt{x}.\]
  5. Take the limit. \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(-2\sqrt{x}\right) = -2\cdot 0 = 0.\]

Answer: \(0\).

Problem 13
Find the equation of the tangent line to \(y = 2\sin x + \cos(2x)\) at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).
Hint
You need a point and a slope. For the slope, remember that \(\cos(2x)\) needs the chain rule, so its derivative picks up a factor of 2.
Show full solution
  1. Find the point. At \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\), \(\sin\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\). Also \(2x = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), where \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0\). \[y\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + 0 = \sqrt{2}.\] The point is \(\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}, \sqrt{2}\right)\).
  2. Differentiate term by term. The first term gives \(2\cos x\). For \(\cos(2x)\) use the chain rule with inside \(2x\), whose derivative is 2. \[y' = 2\cos x + \left(-\sin(2x)\right)\cdot 2 = 2\cos x - 2\sin(2x).\]
  3. Find the slope at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\), using \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\) and \(\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 1\). \[y'\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - 2\cdot 1 = \sqrt{2} - 2.\]
  4. Write the line in point-slope form. \[y - \sqrt{2} = \left(\sqrt{2} - 2\right)\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right).\]
  5. Solve for \(y\). First multiply out the constant part. \[\left(\sqrt{2} - 2\right)\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\frac{(\sqrt{2} - 2)\pi}{4} = \frac{(2 - \sqrt{2})\pi}{4}.\] Then add \(\sqrt{2}\) to both sides. \[y = \left(\sqrt{2} - 2\right)x + \sqrt{2} + \frac{(2 - \sqrt{2})\pi}{4}.\]

Answer: \(y - \sqrt{2} = \left(\sqrt{2} - 2\right)\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\), or \(y = \left(\sqrt{2} - 2\right)x + \sqrt{2} + \tfrac{(2 - \sqrt{2})\pi}{4}\).

Where students lose points: writing the derivative of \(\cos(2x)\) as \(-\sin(2x)\) without the factor 2, which gives slope \(\sqrt{2} - 1\). Also, using \(y'\) at \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) as the \(y\)-value of the point instead of \(y\) itself.

Problem 14
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{x - \sin x}{x^3}\).
Hint
It is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\). Expect to use the rule more than once, and substitute again after every step to see whether you are still at \(\tfrac{0}{0}\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(0 - \sin 0 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((x - \sin x)' = 1 - \cos x\) and \((x^3)' = 3x^2\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2}.\]
  3. Check the form again. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(1 - \cos 0 = 1 - 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\). Still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule a second time: \((1 - \cos x)' = 0 - (-\sin x) = \sin x\) and \((3x^2)' = 6x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{6x}.\]
  4. Check the form again. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(\sin 0 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\). Still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule a third time: \((\sin x)' = \cos x\) and \((6x)' = 6\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{6x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{6}.\] (You could also stop one step earlier and use the special limit \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\tfrac{\sin x}{x} = 1\), which gives \(\tfrac{1}{6}\cdot 1\).)
  5. Substitute \(x = 0\). The bottom is no longer 0. \[\frac{\cos 0}{6} = \frac{1}{6}.\]

Answer: \(\tfrac{1}{6}\).

Where students lose points: getting the sign of \((1 - \cos x)'\) wrong (it is \(+\sin x\)), and skipping the written form check before the second and third uses of the rule.

Problem 15
Let \(g(x) = \dfrac{1 + \tan x}{\sec x}\). Find \(g'(x)\) with the quotient rule and simplify it to an expression in \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\). Then check your answer by simplifying \(g(x)\) before differentiating.
Hint
Every term of the quotient-rule numerator has a factor \(\sec x\). Pull it out, then use \(\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x\).
Show full solution
  1. Set up the quotient rule \(\left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\) with \[u = 1 + \tan x,\quad v = \sec x,\quad u' = \sec^2 x,\quad v' = \sec x\tan x.\]
  2. Assemble. \[g'(x) = \frac{(\sec^2 x)(\sec x) - (1 + \tan x)(\sec x\tan x)}{\sec^2 x}.\]
  3. Every term in the numerator has a factor \(\sec x\). Factor it out and cancel it with one \(\sec x\) in the denominator. \[g'(x) = \frac{\sec x\left[\sec^2 x - (1 + \tan x)\tan x\right]}{\sec^2 x} = \frac{\sec^2 x - (1 + \tan x)\tan x}{\sec x}.\] Multiply out \((1 + \tan x)\tan x = \tan x + \tan^2 x\), so the numerator is \(\sec^2 x - \tan x - \tan^2 x\).
  4. Use the identity \(\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x\) in the numerator. \[\sec^2 x - \tan x - \tan^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x - \tan x - \tan^2 x = 1 - \tan x.\] So \(g'(x) = \dfrac{1 - \tan x}{\sec x}\).
  5. Dividing by \(\sec x\) is multiplying by \(\cos x\). Then use \(\tan x\cos x = \tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\cdot\cos x = \sin x\). \[g'(x) = (1 - \tan x)\cos x = \cos x - \tan x\cos x = \cos x - \sin x.\]
  6. Check by simplifying first. Dividing by \(\sec x\) is multiplying by \(\cos x\), so \[g(x) = (1 + \tan x)\cos x = \cos x + \tan x\cos x = \cos x + \sin x.\] Differentiating gives \(g'(x) = -\sin x + \cos x\), the same answer.

Answer: \(g'(x) = \cos x - \sin x\).

Where students lose points: mixing up the derivatives of \(\tan x\) and \(\sec x\) (\(\sec^2 x\) versus \(\sec x\tan x\)), and stopping at the unsimplified quotient when the problem asked for an expression in \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\).

Problem 16
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 1^+}\left(\dfrac{1}{\ln x} - \dfrac{1}{x - 1}\right)\).
Hint
This is \(\infty - \infty\). Combine the two fractions over the common denominator \((x - 1)\ln x\) first, then check the new form.
Show full solution
  1. Identify the form. As \(x\to 1^+\), \(\ln x\to 0\) through positive values, so \(\tfrac{1}{\ln x}\to\infty\). Also \(x - 1\to 0\) through positive values, so \(\tfrac{1}{x - 1}\to\infty\). The form is \(\infty - \infty\), so combine into one fraction first.
  2. Use the common denominator \((x - 1)\ln x\). \[\frac{1}{\ln x} - \frac{1}{x - 1} = \frac{x - 1}{(x - 1)\ln x} - \frac{\ln x}{(x - 1)\ln x} = \frac{x - 1 - \ln x}{(x - 1)\ln x}.\] At \(x = 1\) the top is \(0 - 0 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\cdot 0 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  3. Differentiate the top: \((x - 1 - \ln x)' = 1 - \tfrac{1}{x}\). The bottom is a product, so use the product rule with \(u = x - 1\), \(v = \ln x\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = \tfrac{1}{x}\). Its derivative is \(1\cdot\ln x + (x - 1)\cdot\tfrac{1}{x} = \ln x + \tfrac{x - 1}{x}\). \[\lim_{x\to 1^+}\frac{x - 1 - \ln x}{(x - 1)\ln x} = \lim_{x\to 1^+}\frac{1 - \tfrac{1}{x}}{\ln x + \tfrac{x - 1}{x}}.\]
  4. Clear the small fractions by multiplying the top and bottom by \(x\). On top, \(x\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{x}\right) = x - 1\). On the bottom, \(x\left(\ln x + \tfrac{x - 1}{x}\right) = x\ln x + x - 1\). \[\frac{1 - \tfrac{1}{x}}{\ln x + \tfrac{x - 1}{x}}\cdot\frac{x}{x} = \frac{x - 1}{x\ln x + x - 1}.\]
  5. Check the form again. At \(x = 1\) the top is \(0\) and the bottom is \(1\cdot 0 + 1 - 1 = 0\). Still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule again. The top has derivative 1. For the bottom, \(x\ln x\) has derivative \(1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\tfrac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1\) by the product rule, and \(x - 1\) has derivative 1. So the whole bottom has derivative \(\ln x + 1 + 1 = \ln x + 2\). \[\lim_{x\to 1^+}\frac{x - 1}{x\ln x + x - 1} = \lim_{x\to 1^+}\frac{1}{\ln x + 2}.\]
  6. Substitute \(x = 1\). \[\frac{1}{\ln 1 + 2} = \frac{1}{0 + 2} = \frac{1}{2}.\]

Answer: \(\tfrac{1}{2}\).

Where students lose points: writing \(\infty - \infty = 0\) and stopping, or applying L'Hospital's rule to each fraction separately. The other common slip is forgetting the product rule on the bottom \((x - 1)\ln x\).

Problem 17
The depth of water in a harbor \(t\) hours after midnight is \(D(t) = 8 + 3\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right)\) meters. (a) Find \(D'(t)\). (b) How fast is the depth changing at 2 am? (c) How fast is it changing at 4 pm? (d) Between midnight and noon (\(0 \le t \le 12\)), at what time is the water level falling fastest, and how fast is it falling then?
Hint
Turn each clock time into a value of \(t\) (4 pm is \(t = 16\)). For part (d), \(D'(t)\) is a constant times a cosine, and a cosine is never smaller than \(-1\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Differentiate with the chain rule. The outside is sine and the inside is \(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\), with derivative \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\). The constant 8 has derivative 0. \[D'(t) = 3\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right)\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{6} = \tfrac{3\pi}{6}\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right).\]
  2. (b) 2 am is \(t = 2\). The angle is \(\tfrac{2\pi}{6} = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\), and \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{1}{2}\). \[D'(2) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{\pi}{4}.\] This is positive, so the water is rising.
  3. (c) 4 pm is 16 hours after midnight, so \(t = 16\). The angle is \(\tfrac{16\pi}{6} = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}\). Subtract \(2\pi\) to get the same point on the circle: \(\tfrac{8\pi}{3} - 2\pi = \tfrac{8\pi}{3} - \tfrac{6\pi}{3} = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\), and \(\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\). \[D'(16) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = -\tfrac{\pi}{4}.\] This is negative, so the water is falling.
  4. (d) The water falls fastest when \(D'(t)\) is as negative as possible. Since \(\cos\theta \ge -1\) for every \(\theta\), \[D'(t) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right) \ge \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot(-1) = -\tfrac{\pi}{2},\] with equality exactly when \(\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right) = -1\).
  5. Solve \(\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right) = -1\) on \(0 \le t \le 12\). Here the angle \(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\) runs from \(0\) (at \(t = 0\)) to \(2\pi\) (at \(t = 12\)), and on that range cosine equals \(-1\) only at angle \(\pi\). \[\frac{\pi t}{6} = \pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 6.\] At that time \(D'(6) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cos\pi = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot(-1) = -\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  6. Check with the graph. At \(t = 6\) the depth is \(D(6) = 8 + 3\sin\pi = 8\), the average level. That is halfway between high tide (\(t = 3\), depth \(8 + 3 = 11\)) and low tide (\(t = 9\), depth \(8 - 3 = 5\)). A sine curve is steepest as it passes through its middle level, which agrees.

Answer: (a) \(D'(t) = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi t}{6}\right)\). (b) At 2 am the depth is rising at \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) meters per hour. (c) At 4 pm it is falling at \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) meters per hour. (d) The water falls fastest at 6 am (\(t = 6\)), at \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) meters per hour.

Where students lose points: using \(t = 4\) for 4 pm, and in part (d) finding where the depth \(D\) is lowest (\(t = 9\)) instead of where the rate \(D'\) is most negative (\(t = 6\)). Answers without "meters per hour" or without saying rising or falling also lose points.

Problem 18
Find the derivative of the function \(f(x) = \ln\left[\sin\left(x^2 e^{3x}\right)\right]\).
Hint
There are three layers: \(\ln\) on the outside, then \(\sin\), then the product \(x^2e^{3x}\). Peel them from the outside in, one derivative per layer.
Show full solution
  1. Identify the layers from the outside in. The outside function is \(\ln u\). Inside it is \(u = \sin w\). Inside that is \(w = x^2 e^{3x}\), which is a product. The chain rule will be used twice and the product rule once.
  2. Differentiate the outside layer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln u = \dfrac{u'}{u}\) with \(u = \sin\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)\). \[f'(x) = \frac{\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)}{\sin\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)}.\]
  3. Differentiate the middle layer with the chain rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin w = \cos w\cdot w'\). \[\frac{d}{dx}\sin\left(x^2e^{3x}\right) = \cos\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2e^{3x}\right).\]
  4. Differentiate the inside \(w = x^2e^{3x}\) with the product rule, using \[p = x^2,\quad q = e^{3x},\quad p' = 2x,\quad q' = 3e^{3x}\] (the last one by the chain rule, since the inside \(3x\) has derivative 3). \[w' = 2x\cdot e^{3x} + x^2\cdot 3e^{3x} = 2xe^{3x} + 3x^2e^{3x} = xe^{3x}(2 + 3x).\]
  5. Put the pieces together. \[f'(x) = \frac{\cos\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)\cdot xe^{3x}(2 + 3x)}{\sin\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)}.\]
  6. Simplify with \(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \cot\theta\) for \(\theta = x^2e^{3x}\). \[f'(x) = xe^{3x}(2 + 3x)\cot\left(x^2e^{3x}\right).\]

Answer: \(f'(x) = \left(2xe^{3x} + 3x^2e^{3x}\right)\cot\left(x^2e^{3x}\right) = xe^{3x}(2 + 3x)\cot\left(x^2e^{3x}\right)\).

Where students lose points: stopping after one layer (writing \(\tfrac{\cos(x^2e^{3x})}{\sin(x^2e^{3x})}\) with no inside derivative), and writing \(q' = e^{3x}\) instead of \(3e^{3x}\) in the product rule.

Problem 19
Find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{2x} - 1 - 2\sin x}{x^2}\). Justify each step.
Hint
Substitute \(x = 0\) in the top and bottom first. You will need the rule twice, and "justify" means a written form check before each use.
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the numerator is \(e^0 - 1 - 2\sin 0 = 1 - 1 - 0 = 0\) and the denominator is \(0^2 = 0\). The limit has the form \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the numerator and denominator separately. The numerator has derivative \(2e^{2x} - 2\cos x\) (chain rule on \(e^{2x}\)). The denominator has derivative \(2x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^{2x} - 1 - 2\sin x}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2e^{2x} - 2\cos x}{2x}.\]
  3. Check the form again. At \(x = 0\) the numerator is \(2\cdot 1 - 2\cdot 1 = 0\) and the denominator is \(0\). Still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies again. The numerator has derivative \(4e^{2x} - 2(-\sin x) = 4e^{2x} + 2\sin x\), and the denominator has derivative \(2\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2e^{2x} - 2\cos x}{2x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{4e^{2x} + 2\sin x}{2}.\]
  4. Substitute \(x = 0\). The denominator is the nonzero constant 2, so substitution is allowed. \[\frac{4e^0 + 2\sin 0}{2} = \frac{4 + 0}{2} = 2.\]

Answer: \(2\).

Where students lose points: writing the derivative of \(e^{2x}\) as \(e^{2x}\) (missing the 2), and losing track of the sign in \(-2\cos x \to +2\sin x\). Leaving out the form check before the second use costs the "justify" points.

Problem 20
Find the equation of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x) = e^{\tan x}\sin(2x)\) at the point where \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).
Hint
Product rule with \(u = e^{\tan x}\) and \(v = \sin(2x)\). Each of \(u'\) and \(v'\) needs the chain rule. Plug in \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) only after you have the full derivative.
Show full solution
  1. Find the point. At \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\), \(\tan\tfrac{\pi}{4} = 1\) and \(\sin\left(2\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 1\). \[f\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = e^1\cdot 1 = e.\] The point is \(\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}, e\right)\).
  2. Differentiate with the product rule, using \[u = e^{\tan x},\quad v = \sin(2x),\quad u' = e^{\tan x}\sec^2 x,\quad v' = 2\cos(2x).\] Here \(u'\) comes from the chain rule with inside \(\tan x\) (whose derivative is \(\sec^2 x\)), and \(v'\) comes from the chain rule with inside \(2x\). \[f'(x) = u'v + uv' = e^{\tan x}\sec^2 x\,\sin(2x) + 2e^{\tan x}\cos(2x).\]
  3. Find the slope at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\). Use \(e^{\tan(\pi/4)} = e^1 = e\), \(\sec^2\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \left(\sqrt{2}\right)^2 = 2\), \(\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 1\), and \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0\). \[f'\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = e\cdot 2\cdot 1 + 2e\cdot 0 = 2e.\]
  4. Write the line in point-slope form. \[y - e = 2e\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right).\]
  5. Solve for \(y\). Multiply out \(2e\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\tfrac{2e\pi}{4} = -\tfrac{e\pi}{2}\), then add \(e\) to both sides. \[y = 2ex - \frac{e\pi}{2} + e.\]

Answer: \(y - e = 2e\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\), or \(y = 2ex + e - \tfrac{e\pi}{2}\).

Where students lose points: forgetting the \(\sec^2 x\) factor in the derivative of \(e^{\tan x}\), and using \(\sec^2\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}\) (that is \(\cos^2\tfrac{\pi}{4}\)) instead of 2.

Problem 21
Evaluate each limit, if it exists. (a) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0^+}(\sin x)(\ln x)\). (b) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\left(\csc x - \cot x\right)\).
Hint
(a) is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\): keep \(\ln x\) on top and move \(\sin x\) to the bottom as \(\csc x\). (b) is a difference: write both terms over \(\sin x\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Identify the form. As \(x\to 0^+\), \(\sin x\to 0\) and \(\ln x\to-\infty\). The form is \(0\cdot(-\infty)\), which must be rewritten as a quotient before L'Hospital's rule can be used.
  2. Keep \(\ln x\) on top. Since \(\sin x = \dfrac{1}{\csc x}\), \[(\sin x)(\ln x) = \frac{\ln x}{\csc x}.\] As \(x\to 0^+\), the top goes to \(-\infty\). The bottom \(\csc x = \tfrac{1}{\sin x}\) goes to \(\infty\), because \(\sin x\to 0\) through positive values. The form is \(\tfrac{-\infty}{\infty}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  3. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((\ln x)' = \tfrac{1}{x}\) and \((\csc x)' = -\csc x\cot x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{\csc x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1/x}{-\csc x\cot x}.\]
  4. Simplify. Since \(\tfrac{1}{\csc x} = \sin x\) and \(\tfrac{1}{\cot x} = \tan x\), \[\frac{1/x}{-\csc x\cot x} = -\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{\csc x}\cdot\frac{1}{\cot x} = -\frac{1}{x}\cdot\sin x\cdot\tan x = -\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\tan x.\]
  5. Take the limit using the special limit \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\tfrac{\sin x}{x} = 1\) and \(\tan 0 = 0\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(-\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\tan x\right) = -1\cdot 0 = 0.\]
  6. (b) Identify the form. As \(x\to 0^+\), \(\csc x\to\infty\) and \(\cot x = \tfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\to\infty\). As \(x\to 0^-\), both go to \(-\infty\). Either way the form is a difference of two infinite quantities of the same sign, which is indeterminate. Combine into one fraction.
  7. Write both in terms of sine and cosine and use the common denominator \(\sin x\). \[\csc x - \cot x = \frac{1}{\sin x} - \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{1 - \cos x}{\sin x}.\] At \(x = 0\) the top is \(1 - 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  8. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((1 - \cos x)' = 0 - (-\sin x) = \sin x\) and \((\sin x)' = \cos x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{\sin x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}.\]
  9. Substitute \(x = 0\). The bottom is \(\cos 0 = 1\), not 0. \[\frac{\sin 0}{\cos 0} = \frac{0}{1} = 0.\]

Answer: (a) \(0\). (b) \(0\).

Where students lose points: in (a), putting \(\tfrac{1}{\ln x}\) on the bottom, which makes the derivative much harder, or differentiating the product directly. In (b), writing \(\infty - \infty = 0\) with no work: the answer happens to be 0, but the grader gives no credit without the combined fraction and the form check.

Problem 22
Verify the two derivative formulas \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\csc x = -\csc x\cot x\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot x = -\csc^2 x\). Use only \(\csc x = \dfrac{1}{\sin x}\), \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), the derivatives of \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\), and the quotient rule.
Hint
"Verify" means you may not quote the formula you are proving. Write each function as a quotient of sines and cosines, apply the quotient rule, then regroup the answer into \(\csc\) and \(\cot\).
Show full solution
  1. Set up the quotient rule \(\left(\dfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\) for \(\csc x = \dfrac{1}{\sin x}\), with \[u = 1,\quad v = \sin x,\quad u' = 0,\quad v' = \cos x.\]
  2. Assemble. \[\frac{d}{dx}\csc x = \frac{(0)(\sin x) - (1)(\cos x)}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-\cos x}{\sin^2 x}.\]
  3. Split \(\sin^2 x\) into \(\sin x\cdot\sin x\) and regroup into two factors. \[\frac{-\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\frac{1}{\sin x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = -\csc x\cot x.\] This is the first formula.
  4. Set up the quotient rule for \(\cot x = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}\), with \[u = \cos x,\quad v = \sin x,\quad u' = -\sin x,\quad v' = \cos x.\]
  5. Assemble and multiply out the numerator. \[\frac{d}{dx}\cot x = \frac{(-\sin x)(\sin x) - (\cos x)(\cos x)}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x}.\]
  6. Factor out the minus sign and use \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\). \[\frac{-\left(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x\right)}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-1}{\sin^2 x} = -\left(\frac{1}{\sin x}\right)^2 = -\csc^2 x.\] This is the second formula.

Answer: Both formulas hold: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\csc x = \dfrac{-\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc x\cot x\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot x = \dfrac{-1}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc^2 x\).

Problem 23
Answer each part and justify it in one or two sentences.
(a) A student writes \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^x + x}{3x + 2} = \lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^x + 1}{3} = \dfrac{2}{3}\). Explain what is wrong and find the correct value.
(b) True or false: if \(\lim\limits_{x\to 2}f(x) = 3\) and \(\lim\limits_{x\to 2}g(x) = 0\), then L'Hospital's rule can be used to find \(\lim\limits_{x\to 2}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\).
(c) True or false: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x\right) = 0\) for every \(x\).
(d) True or false: at every \(x\) where \(\sin x\) has a local maximum, \(\cos x = 0\).
Hint
For (a) and (b), substitute and see what form you really have. For (c), there are two ways: differentiate directly, or use an identity first. For (d), think about what the derivative of a smooth function is at a peak.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Check the form the student skipped. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(e^0 + 0 = 1\) and the bottom is \(3(0) + 2 = 2\). The form is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), which is not indeterminate, so L'Hospital's rule does not apply. The bottom is not 0, so the limit is found by substitution. \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^x + x}{3x + 2} = \frac{1}{2}.\] The student's \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) is wrong because the rule was used without the \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\) condition.
  2. (b) False. The form is \(\tfrac{3}{0}\), a nonzero number over zero, which is not indeterminate. For a concrete example take \(f(x) = 3\) and \(g(x) = x - 2\). Then \(\tfrac{3}{x - 2}\to\infty\) as \(x\to 2^+\) and \(\to-\infty\) as \(x\to 2^-\), so the limit does not exist. Using the rule anyway would give \(\lim\limits_{x\to 2}\tfrac{0}{1} = 0\), which is wrong.
  3. (c) True. By the identity, \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\) for every \(x\), and the derivative of the constant 1 is 0. Directly, with the generalized power rule: \[\frac{d}{dx}\left(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x\right) = 2\sin x\cos x + 2\cos x(-\sin x) = 2\sin x\cos x - 2\sin x\cos x = 0.\]
  4. (d) True. The function \(\sin x\) is differentiable everywhere, and at a local maximum of a differentiable function the derivative is 0. The derivative of \(\sin x\) is \(\cos x\), so \(\cos x = 0\) there. You can also see it directly: the maxima of \(\sin x\) are at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi n\), and \(\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi n\right) = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0\).

Answer: (a) The form is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), not indeterminate, so the rule does not apply; the correct value is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). (b) False. (c) True. (d) True.

Problem 24
Evaluate \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{e^{3x} - 1}{x^2}\), or explain why it does not exist. Also give the two one-sided limits.
Hint
One use of the rule is legal. After that, substitute again before you differentiate a second time.
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(e^0 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0^2 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \((e^{3x} - 1)' = 3e^{3x}\) (chain rule) and \((x^2)' = 2x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{e^{3x} - 1}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{3e^{3x}}{2x}.\]
  3. Check the form again. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(3e^0 = 3\) and the bottom is \(2(0) = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{3}{0}\), a nonzero number over zero. This is not indeterminate, so do not use the rule again. The quotient blows up, and the sign of the bottom decides the direction.
  4. From the right (\(x\to 0^+\)): the top is close to 3, which is positive, and the bottom \(2x\) is a small positive number. A positive number divided by a tiny positive number is huge and positive. \[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{3e^{3x}}{2x} = \infty.\]
  5. From the left (\(x\to 0^-\)): the top is still close to 3, and the bottom \(2x\) is a small negative number. \[\lim_{x\to 0^-}\frac{3e^{3x}}{2x} = -\infty.\]
  6. L'Hospital's rule holds for one-sided limits too, so these are also the one-sided limits of the original \(\tfrac{e^{3x} - 1}{x^2}\). The two sides go in opposite directions, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

Answer: The limit does not exist. As \(x\to 0^+\) the quotient goes to \(\infty\), and as \(x\to 0^-\) it goes to \(-\infty\).

Problem 25
Evaluate each limit. (a) \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}x\tan\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right)\). (b) \(\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} - x\right)\).
Hint
(a) is a product \(\infty\cdot 0\) in disguise: move the \(x\) into the bottom as \(\tfrac{1}{x}\). (b) has a square root, so multiply and divide by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x\). L'Hospital's rule is not needed in (b).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Identify the form. As \(x\to\infty\), the factor \(x\to\infty\). Also \(\tfrac{2}{x}\to 0\), so \(\tan\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right)\to\tan 0 = 0\). The form is \(\infty\cdot 0\), so rewrite it as a quotient.
  2. Move \(x\) to the bottom as \(\tfrac{1}{x}\). \[x\tan\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right) = \frac{\tan(2/x)}{1/x}.\] As \(x\to\infty\), the top goes to 0 and the bottom goes to 0. The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  3. Differentiate the top with the chain rule. The inside is \(\tfrac{2}{x} = 2x^{-1}\), with derivative \(-2x^{-2} = -\tfrac{2}{x^2}\). \[\frac{d}{dx}\tan\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right) = \sec^2\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right)\cdot\left(-\frac{2}{x^2}\right).\] The bottom \(\tfrac{1}{x} = x^{-1}\) has derivative \(-x^{-2} = -\tfrac{1}{x^2}\).
  4. Form the new quotient and cancel the common factor \(-\tfrac{1}{x^2}\). \[\frac{\sec^2(2/x)\cdot\left(-\tfrac{2}{x^2}\right)}{-\tfrac{1}{x^2}} = 2\sec^2\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right).\]
  5. Take the limit. As \(x\to\infty\), \(\tfrac{2}{x}\to 0\), and \(\sec^2 0 = \tfrac{1}{\cos^2 0} = 1\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}2\sec^2\left(\tfrac{2}{x}\right) = 2\cdot 1 = 2.\]
  6. (b) Identify the form. As \(x\to\infty\), both \(\sqrt{x^2 + 6x}\) and \(x\) go to \(\infty\). The form is \(\infty - \infty\), which is indeterminate. There is a square root, so use the conjugate.
  7. Multiply and divide by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x\). On top, use \((A - B)(A + B) = A^2 - B^2\). \[\left(\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} - x\right)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x} = \frac{(x^2 + 6x) - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x} = \frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x}.\]
  8. Divide the top and bottom by \(x\). For \(x \gt 0\), \(x = \sqrt{x^2}\), so \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x}}{x} = \sqrt{\dfrac{x^2 + 6x}{x^2}} = \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{6}{x}}\). \[\frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2 + 6x} + x} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{6}{x}} + 1}.\]
  9. Take the limit. As \(x\to\infty\), \(\tfrac{6}{x}\to 0\). \[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{6}{\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{6}{x}} + 1} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{1} + 1} = \frac{6}{2} = 3.\]

Answer: (a) \(2\). (b) \(3\).

Problem 26
The functions \(f\) and \(g\) have continuous derivatives, and you are told only these values: \[f(3) = 0,\qquad g(3) = 0,\qquad f'(3) = 4,\qquad g'(3) = -2.\] Find each limit, or explain why it does not exist. (a) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}\). (b) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}\dfrac{f(x)}{x^2 - 9}\). (c) \(\lim\limits_{x\to 3}\dfrac{f(x) + 2}{g(x)}\).
Hint
You do not need formulas for \(f\) and \(g\). Check each form with the given values. When it is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), the rule turns the limit into derivatives at 3, which you are given.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Check the form. Because \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable, they are continuous, so \(f(x)\to f(3) = 0\) and \(g(x)\to g(3) = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so L'Hospital's rule applies. \[\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}.\]
  2. The derivatives are continuous and \(g'(3) \ne 0\), so substitute \(x = 3\). \[\frac{f'(3)}{g'(3)} = \frac{4}{-2} = -2.\]
  3. (b) Check the form. The top goes to \(f(3) = 0\) and the bottom goes to \(3^2 - 9 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\). Differentiate the top and bottom separately: \(f(x)\) gives \(f'(x)\), and \(x^2 - 9\) gives \(2x\). \[\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{f(x)}{x^2 - 9} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{f'(x)}{2x} = \frac{f'(3)}{2\cdot 3} = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}.\]
  4. (c) Check the form. The top goes to \(f(3) + 2 = 0 + 2 = 2\) and the bottom goes to \(g(3) = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{2}{0}\), which is not indeterminate. L'Hospital's rule does not apply, and the quotient blows up. The sign of \(g\) near 3 decides the direction.
  5. Find the sign of \(g\) near 3. Since \(g'(3) = -2 \lt 0\) and \(g'\) is continuous, \(g'(x) \lt 0\) for \(x\) near 3, so \(g\) is decreasing there. A decreasing function that equals 0 at \(x = 3\) is positive just to the left of 3 and negative just to the right.
  6. Conclude. From the left, the top is near 2 and the bottom is a tiny positive number, so the quotient goes to \(\infty\). From the right, the bottom is a tiny negative number, so the quotient goes to \(-\infty\). The two sides disagree, so the limit does not exist.

Answer: (a) \(-2\). (b) \(\tfrac{2}{3}\). (c) Does not exist: the quotient goes to \(\infty\) as \(x\to 3^-\) and to \(-\infty\) as \(x\to 3^+\).

Problem 27
The graph of \(f(x) = \sqrt{3}\sin x + \cos x\) on the interval \([0, \pi]\) is shown below.
(a) From the graph, estimate where \(f\) has its absolute maximum and absolute minimum on \([0, \pi]\). (b) Find all critical numbers of \(f\) in \([0, \pi]\) exactly. (c) Find the exact absolute maximum and minimum values and where they occur.
Hint
Set \(f'(x) = 0\) and divide by \(\cos x\) to get an equation for \(\tan x\). It has two solutions between \(0\) and \(2\pi\), but only one of them is inside \([0, \pi]\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Read the graph. The curve starts at height 1 at \(x = 0\), climbs to a peak of about 2 a little past \(x = 1\), then falls to about \(-1\) at the right end \(x = \pi\). So the maximum looks like it is near \(x = 1\) and the minimum looks like it is at the endpoint \(x = \pi\).
  2. (b) Differentiate term by term. \[f'(x) = \sqrt{3}\cos x - \sin x.\] This is defined for every \(x\), so the only critical numbers are where \(f'(x) = 0\).
  3. Solve \(f'(x) = 0\). \[\sqrt{3}\cos x - \sin x = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin x = \sqrt{3}\cos x.\] At a solution \(\cos x\) cannot be 0: if \(\cos x = 0\), the equation would force \(\sin x = 0\) too, but \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are never both 0 (since \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\)). So divide both sides by \(\cos x\). \[\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \sqrt{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan x = \sqrt{3}.\]
  4. List the solutions and keep only those in the interval. From the table, \(\tan x = \sqrt{3}\) at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\) and at \(x = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\) (between \(0\) and \(2\pi\)). Since \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3} \gt \pi\), it is outside \([0, \pi]\) and is thrown out. The only critical number in the interval is \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\).
  5. (c) Use the closed interval method: evaluate \(f\) at the critical number in the interval and at both endpoints. \[\begin{aligned} f(0) &= \sqrt{3}\cdot 0 + 1 = 1,\\ f\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) &= \sqrt{3}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} = 2,\\ f(\pi) &= \sqrt{3}\cdot 0 + (-1) = -1.\end{aligned}\]
  6. Compare the three values \(1\), \(2\), \(-1\). The largest is 2, at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{3} \approx 1.05\). The smallest is \(-1\), at the endpoint \(x = \pi\). This matches the estimates from the graph.

Answer: (a) From the graph, the maximum is near \(x = 1\) and the minimum is at the endpoint \(x = \pi\). (b) The only critical number in \([0, \pi]\) is \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\) (the other solution \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\) is outside the interval). (c) The absolute maximum is \(2\) at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\), and the absolute minimum is \(-1\) at \(x = \pi\).

Problem 28
A particle moves along a straight line. Its position after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t) = 3\cos(2t) + 4\sin(2t)\) meters. (a) Find the velocity \(v(t)\). (b) Find the velocity at \(t = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\) and say which way the particle is moving. (c) Find the acceleration \(a(t)\) and its value at \(t = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\). (d) Show that \(a(t) = -4s(t)\) for every \(t\). (e) Find the positive constant \(k\) for which \(y = \sin(kt)\) satisfies \(y'' + 25y = 0\) for every \(t\).
Hint
Velocity is the first derivative of position and acceleration is the second. Each term picks up a factor of 2 from the chain rule. For (e), compute \(y''\), add \(25y\), and ask what makes the result 0 for every \(t\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Velocity is \(v(t) = s'(t)\). Differentiate each term with the chain rule; the inside \(2t\) has derivative 2. \[v(t) = 3\left(-\sin(2t)\right)\cdot 2 + 4\cos(2t)\cdot 2 = -6\sin(2t) + 8\cos(2t).\]
  2. (b) At \(t = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\) the angle is \(2t = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), where \(\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 1\) and \(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2} = 0\). \[v\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -6(1) + 8(0) = -6.\] The velocity is negative, so the particle is moving in the negative direction at 6 meters per second.
  3. (c) Acceleration is \(a(t) = v'(t)\). Differentiate again with the chain rule. \[a(t) = -6\cos(2t)\cdot 2 + 8\left(-\sin(2t)\right)\cdot 2 = -12\cos(2t) - 16\sin(2t).\] At \(t = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\): \[a\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -12(0) - 16(1) = -16 \text{ meters per second per second}.\]
  4. (d) Multiply the position by \(-4\) and compare. \[-4s(t) = -4\left(3\cos(2t) + 4\sin(2t)\right) = -12\cos(2t) - 16\sin(2t) = a(t).\]
  5. (e) Differentiate \(y = \sin(kt)\) twice with the chain rule (the inside \(kt\) has derivative \(k\)). \[y' = k\cos(kt), \qquad y'' = k\cdot\left(-\sin(kt)\right)\cdot k = -k^2\sin(kt).\]
  6. Substitute into \(y'' + 25y\) and factor. \[y'' + 25y = -k^2\sin(kt) + 25\sin(kt) = (25 - k^2)\sin(kt).\] For this to be 0 for every \(t\), the factor \(25 - k^2\) must be 0, because \(\sin(kt)\) is not always 0. So \(k^2 = 25\), and the positive solution is \(k = 5\).

Answer: (a) \(v(t) = -6\sin(2t) + 8\cos(2t)\) meters per second. (b) \(v\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -6\), so the particle moves in the negative direction at 6 meters per second. (c) \(a(t) = -12\cos(2t) - 16\sin(2t)\), and \(a\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = -16\) meters per second per second. (d) \(-4s(t) = -12\cos(2t) - 16\sin(2t) = a(t)\). (e) \(k = 5\).

Problem 29
Find the value of the constant \(a\) for which \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\sin(2x) - ax}{x^3}\) is a finite number. Then find the value of the limit for that \(a\). Justify each step.
Hint
The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) for every \(a\). After one use of the rule, the bottom goes to 0. The limit can only be finite if the top also goes to 0 there, and that condition gives \(a\).
Show full solution
  1. Check the form. At \(x = 0\) the top is \(\sin 0 - a\cdot 0 = 0\) and the bottom is \(0^3 = 0\). The form is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\) no matter what \(a\) is, so L'Hospital's rule applies.
  2. Differentiate the top and bottom separately. The top gives \(2\cos(2x) - a\) (chain rule on \(\sin(2x)\)), and the bottom gives \(3x^2\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin(2x) - ax}{x^3} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2\cos(2x) - a}{3x^2}.\]
  3. Find the condition on \(a\). The bottom \(3x^2\to 0\). The top goes to \(2\cos 0 - a = 2 - a\). If \(a \ne 2\), the form is a nonzero number over 0, so the quotient blows up and the limit is not a finite number. So the limit can only be finite when \(2 - a = 0\), that is \(a = 2\).
  4. With \(a = 2\), the new limit is \(\lim\limits_{x\to 0}\dfrac{2\cos(2x) - 2}{3x^2}\). Check the form: the top is \(2 - 2 = 0\) and the bottom is 0. It is \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule again. The top has derivative \(2\left(-\sin(2x)\right)\cdot 2 = -4\sin(2x)\), and the bottom has derivative \(6x\). \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2\cos(2x) - 2}{3x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{-4\sin(2x)}{6x}.\]
  5. Check the form again. The top is \(-4\sin 0 = 0\) and the bottom is 0. Still \(\tfrac{0}{0}\), so apply the rule a third time. The top has derivative \(-4\cos(2x)\cdot 2 = -8\cos(2x)\), and the bottom has derivative 6. \[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{-4\sin(2x)}{6x} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{-8\cos(2x)}{6}.\]
  6. Substitute \(x = 0\). The bottom is the nonzero constant 6. \[\frac{-8\cos 0}{6} = \frac{-8}{6} = -\frac{4}{3}.\]

Answer: \(a = 2\), and then the limit is \(-\tfrac{4}{3}\).
