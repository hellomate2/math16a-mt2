Section 5.3: Higher derivatives, concavity, and the second derivative test

The big idea

The second derivative is the derivative of the derivative, and its sign tells you which way the graph bends. Positive means the graph bends up like a cup, and negative means it bends down like an upside-down cup. Remember this one pairing: the first derivative says whether the graph goes up or down, the second derivative says how it bends, and at a flat spot a cup is a minimum while a cap is a maximum.

In Section 5.2 you used the first derivative \(f'\) to find where a function goes up and where it goes down. This section takes one more derivative. The derivative of \(f'\) does three jobs. It tells you how the graph of \(f\) bends. It gives a second way to decide whether a critical number is a local maximum or a local minimum. And in physics it is the acceleration of a moving object. Everything here is built from derivative rules you already know, applied twice or more.

Second and higher derivatives

The second derivative of a function \(f\) is the derivative of \(f'\). In other words, you differentiate \(f\), and then you differentiate the answer. Nothing new is needed: you use the same power, product, quotient, and chain rules again on \(f'\).

Notation for higher derivatives

All of these mean the second derivative of \(y = f(x)\):

\[ f'' \qquad f''(x) \qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \qquad \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\left[f(x)\right] \qquad D_x^2\left[f(x)\right] \]

The third derivative is the derivative of \(f''\) and is written \(f'''\) or \(f'''(x)\). From the fourth derivative on, primes get hard to count, so you write the number in parentheses: \(f^{(4)}(x)\), \(f^{(5)}(x)\), and in general the \(n\)th derivative \(f^{(n)}(x)\), which is the derivative of the \((n-1)\)th derivative.

The parentheses matter. \(f^{(4)}(x)\) is the fourth derivative of \(f\), while \(f^4(x)\) means \(\left[f(x)\right]^4\), the function raised to the fourth power.

In plain words: keep taking the derivative of your last answer. The number of prime marks, or the number in parentheses, counts how many times you have differentiated.

A useful fact about polynomials: each derivative lowers the degree by one. If \(f\) is a polynomial of degree \(m\), then \(f'\) has degree \(m-1\), \(f''\) has degree \(m-2\), and so on, until \(f^{(m)}\) has degree 0, which means it is a constant. Every derivative after that is 0.

The symbol \(n!\) (read "\(n\) factorial") means the product \(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n\). For example \(4! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24\). Factorials show up whenever you differentiate a power many times, because each step multiplies by the current exponent.

Worked example 1

Compute the first four derivatives of each function, and use the pattern to find the 100th derivative. (a) \(f(x) = x^4 - 2x^3 + 5x - 7\). (b) \(g(x) = e^{-3x}\). (c) For \(h(x) = \dfrac{1}{x+1}\), find the first four derivatives and a formula for \(h^{(n)}(x)\).

How to spot this type: the problem says "first four derivatives", "the 100th derivative", or "a formula for the \(n\)th derivative", so you differentiate a few times, keep the coefficients visible, and read off the pattern.

  1. For (a), differentiate term by term with the power rule, four times. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 4x^3 - 6x^2 + 5 \\ f''(x) &= 12x^2 - 12x \\ f'''(x) &= 24x - 12 \\ f^{(4)}(x) &= 24. \end{aligned}\]
  2. The fourth derivative is the constant 24, and the derivative of a constant is 0. So every derivative after the fourth is 0. \[ f^{(5)}(x) = 0, \qquad f^{(100)}(x) = 0. \]
  3. For (b), use the chain rule: the derivative of \(e^{kx}\) is \(k e^{kx}\). Here \(k = -3\), so each derivative multiplies by another factor of \(-3\). \[\begin{aligned} g'(x) &= -3e^{-3x} \\ g''(x) &= (-3)(-3)e^{-3x} = 9e^{-3x} \\ g'''(x) &= 9(-3)e^{-3x} = -27e^{-3x} \\ g^{(4)}(x) &= -27(-3)e^{-3x} = 81e^{-3x}. \end{aligned}\]
  4. After \(n\) derivatives there are \(n\) factors of \(-3\), so \(g^{(n)}(x) = (-3)^n e^{-3x}\). For \(n = 100\) the exponent is even, so the sign is positive. \[ g^{(100)}(x) = (-3)^{100}e^{-3x} = 3^{100}e^{-3x}. \]
  5. For (c), rewrite \(h\) as a power so the power rule and chain rule apply (the inside function \(x + 1\) has derivative 1). \[ h(x) = (x+1)^{-1}. \]
  6. Differentiate four times. Each time, bring down the exponent and lower it by one. \[\begin{aligned} h'(x) &= -1(x+1)^{-2} = -(x+1)^{-2} \\ h''(x) &= -1 \cdot (-2)(x+1)^{-3} = 2(x+1)^{-3} \\ h'''(x) &= 2 \cdot (-3)(x+1)^{-4} = -6(x+1)^{-4} \\ h^{(4)}(x) &= -6 \cdot (-4)(x+1)^{-5} = 24(x+1)^{-5}. \end{aligned}\]
  7. Read the pattern. The coefficients are \(1, 2, 6, 24\), which are \(1!, 2!, 3!, 4!\). The signs alternate, starting negative, which is \((-1)^n\). The exponent after \(n\) derivatives is \(-(n+1)\). \[ h^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n\, n!\,(x+1)^{-(n+1)} = \frac{(-1)^n\, n!}{(x+1)^{n+1}}. \] Check with \(n = 2\): \(\dfrac{(-1)^2 \cdot 2!}{(x+1)^3} = \dfrac{2}{(x+1)^3}\), which matches \(h''(x)\).

Answer: (a) \(f' = 4x^3 - 6x^2 + 5\), \(f'' = 12x^2 - 12x\), \(f''' = 24x - 12\), \(f^{(4)} = 24\), and \(f^{(100)} = 0\). (b) \(g' = -3e^{-3x}\), \(g'' = 9e^{-3x}\), \(g''' = -27e^{-3x}\), \(g^{(4)} = 81e^{-3x}\), and \(g^{(100)} = 3^{100}e^{-3x}\). (c) \(h' = -(x+1)^{-2}\), \(h'' = 2(x+1)^{-3}\), \(h''' = -6(x+1)^{-4}\), \(h^{(4)} = 24(x+1)^{-5}\), and \(h^{(n)}(x) = \dfrac{(-1)^n\, n!}{(x+1)^{n+1}}\).

For products, you use the product rule at every step. The trick that keeps the work short is to factor out the common exponential after each step, so the next product rule has simple pieces.

Worked example 2

Let \(f(x) = xe^{2x}\). Find \(f'(x)\), \(f''(x)\), and \(f'''(x)\), find \(f''(0)\), and guess a formula for \(f^{(n)}(x)\).

How to spot this type: the function is a product, usually a power of \(x\) times an exponential, and you need several derivatives. Use the product rule each time and factor out the exponential before the next step.

  1. First derivative by the product rule. Let \(u = x\) and \(v = e^{2x}\). Then \(u' = 1\), and by the chain rule \(v' = 2e^{2x}\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= u'v + uv' = 1 \cdot e^{2x} + x \cdot 2e^{2x} \\ &= (2x + 1)e^{2x}. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Second derivative by the product rule again. Now \(u = 2x + 1\) and \(v = e^{2x}\), so \(u' = 2\) and \(v' = 2e^{2x}\). \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= 2e^{2x} + (2x+1) \cdot 2e^{2x} \\ &= (2 + 4x + 2)e^{2x} \\ &= (4x + 4)e^{2x}. \end{aligned}\]
  3. Third derivative the same way, with \(u = 4x + 4\), \(u' = 4\), \(v = e^{2x}\), \(v' = 2e^{2x}\). \[\begin{aligned} f'''(x) &= 4e^{2x} + (4x+4) \cdot 2e^{2x} \\ &= (4 + 8x + 8)e^{2x} \\ &= (8x + 12)e^{2x}. \end{aligned}\]
  4. Evaluate the second derivative at 0, using \(e^0 = 1\). \[ f''(0) = (4 \cdot 0 + 4)e^{0} = 4. \]
  5. Look for the pattern. Write each answer with a factor pulled out: \(f' = 1 \cdot (2x + 1)e^{2x}\), \(f'' = 2(2x + 2)e^{2x}\), \(f''' = 4(2x + 3)e^{2x}\). The front factor doubles each time (\(1, 2, 4\) are \(2^0, 2^1, 2^2\)), and the number added to \(2x\) is the number of derivatives taken. \[ f^{(n)}(x) = 2^{n-1}(2x + n)e^{2x}. \] Check with \(n = 3\): \(2^2(2x + 3)e^{2x} = (8x + 12)e^{2x}\), which matches.

Answer: \(f'(x) = (2x+1)e^{2x}\), \(f''(x) = (4x+4)e^{2x}\), \(f'''(x) = (8x+12)e^{2x}\), \(f''(0) = 4\), and \(f^{(n)}(x) = 2^{n-1}(2x+n)e^{2x}\).

Common mistake. For a quotient, using the quotient rule twice in a row creates a large numerator, and students often forget to cancel. After the first quotient rule, the denominator is \((\text{bottom})^2\). After the second, it is \((\text{bottom})^4\), and every term in the numerator has a factor of the bottom, so cancel one copy to get \((\text{bottom})^3\). Even better, rewrite first when you can: \(\dfrac{x}{x-3} = \dfrac{(x - 3) + 3}{x-3} = 1 + 3(x-3)^{-1}\), and then only the power rule and chain rule are needed.

Velocity and acceleration

Suppose an object moves along a straight line, and \(s(t)\) is its position at time \(t\). The velocity is \(v(t) = s'(t)\). Its sign tells you the direction: \(v(t) \gt 0\) means \(s\) is increasing, so the object moves in the positive direction, and \(v(t) \lt 0\) means it moves in the negative direction. The speed is \(|v(t)|\), the absolute value of the velocity. It says how fast the object moves, without the direction.

The acceleration is the rate of change of the velocity: \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)\). Its sign tells you whether the velocity is increasing or decreasing.

An object is speeding up when its speed \(|v|\) is increasing, and slowing down when its speed is decreasing. The sign of \(a\) alone does not decide this. Suppose \(v = -5\) and then a moment later \(v = -3\). The velocity went up, so \(a \gt 0\), but the speed went from 5 down to 3, so the object slowed down. The rule is to compare the signs of \(v\) and \(a\).

Velocity \(v(t)\)Acceleration \(a(t)\)Direction of motionSpeed

Speeding up or slowing down

When \(v(t)\) and \(a(t)\) have the same sign, the object is speeding up. When they have opposite signs, the object is slowing down.

In plain words: if the push is in the same direction as the motion, the object goes faster. If the push is against the motion, it goes slower.

Worked example 3

A particle moves along a line so that its position after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t\) meters, for \(t \ge 0\). (a) Find the velocity and the acceleration. (b) When is the particle at rest? (c) On which intervals is it speeding up, and on which is it slowing down?

Concavity: which way the graph bends

Two graphs can both be increasing and still look very different: one curves upward like the inside of a cup, the other curves downward like an upside-down cup. Concavity is the name for this bending. The professor defines it with tangent lines.

Concave up and concave down

The graph of \(f\) is concave upward on an interval \(I\) if the graph lies above all of its tangent lines on \(I\). The professor writes this as "concave up" or C.U.

The graph of \(f\) is concave downward on \(I\) if the graph lies below all of its tangent lines on \(I\). This is written "concave down" or C.D.

In plain words: concave up means the graph bends like a cup that could hold water. Concave down means it bends like an upside-down cup.

Now look at the slopes. On the concave up graph \(y = x^2\), the tangent lines get steeper as you move right: the slope is \(-4\) at \(x = -2\), \(0\) at \(x = 0\), and \(2\) at \(x = 1\). So the slope \(f'\) is increasing, which means its derivative \(f''\) is positive. On the concave down graph the slopes decrease as you move right, so \(f'' \lt 0\). Running this reasoning backward gives the test you will use every time.

Concavity test

If \(f''(x) \gt 0\) for all \(x\) in an interval \(I\), then the graph of \(f\) is concave up on \(I\).

If \(f''(x) \lt 0\) for all \(x\) in an interval \(I\), then the graph of \(f\) is concave down on \(I\).

In plain words: a positive second derivative means a cup shape, and a negative second derivative means a cap shape. To find the bending, you only need the sign of the second derivative.

A straight line is neither concave up nor concave down. Its second derivative is 0 everywhere, and it lies on its own tangent line, so it is neither above nor below it.

Two standard facts follow right away. For \(y = e^x\), \(y'' = e^x \gt 0\) for every \(x\), so the graph of \(e^x\) is concave up on all of \((-\infty, \infty)\). For \(y = \ln x\), \(y' = \tfrac{1}{x} = x^{-1}\) and \(y'' = -x^{-2} = -\tfrac{1}{x^2} \lt 0\) for every \(x\) in the domain \((0, \infty)\), so the graph of \(\ln x\) is concave down on \((0, \infty)\).

Inflection points

Inflection point

A point \(P = (c, f(c))\) on the graph of \(y = f(x)\) is an inflection point if \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) and the graph changes concavity at \(P\): from concave up to concave down, or from concave down to concave up.

At an inflection point, \(f''(c) = 0\) or \(f''(c)\) does not exist. If the graph has a tangent line there, the graph crosses that tangent line.

In plain words: an inflection point is a point on the graph where the bending switches, from cup to cap or from cap to cup. The second derivative being zero there is a clue, but the switch is what counts.

Three things in this definition trip people up.

  • An inflection point is a point, so the answer has two coordinates. Find \(c\), then compute \(f(c)\) from the original function \(f\).
  • The point must be on the graph. The graph of \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{x}\) is concave down for \(x \lt 0\) and concave up for \(x \gt 0\), yet it has no inflection point, because \(x = 0\) is not in the domain.
  • Solving \(f''(x) = 0\) only gives candidates. The concavity has to actually change. The standard counterexample is \(f(x) = x^4\): here \(f''(x) = 12x^2\), so \(f''(0) = 0\), but \(12x^2 \gt 0\) on both sides of 0. The graph is concave up on both sides, so \((0, 0)\) is not an inflection point.

A candidate can also come from \(f''\) being undefined. For \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\), \(f''(x) = -\tfrac{2}{9}x^{-5/3}\) is undefined at \(x = 0\), it is positive for \(x \lt 0\) and negative for \(x \gt 0\), and \(f(0) = 0\) is defined. So \((0, 0)\) is an inflection point even though \(f''(0)\) does not exist.

How to find the intervals of concavity and the inflection points
  1. Find the domain of \(f\).
  2. Compute \(f''(x)\) and factor it completely. Write negative powers as fractions so you can see where it is undefined.
  3. List every \(x\) where \(f''(x) = 0\) or \(f''(x)\) is undefined. Also list any \(x\) not in the domain, since the concavity can change across a gap.
  4. Mark these numbers on a number line. In each interval between them, pick a test value and find the sign of \(f''\) there.
  5. Where \(f'' \gt 0\) the graph is concave up, and where \(f'' \lt 0\) it is concave down. Write the answer as open intervals.
  6. Each listed \(c\) that is in the domain and where the sign of \(f''\) changes gives an inflection point. Compute \(f(c)\) from the original \(f\) and write the point \((c, f(c))\).

The second derivative test

The first derivative test (Section 5.2) decides whether a critical number \(c\) gives a local maximum or minimum by checking whether \(f'\) changes sign at \(c\). The second derivative test does the same job by looking at concavity. Here is the idea. If \(f'(c) = 0\), the tangent line at \(c\) is horizontal. If the graph is concave up near \(c\), it lies above that horizontal tangent line, so \(f(c)\) is lower than the nearby values: a local minimum. If the graph is concave down near \(c\), it lies below the horizontal tangent line, so \(f(c)\) is a local maximum.

Second derivative test

Suppose that \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''\) is continuous near \(c\) (on some open interval containing \(c\)).

  • If \(f''(c) \gt 0\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = c\).
  • If \(f''(c) \lt 0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = c\).
  • If \(f''(c) = 0\), this test is useless: try the first derivative test instead.

In plain words: at a flat spot on the graph, a cup shape means you are at the bottom of a valley (a minimum), and a cap shape means you are at the top of a hill (a maximum). If the second derivative is zero there, this test tells you nothing, and you go back to checking the sign of the first derivative.

A memory aid: concave up holds water like a cup, and the bottom of a cup is a minimum. So positive second derivative goes with minimum.

How to use the second derivative test
  1. Compute \(f'(x)\) and solve \(f'(x) = 0\) to get the critical numbers \(c\) where the tangent is horizontal.
  2. Compute \(f''(x)\).
  3. Substitute each critical number into \(f''\) and find the sign of \(f''(c)\).
  4. If \(f''(c) \gt 0\), write "local minimum at \(x = c\)". If \(f''(c) \lt 0\), write "local maximum at \(x = c\)".
  5. If \(f''(c) = 0\), say the test is inconclusive and use the first derivative test at that \(c\): check the sign of \(f'\) just to the left and just to the right of \(c\).
  6. Critical numbers where \(f'(c)\) does not exist are outside this test. Use the first derivative test for them too.
  7. Give the local extreme values by computing \(f(c)\) from the original function.
Worked example 5

Find the local extrema of \(f(x) = x^4 - 2x^3\). Use the second derivative test whenever it gives an answer.

How to spot this type: the words "use the second derivative test" or "classify the critical numbers". Solve \(f'(x) = 0\), then plug each solution into \(f''\) and read the sign.

All three functions \(x^4\), \(-x^4\), and \(x^3\) have \(f'(0) = 0\) and \(f''(0) = 0\), yet \(x^4\) has a local minimum at 0, \(-x^4\) has a local maximum at 0, and \(x^3\) has neither. When the test fails: "\(f''(0) = 0\), so the second derivative test is inconclusive. By the first derivative test, \(f'\) is negative on both sides of 0, so \(f\) has no local extremum at 0."

How many inflection points can a polynomial have

The lecture notes ask two questions of this kind. The first is about cubics. If \(f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d\) with \(a \ne 0\), then \(f''(x) = 6ax + 2b\), a line with nonzero slope. It has exactly one zero, \(x = -\tfrac{b}{3a}\), and a line with nonzero slope changes sign at its zero. So the graph of every cubic has exactly one inflection point. The second question, about degree 4, is less obvious than it looks.

Worked example 6

How many inflection points can the graph of a polynomial of degree 4 have? Give an example of each possibility.

How to spot this type: the question asks "how many inflection points can ... have" or "show that every ... has". Write a general polynomial with letters for the coefficients, take two derivatives, and count how many times \(f''\) can change sign.

  1. Write a general quartic and take two derivatives. Let \(f(x) = ax^4 + bx^3 + cx^2 + dx + e\) with \(a \ne 0\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 4ax^3 + 3bx^2 + 2cx + d \\ f''(x) &= 12ax^2 + 6bx + 2c. \end{aligned}\] So \(f''\) is a quadratic with leading coefficient \(12a \ne 0\). Inflection points can only happen where \(f''\) changes sign, and a polynomial changes sign only at its roots.
  2. Case 1: \(f''\) has two different real roots \(r_1 \lt r_2\). Then \(f''(x) = 12a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\). As \(x\) passes \(r_1\), the factor \(x - r_1\) changes sign and the factor \(x - r_2\) does not, so \(f''\) changes sign at \(r_1\). The same happens at \(r_2\). This gives exactly 2 inflection points.
  3. Case 2: \(f''\) has one repeated root \(r\) (a double root). Then \(f''(x) = 12a(x - r)^2\). The square \((x - r)^2\) is positive on both sides of \(r\), so \(f''\) has the sign of \(a\) on both sides. The concavity does not change, so there are 0 inflection points, even though \(f''(r) = 0\).
  4. Case 3: \(f''\) has no real roots. Then \(f''\) is never 0, and since it is continuous it can never switch sign. There are 0 inflection points.
  5. A quadratic has at most two real roots, so there can never be 3 or more. And the only way to get exactly one root is the double root of Case 2, which gives no sign change, so there can never be exactly 1.
  6. Example with two: \(f(x) = x^4 - 6x^2\). Here \(f''(x) = 12x^2 - 12 = 12(x-1)(x+1)\), which changes sign at \(x = \pm 1\), and \(f(\pm 1) = 1 - 6 = -5\). The inflection points are \((-1, -5)\) and \((1, -5)\).
  7. Example with none because \(f''\) has no roots: \(f(x) = x^4 + 3x^2\). Here \(f''(x) = 12x^2 + 6 \ge 6 \gt 0\) for every \(x\), so the graph is concave up everywhere.
  8. Example with none even though \(f'' = 0\) somewhere: \(f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3 + 6x^2\). Here \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= 12x^2 - 24x + 12 \\ &= 12(x^2 - 2x + 1) \\ &= 12(x-1)^2. \end{aligned}\] So \(f''(1) = 0\), but \(f'' \gt 0\) on both sides of 1. The graph is concave up everywhere, and \((1, f(1)) = (1, 3)\) is not an inflection point.

Answer: A degree 4 polynomial has either 0 or 2 inflection points, never 1 and never 3 or more, because \(f''\) is a quadratic and a double root of \(f''\) does not produce a sign change. Examples: \(x^4 - 6x^2\) has 2, while \(x^4 + 3x^2\) and \(x^4 - 4x^3 + 6x^2\) have 0.

Reading concavity from a graph of \(f\) or of \(f'\)

On a graph of \(f\) itself, look at the bending. A cup shape (like the bottom of a valley) is concave up, a cap shape (like the top of a hill) is concave down, and the inflection points are where the bending switches. Near a local minimum the graph is usually concave up, and near a local maximum it is usually concave down.

When you are given the graph of the derivative \(f'\) instead, you have to read it differently, because \(f''\) is the slope of that graph.

What you see on the graph of \(f'\)What it says about \(f\)
the graph of \(f'\) is above the \(x\)-axis\(f\) is increasing
the graph of \(f'\) is below the \(x\)-axis\(f\) is decreasing
\(f'\) crosses the \(x\)-axis from above to below\(f\) has a local maximum
\(f'\) crosses the \(x\)-axis from below to above\(f\) has a local minimum
the graph of \(f'\) is rising\(f'' \gt 0\), so \(f\) is concave up
the graph of \(f'\) is falling\(f'' \lt 0\), so \(f\) is concave down

For example, suppose the graph below is the graph of \(f'\), the derivative of some function \(f\). The graph of \(f'\) is above the axis for \(x \lt 1\) and \(x \gt 3\), and below it on \((1, 3)\). So \(f\) increases, then decreases, then increases. That gives a local maximum of \(f\) at \(x = 1\) and a local minimum at \(x = 3\). Next, the graph of \(f'\) falls until \(x = 2\) and rises after. So \(f\) is concave down on \((-\infty, 2)\) and concave up on \((2, \infty)\), with an inflection point at \(x = 2\). Notice that the inflection point of \(f\) is at the lowest point of the graph of \(f'\). It is not where \(f'\) crosses the axis.

Common mistake. Reading a graph of \(f'\) as if it were a graph of \(f\). Before you answer, check the label on the axis or in the caption. Zeros of \(f'\) give candidates for extrema of \(f\), and turning points of \(f'\) give inflection points of \(f\).

The point of diminishing returns

Suppose \(R(x)\) is the revenue earned when \(x\) dollars are spent on advertising (or on investment or production). Often the graph of \(R\) is concave up at first and concave down later. The inflection point \((c, R(c))\) where the concavity changes from up to down is called the point of diminishing returns.

In plain words: the point of diminishing returns is the moment when spending one more dollar stops paying off better than the dollar before. Revenue still goes up after it, just by less each time.

For \(x \lt c\) the graph is concave up, so \(R'\) is increasing: each additional dollar spent brings in more extra revenue than the dollar before. For \(x \gt c\) the graph is concave down, so \(R'\) is decreasing: revenue still grows, but each additional dollar brings in less than the one before. The slope \(R'\) is largest at \(x = c\).

Worked example 7

A company's revenue is \(R(x) = -x^3 + 30x^2 + 100x\) thousand dollars when it spends \(x\) thousand dollars on advertising, for \(0 \le x \le 20\). Find the point of diminishing returns.

How to spot this type: the words "point of diminishing returns", or "when is the revenue (or growth) increasing fastest". You want the inflection point where the graph changes from concave up to concave down.

  1. The point of diminishing returns is the inflection point where the graph changes from concave up to concave down, so compute two derivatives. \[\begin{aligned} R'(x) &= -3x^2 + 60x + 100 \\ R''(x) &= -6x + 60. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Solve \(R''(x) = 0\). \[ -6x + 60 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 10. \]
  3. Check that the concavity really changes from up to down at 10, using test values 5 and 15. \[\begin{aligned} R''(5) &= -30 + 60 = 30 \gt 0 \quad \text{(concave up on } (0, 10)) \\ R''(15) &= -90 + 60 = -30 \lt 0 \quad \text{(concave down on } (10, 20)). \end{aligned}\]
  4. Compute the revenue at \(x = 10\). \[\begin{aligned} R(10) &= -(10)^3 + 30(10)^2 + 100(10) \\ &= -1000 + 3000 + 1000 = 3000. \end{aligned}\]
  5. For interpretation, compute the marginal revenue there. \[ R'(10) = -3(10)^2 + 60(10) + 100 = -300 + 600 + 100 = 400. \] This is the largest value of \(R'\) on \([0, 20]\): the derivative of \(R'\) is \(R''\), which is positive before 10 and negative after, so \(R'\) rises up to \(x = 10\) and falls after it.

Answer: The point of diminishing returns is \((10, 3000)\). After \$10,000 is spent on advertising (revenue \$3,000,000), each additional advertising dollar brings in less extra revenue than the one before.

More common mistakes

Common mistake. Mixing up increasing and concave up. A function can be increasing and concave down (like \(\ln x\)), or decreasing and concave up (like \(e^{-x}\)). Increasing and decreasing come from the sign of \(f'\). Concavity comes from the sign of \(f''\).
Common mistake. Forgetting the places where \(f''\) is undefined. With fractional powers, such as \(f''(x) = \dfrac{10(x+1)}{9x^{4/3}}\), the denominator is 0 at \(x = 0\), and that number must go on the sign chart along with \(x = -1\).
Common mistake. Computing the \(y\)-coordinate of an inflection point by plugging \(c\) into \(f''\) or \(f'\). Plugging into \(f''\) always gives 0 and tells you nothing. The point is \((c, f(c))\), using the original function.
Common mistake. Saying an object speeds up because \(a \gt 0\). Speeding up means \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign. If \(v \lt 0\) and \(a \gt 0\), the object is slowing down.
Common mistake. Writing \(f^4(x)\) or \(f^{4}\) for the fourth derivative. Use \(f^{(4)}(x)\) with parentheses.
Common mistake. Reading "falling more slowly" as \(f'' \lt 0\). If a quantity is falling, \(f' \lt 0\). If it falls more slowly, the negative rate is moving up toward 0, so \(f'\) is increasing and \(f'' \gt 0\). Decide the sign of \(f'\) and the sign of \(f''\) as two separate questions.
If you get stuck
  • Factor \(f''\) completely before you make a sign chart. Pull out the common factor first (such as \(12x\), \(e^{-x}\), or the smallest power of \(x\)), then factor what is left.
  • If \(f''\) has negative or fractional exponents, write it as one fraction. The zeros of the top and the zeros of the bottom are your candidates.
  • Exponential factors such as \(e^{x}\) or \(e^{-x^2/2}\) are always positive. Ignore them when you find the sign; the other factor decides it.
  • For many derivatives in a row, leave each coefficient as a product (such as \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4\)) for a moment, so the factorial or power pattern shows.
  • If the second derivative test gives \(f''(c) = 0\), do not stop. Check the sign of \(f'\) at one number just left of \(c\) and one just right of \(c\).
  • For speeding up or slowing down, put the zeros of \(v\) and the zeros of \(a\) on one number line, and fill in a table with both signs on each interval.
Quick check
If \(f''(c) = 0\), must \((c, f(c))\) be an inflection point? Give an example.

No. For \(f(x) = x^4\), \(f''(x) = 12x^2\) is 0 at \(x = 0\) but positive on both sides, so the concavity does not change and \((0, 0)\) is not an inflection point.

Can a function be increasing and concave down at the same time? Give an example.

Yes. For \(f(x) = \ln x\) on \((0, \infty)\), \(f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{x} \gt 0\) and \(f''(x) = -\tfrac{1}{x^2} \lt 0\), so it rises while bending down.

A particle has \(v(2) = -4\) and \(a(2) = 3\). Is it speeding up or slowing down at \(t = 2\)?

Slowing down, because the velocity and the acceleration have opposite signs.

Suppose \(f'(5) = 0\) and \(f''(5) = -2\). What does \(f\) have at \(x = 5\)?

A local maximum, by the second derivative test: the graph has a horizontal tangent and bends down like a cap there.

What are the 9th and 10th derivatives of \(x^9 + 4x^3\)?

The 9th derivative is the constant \(9! = 362880\), because only the \(x^9\) term survives nine derivatives. The 10th derivative is 0, because it is the derivative of a constant.

What to have memorized
  • \(f''\) is the derivative of \(f'\). Notation: \(f''(x)\), \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\), \(f'''(x)\), \(f^{(4)}(x)\), \(f^{(n)}(x)\).
  • Each derivative of a degree \(m\) polynomial lowers the degree by one, so \(f^{(m)}\) is a constant and \(f^{(m+1)} = 0\).
  • Patterns: the \(n\)th derivative of \(e^{kx}\) is \(k^n e^{kx}\), and the \(n\)th derivative of \((x + b)^{-1}\) is \(\dfrac{(-1)^n n!}{(x+b)^{n+1}}\).
  • \(v(t) = s'(t)\), \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)\), speed \(= |v(t)|\). Same signs of \(v\) and \(a\) means speeding up, opposite signs means slowing down.
  • Concave up: graph above its tangent lines, \(f'\) increasing, \(f'' \gt 0\). Concave down: graph below its tangent lines, \(f'\) decreasing, \(f'' \lt 0\).
  • Inflection point: a point \((c, f(c))\) on the graph, with \(f\) continuous at \(c\), where the concavity changes. \(f''(c) = 0\) is not enough (\(x^4\) at 0).
  • Second derivative test: if \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''(c) \gt 0\), local min. If \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''(c) \lt 0\), local max. If \(f''(c) = 0\), use the first derivative test.
  • A cubic always has exactly one inflection point. A quartic has 0 or 2.
  • Point of diminishing returns: the inflection point of a revenue curve where the concavity changes from up to down. It is where \(R'\) is largest.
  • \(e^x\) is concave up on \((-\infty, \infty)\), and \(\ln x\) is concave down on \((0, \infty)\).

Problem 1
Let \(f(x) = 4x^3 - 5x^2 + 7x - 2\). Find \(f''(x)\), and decide whether the graph of \(f\) is concave up or concave down at \(x = 1\).
Show full solution
  1. Differentiate once with the power rule, term by term. \[ f'(x) = 12x^2 - 10x + 7. \]
  2. Differentiate again to get the second derivative. The constant 7 has derivative 0. \[ f''(x) = 24x - 10. \]
  3. Substitute \(x = 1\) and look at the sign. \[ f''(1) = 24(1) - 10 = 14 \gt 0. \] A positive second derivative means the graph is concave up there, by the concavity test.

Answer: \(f''(x) = 24x - 10\). Since \(f''(1) = 14 \gt 0\), the graph is concave up at \(x = 1\).

Problem 2
Let \(y = \sqrt{x}\). Find \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\), and evaluate it at \(x = 4\).
Show full solution
  1. Write the square root as a power so the power rule applies. \[ y = x^{1/2}. \]
  2. First derivative: bring down \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and subtract 1 from the exponent. \[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{1/2 - 1} = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}. \]
  3. Second derivative: bring down \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) and subtract 1 again. \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)x^{-1/2 - 1} \\ &= -\tfrac{1}{4}x^{-3/2} = -\frac{1}{4x^{3/2}}. \end{aligned}\]
  4. Substitute \(x = 4\). Since \(4^{3/2} = (\sqrt{4})^3 = 2^3 = 8\), \[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\bigg|_{x=4} = -\frac{1}{4 \cdot 8} = -\frac{1}{32}. \]

Answer: \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}\), and at \(x = 4\) its value is \(-\tfrac{1}{32}\).

Problem 3
Let \(f(x) = e^{-2x}\). Find \(f'(x)\), \(f''(x)\), \(f'''(x)\), and \(f^{(4)}(x)\), then write a formula for \(f^{(n)}(x)\).
Show full solution
  1. By the chain rule, the derivative of \(e^{-2x}\) is \(e^{-2x}\) times the derivative of the exponent, which is \(-2\). Each derivative multiplies by another \(-2\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= -2e^{-2x} \\ f''(x) &= (-2)(-2)e^{-2x} = 4e^{-2x} \\ f'''(x) &= 4(-2)e^{-2x} = -8e^{-2x} \\ f^{(4)}(x) &= -8(-2)e^{-2x} = 16e^{-2x}. \end{aligned}\]
  2. The coefficients are \(-2, 4, -8, 16\), which are \((-2)^1, (-2)^2, (-2)^3, (-2)^4\). After \(n\) derivatives there are \(n\) factors of \(-2\). \[ f^{(n)}(x) = (-2)^n e^{-2x}. \]

Answer: \(f' = -2e^{-2x}\), \(f'' = 4e^{-2x}\), \(f''' = -8e^{-2x}\), \(f^{(4)} = 16e^{-2x}\), and \(f^{(n)}(x) = (-2)^n e^{-2x}\).

Problem 4
The graph of a function \(f\) is shown below. Using only the picture, give the open intervals where \(f\) is concave up and concave down, and the inflection point.
Show full solution
  1. Look at the left part of the graph, up to the point \((1, 3)\). It bends like a cup, with its low point at \((0, 1)\), and the tangent lines lie below the curve. So the graph is concave up on \((-\infty, 1)\).
  2. Look at the right part, after \((1, 3)\). It bends like an upside-down cup, with its high point at \((2, 5)\), and the tangent lines lie above the curve. So the graph is concave down on \((1, \infty)\).
  3. The bending switches from up to down at \((1, 3)\), and that point is on the graph, so it is the inflection point. The points \((0, 1)\) and \((2, 5)\) are a local minimum and a local maximum, which are a different thing.
  4. Check with the formula behind the picture, \(f(x) = -x^3 + 3x^2 + 1\): \(f''(x) = -6x + 6\), which is positive for \(x \lt 1\) and negative for \(x \gt 1\), and \(f(1) = -1 + 3 + 1 = 3\).

Answer: Concave up on \((-\infty, 1)\), concave down on \((1, \infty)\), and the inflection point is \((1, 3)\).

Problem 5
Let \(f(x) = 2x^3 - 6x\). Find the critical numbers and use the second derivative test to classify each one.
Show full solution
  1. Find the critical numbers by solving \(f'(x) = 0\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 6x^2 - 6 = 6(x^2 - 1) \\ &= 6(x - 1)(x + 1) = 0. \end{aligned}\] So \(x = 1\) or \(x = -1\). Since \(f'\) is a polynomial, there are no other critical numbers.
  2. Compute the second derivative. \[ f''(x) = 12x. \]
  3. Test \(x = 1\): \(f''(1) = 12 \gt 0\), so by the second derivative test \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = 1\). Its value is \(f(1) = 2(1)^3 - 6(1) = 2 - 6 = -4\).
  4. Test \(x = -1\): \(f''(-1) = -12 \lt 0\), so by the second derivative test \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = -1\). Its value is \(f(-1) = 2(-1)^3 - 6(-1) = -2 + 6 = 4\).

Answer: Local maximum \(f(-1) = 4\) at \(x = -1\), and local minimum \(f(1) = -4\) at \(x = 1\).

Problem 6
An object moves along a line with position \(s(t) = 2t^3 - 9t^2 + 4\) feet after \(t\) seconds. Find its velocity and acceleration at \(t = 2\), and say whether it is speeding up or slowing down at that moment.
Show full solution
  1. Velocity is the derivative of position, and acceleration is the derivative of velocity. \[\begin{aligned} v(t) &= s'(t) = 6t^2 - 18t \\ a(t) &= v'(t) = 12t - 18. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Substitute \(t = 2\). \[\begin{aligned} v(2) &= 6(4) - 18(2) = 24 - 36 = -12 \ \text{ft/s} \\ a(2) &= 12(2) - 18 = 24 - 18 = 6 \ \text{ft/s}^2. \end{aligned}\]
  3. Compare signs. The velocity is negative and the acceleration is positive. Opposite signs mean the speed is decreasing, so the object is slowing down. (It is moving in the negative direction at 12 ft/s, and the positive acceleration is pulling the velocity up toward 0.)

Answer: \(v(2) = -12\) ft/s and \(a(2) = 6\) ft/s\(^2\). They have opposite signs, so the object is slowing down at \(t = 2\).

Problem 7
Find the open intervals where the graph of \(f(x) = x^4 - 6x^2 + 4x\) is concave up and concave down, and find all inflection points.
Show full solution

Problem 9
Let \(f(x) = xe^{-x}\). Find \(f''(x)\), the open intervals where the graph is concave up and concave down, and the inflection point.
Show full solution
  1. First derivative by the product rule. Let \(u = x\) and \(v = e^{-x}\). Then \(u' = 1\), and by the chain rule \(v' = -e^{-x}\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 1 \cdot e^{-x} + x(-e^{-x}) \\ &= (1 - x)e^{-x}. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Second derivative by the product rule again, with \(u = 1 - x\), \(u' = -1\), \(v = e^{-x}\), \(v' = -e^{-x}\). \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= (-1)e^{-x} + (1 - x)(-e^{-x}) \\ &= (-1 - 1 + x)e^{-x} \\ &= (x - 2)e^{-x}. \end{aligned}\]
  3. The factor \(e^{-x}\) is positive for every \(x\), so the sign of \(f''\) is the sign of \(x - 2\). That is negative for \(x \lt 2\) and positive for \(x \gt 2\). For example, \(f''(0) = -2 \lt 0\) and \(f''(3) = e^{-3} \gt 0\).
  4. The concavity changes from down to up at \(x = 2\), so there is an inflection point there. Its \(y\)-coordinate is \[ f(2) = 2e^{-2} = \frac{2}{e^2}. \]

Answer: \(f''(x) = (x - 2)e^{-x}\). Concave down on \((-\infty, 2)\), concave up on \((2, \infty)\). Inflection point \(\left(2, \tfrac{2}{e^2}\right)\).

Problem 11

(c) Concave down on \((-\infty, 3)\), concave up on \((3, \infty)\), and no inflection point because \(x = 3\) is not in the domain.

Problem 13
Let \(f(x) = x^{5/3} - 5x^{2/3}\). (a) Find the open intervals where the graph is concave up and concave down, and find all inflection points. (b) Find and classify the local extrema, using the second derivative test where it applies.
Hint
Write \(f'\) and \(f''\) with the smallest power of \(x\) factored out. The number \(x = 0\) is in the domain, so put it on the sign chart even though \(f''(0)\) does not exist.
Show full solution
  1. The powers \(x^{5/3}\) and \(x^{2/3}\) are cube roots raised to a power, so they are defined for every real \(x\). The domain is all real numbers. Differentiate with the power rule. \[ f'(x) = \tfrac{5}{3}x^{2/3} - \tfrac{10}{3}x^{-1/3}. \]
  2. Differentiate again. For the first term, \(\tfrac{5}{3} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{10}{9}\) and the exponent becomes \(\tfrac{2}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{1}{3}\). For the second term, \(-\tfrac{10}{3} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{3}\right) = \tfrac{10}{9}\) and the exponent becomes \(-\tfrac{1}{3} - 1 = -\tfrac{4}{3}\). \[ f''(x) = \tfrac{10}{9}x^{-1/3} + \tfrac{10}{9}x^{-4/3}. \]
  3. Factor out the smallest power, \(\tfrac{10}{9}x^{-4/3}\). Since \(x^{-1/3} = x^{-4/3} \cdot x\), what is left is \(x + 1\). \[ f''(x) = \tfrac{10}{9}x^{-4/3}(x + 1) = \frac{10(x + 1)}{9x^{4/3}}. \]
  4. Find the candidates. The numerator is 0 at \(x = -1\). The denominator is 0 at \(x = 0\), so \(f''(0)\) is undefined, while \(f(0) = 0\) is defined. Also \(x^{4/3} = \left(x^{1/3}\right)^4 \gt 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\), so the denominator never affects the sign.
  5. Sign chart for \(f''\). The test values \(-8\), \(-\tfrac{1}{8}\), and \(1\) are chosen because their cube roots are easy: \((-8)^{4/3} = (-2)^4 = 16\) and \(\left(-\tfrac{1}{8}\right)^{4/3} = \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^4 = \tfrac{1}{16}\).
    IntervalTest \(x\)\(f''(x) = \dfrac{10(x+1)}{9x^{4/3}}\)Concavity
    \((-\infty, -1)\)\(-8\)\(\dfrac{10(-7)}{9 \cdot 16} = -\dfrac{35}{72} \lt 0\)concave down
    \((-1, 0)\)\(-\tfrac{1}{8}\)\(\dfrac{10 \cdot \tfrac{7}{8}}{9 \cdot \tfrac{1}{16}} = \dfrac{35}{4} \cdot \dfrac{16}{9} = \dfrac{140}{9} \gt 0\)concave up

  7. For (b), factor \(f'\) to find the critical numbers. Pull out \(\tfrac{5}{3}x^{-1/3}\), using \(x^{2/3} = x^{-1/3} \cdot x\). \[ f'(x) = \tfrac{5}{3}x^{-1/3}(x - 2) = \frac{5(x - 2)}{3x^{1/3}}. \] So \(f'(2) = 0\), and \(f'(0)\) is undefined. Both \(x = 0\) and \(x = 2\) are critical numbers.
  8. Second derivative test at \(x = 2\). The numerator of \(f''(2)\) is \(10 \cdot 3 = 30 \gt 0\) and the denominator \(9 \cdot 2^{4/3}\) is positive, so \(f''(2) \gt 0\) and \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = 2\).   9. At \(x = 0\) the second derivative test does not apply, because \(f'(0)\) does not exist (the test requires \(f'(c) = 0\)). Use the first derivative test with test values \(-1\) and \(1\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(-1) &= \frac{5(-3)}{3(-1)} = 5 \gt 0 \\ f'(1) &= \frac{5(-1)}{3(1)} = -\tfrac{5}{3} \lt 0. \end{aligned}\] Since \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at 0, \(f\) has a local maximum \(f(0) = 0\) there.

(b) Local maximum \(f(0) = 0\) at \(x = 0\) (by the first derivative test) and local minimum \(f(2) = -3\sqrt[3]{4}\) at \(x = 2\) (by the second derivative test).

Where students lose points: Leaving \(x = 0\) off the sign chart because \(f''(0)\) does not exist, or calling \((0, 0)\) an inflection point without checking for a sign change (there is none). Also, using the second derivative test at \(x = 0\), where \(f'(0)\) does not exist.

Problem 15
A small business finds that when it spends \(x\) thousand dollars on online ads, its revenue is \(R(x) = -\tfrac{1}{3}x^3 + 6x^2 + 20x\) thousand dollars, for \(0 \le x \le 12\). (a) Find the point of diminishing returns. (b) Find the marginal revenue \(R'(x)\) at that point, and show that it is the largest value of \(R'\) on \([0, 12]\). (c) Explain in a sentence what the point means for the business.
Hint
The point of diminishing returns is where \(R''\) changes from positive to negative. For part (b), treat \(R'\) as the function to maximize on the closed interval \([0, 12]\).
Show full solution
  1. Define the quantities. \(x\) is the ad spending in thousands of dollars, and \(R(x)\) is the revenue in thousands of dollars. The point of diminishing returns is the inflection point where the graph of \(R\) changes from concave up to concave down. Compute the derivatives. \[\begin{aligned} R'(x) &= -x^2 + 12x + 20 \\ R''(x) &= -2x + 12. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Solve \(R''(x) = 0\). \[ -2x + 12 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 6. \]
  3. Check the sign change with test values 0 and 12. \[\begin{aligned} R''(0) &= 12 \gt 0 \quad \text{(concave up on } (0, 6)) \\ R''(12) &= -24 + 12 = -12 \lt 0 \quad \text{(concave down on } (6, 12)). \end{aligned}\] So the concavity changes from up to down at \(x = 6\).
  4. Compute the revenue at \(x = 6\). \[\begin{aligned} R(6) &= -\tfrac{1}{3}(216) + 6(36) + 20(6) \\ &= -72 + 216 + 120 = 264. \end{aligned}\] The point of diminishing returns is \((6, 264)\).
  5. For (b), the marginal revenue at \(x = 6\) is \[ R'(6) = -(6)^2 + 12(6) + 20 = -36 + 72 + 20 = 56. \]
  6. To show this is the largest value of \(R'\), treat \(R'\) as the function to maximize. Its derivative is \(R''\), which is 0 only at \(x = 6\). Apply the second derivative test to \(R'\): the derivative of \(R''\) is \(R'''(x) = -2 \lt 0\), so \(R'\) has a local maximum at 6. Compare with the endpoints, as in the closed interval method: \[ R'(0) = 20, \qquad R'(6) = 56, \qquad R'(12) = -144 + 144 + 20 = 20. \] The largest of these is 56, so \(R'\) is largest at \(x = 6\).
  7. For (c), interpret. Up to \$6,000 of ad spending, each extra dollar brings in more extra revenue than the dollar before. After \$6,000, revenue keeps rising, since \(R'(x) \gt 0\) on \([0, 12]\), but each extra dollar brings in less than the one before.

Answer: (a) The point of diminishing returns is \((6, 264)\): \$6,000 of ads gives \$264,000 of revenue. (b) \(R'(6) = 56\), meaning about \$56 of extra revenue per extra dollar of ads, and this is the largest marginal revenue on \([0, 12]\). (c) Beyond \$6,000 of ad spending, each additional dollar still raises revenue but by less and less.

Where students lose points: Giving only \(x = 6\) instead of the point \((6, 264)\), or forgetting that both numbers are in thousands of dollars. In (b), stopping at \(R''(6) = 0\) without showing that \(R'\) is actually largest there.

Problem 18
Draw a rough sketch of the graph of \(f(x) = 2x^3 - 3x^2 - 12x + 4\). Use the second derivative test to classify the local extrema, and label all local extrema and inflection points.
Hint
Factor \(f'\) to get the critical numbers, then plug each one into \(f''(x) = 12x - 6\). A cubic has its inflection point where \(f'' = 0\).
Show full solution
  1. The domain is all real numbers. Compute the first derivative and factor it. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 6x^2 - 6x - 12 \\ &= 6(x^2 - x - 2) \\ &= 6(x - 2)(x + 1). \end{aligned}\] Setting \(f'(x) = 0\) gives the critical numbers \(x = -1\) and \(x = 2\).
  2. Compute the second derivative. \[ f''(x) = 12x - 6. \]
  3. Second derivative test at \(x = -1\): \(f''(-1) = -12 - 6 = -18 \lt 0\), so \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = -1\). Its value is \[ f(-1) = 2(-1)^3 - 3(-1)^2 - 12(-1) + 4 = -2 - 3 + 12 + 4 = 11. \]
  4. Second derivative test at \(x = 2\): \(f''(2) = 24 - 6 = 18 \gt 0\), so \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = 2\). Its value is \[ f(2) = 2(2)^3 - 3(2)^2 - 12(2) + 4 = 16 - 12 - 24 + 4 = -16. \]
  5. Increasing and decreasing, for the sketch. Test the sign of \(f'(x) = 6(x-2)(x+1)\): \(f'(-2) = 6(-4)(-1) = 24 \gt 0\), \(f'(0) = 6(-2)(1) = -12 \lt 0\), \(f'(3) = 6(1)(4) = 24 \gt 0\). So \(f\) increases on \((-\infty, -1)\), decreases on \((-1, 2)\), and increases on \((2, \infty)\).
  6. Concavity. \(f''(x) = 12x - 6 = 0\) gives \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}\). For \(x \lt \tfrac{1}{2}\), say \(x = 0\), \(f''(0) = -6 \lt 0\), so the graph is concave down on \((-\infty, \tfrac{1}{2})\). For \(x \gt \tfrac{1}{2}\), say \(x = 1\), \(f''(1) = 6 \gt 0\), so it is concave up on \((\tfrac{1}{2}, \infty)\).
  7. The concavity changes at \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}\), so there is an inflection point there. \[\begin{aligned} f\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) &= 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} - 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} - 12 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 4 \\ &= \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{3}{4} - 6 + 4 = -\tfrac{5}{2}. \end{aligned}\] As a check, \(-\tfrac{5}{2}\) is the average of the local max value 11 and the local min value \(-16\), which always happens for a cubic.
  8. Other features for the sketch: the \(y\)-intercept is \(f(0) = 4\). The leading term \(2x^3\) controls the ends, so \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to \infty\) and \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\). Plot the three labeled points and join them with the shapes found above.

Answer: Local maximum \((-1, 11)\) and local minimum \((2, -16)\), both by the second derivative test. Inflection point \(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{5}{2}\right)\). The graph rises to \((-1, 11)\), falls to \((2, -16)\), then rises, and is concave down on \((-\infty, \tfrac{1}{2})\) and concave up on \((\tfrac{1}{2}, \infty)\).

Where students lose points: Sign mistakes when computing \(f(-1)\) (put every substituted number in parentheses), and giving the inflection point as \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}\) instead of the point \(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{5}{2}\right)\). A sketch with the right shape but no labeled points also loses points.

Problem 19
Let \(f(x) = xe^{-x^2/2}\). (a) Find \(f'(x)\) and \(f''(x)\). (b) Find the local extrema and classify them with the second derivative test. (c) Find the intervals of concavity and the coordinates of all inflection points. Show full solution
  1. For (a), use the product rule. Let \(u = x\) and \(v = e^{-x^2/2}\).   3. For (b), since \(e^{-x^2/2} \gt 0\), \(f'(x) = 0\) exactly when \(1 - x^2 = 0\), so the critical numbers are \(x = 1\) and \(x = -1\).

Problem 20
(a) Let \(f(x) = x^4 + 8x^3 + 24x^2 - 5x\). Show that \(f''(-2) = 0\), but the graph of \(f\) has no inflection points. (b) Let \(g(x) = x^4 + cx^2\), where \(c\) is a constant. For which values of \(c\) does the graph of \(g\) have exactly two inflection points, and for which values does it have none? (c) Find the inflection points of \(g\) when \(c = -24\).
Hint
Factor \(f''\) completely and notice the perfect square, which never changes sign. For \(g\), solve \(g''(x) = 0\) and split into cases by the sign of \(c\).
Show full solution
  1. For (a), differentiate twice with the power rule. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 4x^3 + 24x^2 + 48x - 5 \\ f''(x) &= 12x^2 + 48x + 48. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Factor \(f''\). Pull out 12, then recognize a perfect square. \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= 12(x^2 + 4x + 4) \\ &= 12(x + 2)^2. \end{aligned}\] So \(f''(-2) = 12(0)^2 = 0\).
  3. Check the sign on each side of \(-2\). The square \((x + 2)^2\) is positive for every \(x \ne -2\), so \(f''(x) \gt 0\) on \((-\infty, -2)\) and on \((-2, \infty)\). For example \(f''(-3) = 12\) and \(f''(0) = 48\). The graph is concave up on both sides, so the concavity never changes. Since \(x = -2\) was the only candidate, the graph has no inflection points.
  4. For (b), differentiate \(g\) twice. \[\begin{aligned} g'(x) &= 4x^3 + 2cx \\ g''(x) &= 12x^2 + 2c. \end{aligned}\]
  5. Case \(c \lt 0\). Solve \(12x^2 + 2c = 0\): \(x^2 = -\tfrac{c}{6}\), and \(-\tfrac{c}{6} \gt 0\), so there are two roots \(x = \pm\sqrt{-c/6}\). Then \(g''(x) = 12\left(x - \sqrt{-c/6}\right)\left(x + \sqrt{-c/6}\right)\) changes sign at each root (one factor changes sign, the other does not). That gives exactly two inflection points.
  6. Case \(c = 0\). Then \(g''(x) = 12x^2\), which is 0 at \(x = 0\) but positive on both sides. There is no sign change, so there are no inflection points. This is the \(x^4\) example.
  7. Case \(c \gt 0\). Then \(g''(x) = 12x^2 + 2c \ge 2c \gt 0\) for every \(x\), so the graph is concave up everywhere and there are no inflection points.
  8. For (c), set \(c = -24\). Then \[\begin{aligned} g''(x) &= 12x^2 - 48 = 12(x^2 - 4) \\ &= 12(x - 2)(x + 2), \end{aligned}\] which changes sign at \(x = \pm 2\) (for example \(g''(-3) = 60\), \(g''(0) = -48\), \(g''(3) = 60\)). The \(y\)-coordinates are \[ g(\pm 2) = (\pm 2)^4 - 24(\pm 2)^2 = 16 - 24(4) = 16 - 96 = -80. \]

Answer: (a) \(f''(x) = 12(x + 2)^2\), so \(f''(-2) = 0\), but \(f'' \gt 0\) on both sides of \(-2\), so the graph is concave up everywhere and has no inflection points. (b) Exactly two inflection points when \(c \lt 0\), and none when \(c \ge 0\). (c) For \(c = -24\) the inflection points are \((-2, -80)\) and \((2, -80)\).

Where students lose points: Writing "\(f''(-2) = 0\), so there is an inflection point" without checking the sign on both sides. In (b), forgetting the case \(c = 0\), where \(g''(0) = 0\) but the sign does not change.

Problem 22
(a) Let \(f(x) = \ln(3x)\) for \(x \gt 0\). Find \(f'(x)\), \(f''(x)\), \(f'''(x)\), and \(f^{(4)}(x)\), and give a formula for \(f^{(n)}(x)\). (b) Let \(p(x) = 2x^5 - x^3 + 7x\). Find \(p^{(5)}(x)\) and \(p^{(6)}(x)\).
Hint
Simplify \(\ln(3x)\) with a log rule before you differentiate. For the polynomial, ask which term can survive five derivatives.
Show full solution
  1. For (a), split the logarithm with the rule \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). The number \(\ln 3\) is a constant. \[ f(x) = \ln 3 + \ln x. \]
  2. Differentiate once. The constant \(\ln 3\) has derivative 0, and the derivative of \(\ln x\) is \(\tfrac{1}{x}\). Write the answer as a power so the power rule applies next. \[ f'(x) = \frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}. \] (The chain rule on \(\ln(3x)\) gives the same thing: \(\tfrac{1}{3x} \cdot 3 = \tfrac{1}{x}\).)
  3. Differentiate three more times with the power rule. Each time, bring down the exponent and lower it by one. \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= -1 \cdot x^{-2} = -x^{-2} \\ f'''(x) &= -1 \cdot (-2)x^{-3} = 2x^{-3} \\ f^{(4)}(x) &= 2 \cdot (-3)x^{-4} = -6x^{-4}. \end{aligned}\]
  4. Read the pattern. The coefficients of \(f', f'', f''', f^{(4)}\) are \(1, -1, 2, -6\). Without signs these are \(0!, 1!, 2!, 3!\), so the \(n\)th coefficient has size \((n-1)!\). The signs alternate and start positive, which is \((-1)^{n-1}\). The exponent after \(n\) derivatives is \(-n\). \[ f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\,x^{-n} = \frac{(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!}{x^n}. \] Check with \(n = 4\): \(\dfrac{(-1)^3 \cdot 3!}{x^4} = -\dfrac{6}{x^4}\), which matches.
  5. For (b), differentiate term by term five times. \[\begin{aligned} p'(x) &= 10x^4 - 3x^2 + 7 \\ p''(x) &= 40x^3 - 6x \\ p'''(x) &= 120x^2 - 6 \\ p^{(4)}(x) &= 240x \\ p^{(5)}(x) &= 240. \end{aligned}\] Only the leading term survives: \(2x^5\) becomes \(2 \cdot 5! = 2 \cdot 120 = 240\).
  6. The fifth derivative is a constant, so the sixth derivative is 0. \[ p^{(6)}(x) = 0. \]

Answer: (a) \(f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{x}\), \(f''(x) = -\tfrac{1}{x^2}\), \(f'''(x) = \tfrac{2}{x^3}\), \(f^{(4)}(x) = -\tfrac{6}{x^4}\), and \(f^{(n)}(x) = \dfrac{(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!}{x^n}\). (b) \(p^{(5)}(x) = 240\) and \(p^{(6)}(x) = 0\).

Where students lose points: Using the chain rule and forgetting to cancel the 3, so that \(f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{3x}\). In the pattern, writing \(n!\) instead of \((n-1)!\); always test your formula on \(n = 1\) and \(n = 4\).

Ask two separate questions for each statement. Is the quantity going up or down? That is the sign of the first derivative. Is its rate of change getting bigger or smaller? That is the sign of the second derivative.
Show full solution
  1. For (a), "kept rising" means \(N\) is increasing, so \(N'(t) \gt 0\). "Rose more slowly" means the rate \(N'(t)\) is getting smaller. A decreasing \(N'\) has negative derivative, so \(N''(t) \lt 0\). The graph rises and is concave down.
  2. For (b), "going down" means \(V'(t) \lt 0\). "Loses value more slowly" means the rate is negative but closer to 0 as time goes on, for example \(-3000\) dollars per year one year and \(-2000\) the next. Going from \(-3000\) to \(-2000\) is an increase, so \(V'\) is increasing and \(V''(t) \gt 0\). The graph falls and is concave up.
  3. For (c), "growing" means \(D'(t) \gt 0\). "Faster and faster" means the rate \(D'(t)\) is getting bigger, so \(D''(t) \gt 0\). The graph rises and is concave up.
  4. For (d), "falling" means \(h'(t) \lt 0\). "Falls faster every minute" means the rate is negative and getting farther from 0, for example \(-1\) cm per minute, then \(-2\), then \(-3\). Those numbers are decreasing, so \(h'\) is decreasing and \(h''(t) \lt 0\). The graph falls and is concave down.

Answer: (a) \(N' \gt 0\), \(N'' \lt 0\): rising and concave down. (b) \(V' \lt 0\), \(V'' \gt 0\): falling and concave up. (c) \(D' \gt 0\), \(D'' \gt 0\): rising and concave up. (d) \(h' \lt 0\), \(h'' \lt 0\): falling and concave down.

Where students lose points: In (b) and (d), treating "more slowly" as automatically meaning \(f'' \lt 0\). For a falling quantity, "more slowly" means the negative rate moves up toward 0, which is \(f'' \gt 0\).

Problem 24
Decide whether each statement is true or false. If it is true, say which rule makes it true. If it is false, give a specific counterexample. (a) If \(f\) is concave up on an interval, then \(f\) is increasing on that interval. (b) If \((c, f(c))\) is an inflection point of \(f\), then \(f''(c) = 0\). (c) If \(f\) is a polynomial with \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''(c) \lt 0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\). (d) If \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''(c) = 0\), then \(f\) has no local extremum at \(c\). (e) A function that is increasing and concave down on all of \((-\infty, \infty)\) must eventually start to decrease. (f) Velocity can be positive or negative, but speed is always positive.
Hint
One specific counterexample is enough to show a statement is false. The usual suspects are \(x^2\), \(x^4\), \(x^{1/3}\), and \(-e^{-x}\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) False. Take \(f(x) = x^2\) on the interval \((-\infty, 0)\). Then \(f''(x) = 2 \gt 0\), so it is concave up there, but \(f'(x) = 2x \lt 0\) for \(x \lt 0\), so \(f\) is decreasing on that interval. Concavity comes from \(f''\), and increasing comes from \(f'\).
  2. (b) False. Take \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\). Then \(f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}\) and \[ f''(x) = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{2}{3}\right)x^{-5/3} = -\frac{2}{9x^{5/3}}. \] For \(x \lt 0\), \(x^{5/3} \lt 0\), so \(f''(x) \gt 0\). For \(x \gt 0\), \(f''(x) \lt 0\). The concavity changes at 0 and \(f(0) = 0\) is defined, so \((0, 0)\) is an inflection point, yet \(f''(0)\) does not exist. The correct statement is that \(f''(c)\) is 0 or does not exist.
  3. (c) True. This is the second derivative test. A polynomial has a continuous second derivative everywhere, so the hypothesis of the test holds, and \(f'(c) = 0\) with \(f''(c) \lt 0\) gives a local maximum.
  4. (d) False. Take \(f(x) = x^4\) and \(c = 0\). Then \(f'(x) = 4x^3\) and \(f''(x) = 12x^2\), so \(f'(0) = 0\) and \(f''(0) = 0\). But \(f(x) = x^4 \ge 0 = f(0)\) for every \(x\), so \(f\) has a local minimum at 0. When \(f''(c) = 0\), the second derivative test simply gives no information.
  5. (e) False. Take \(f(x) = -e^{-x}\). By the chain rule \(f'(x) = -e^{-x} \cdot (-1) = e^{-x} \gt 0\), so \(f\) is increasing everywhere. Then \(f''(x) = -e^{-x} \lt 0\), so \(f\) is concave down everywhere. The slope \(e^{-x}\) shrinks toward 0 but never reaches it, so \(f\) never starts to decrease. Its graph rises toward the horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\).
  6. (f) False. Speed is \(|v(t)|\), which is never negative, but it equals 0 whenever the object is at rest. For example, if \(s(t) = t^2\), then \(v(t) = 2t\) and the speed at \(t = 0\) is \(|0| = 0\). The correct statement is that speed is always greater than or equal to 0.

Answer: (a) False, \(x^2\) on \((-\infty, 0)\). (b) False, \(x^{1/3}\) at 0. (c) True, by the second derivative test. (d) False, \(x^4\) at 0. (e) False, \(-e^{-x}\). (f) False, the speed is 0 when the object is at rest.

Where students lose points: Answering "false" without a specific function, or giving a counterexample without showing the derivatives that prove it works.

Problem 25
A ball is thrown straight up from the edge of a roof. Its height above the ground after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t) = -16t^2 + 96t + 112\) feet. (a) Find the velocity and the acceleration. (b) Find the maximum height of the ball, and justify that it is a maximum. (c) When does the ball hit the ground, and what are its velocity and its speed at that moment? (d) On which time intervals is the ball speeding up, and on which is it slowing down?
Hint
The ball is highest when \(v(t) = 0\). It hits the ground when \(s(t) = 0\); divide the equation by \(-16\) before factoring.
Show full solution
  1. Define the quantities. \(t\) is the time in seconds after the throw, \(s(t)\) is the height in feet, \(v(t)\) is the velocity in feet per second (positive means upward), and \(a(t)\) is the acceleration in feet per second squared.
  2. For (a), differentiate the position once and then again. \[\begin{aligned} v(t) &= s'(t) = -32t + 96 \ \text{ft/s} \\ a(t) &= v'(t) = -32 \ \text{ft/s}^2. \end{aligned}\]
  3. For (b), the ball stops rising when the velocity is 0. \[ -32t + 96 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 32t = 96 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = 3. \]
  4. Justify the maximum with the second derivative test. The number \(t = 3\) is the only critical number of \(s\), and \(s''(3) = a(3) = -32 \lt 0\), so \(s\) has a local maximum at \(t = 3\). Since \(s\) is a parabola that opens downward, this is the highest point overall. \[\begin{aligned} s(3) &= -16(3)^2 + 96(3) + 112 \\ &= -144 + 288 + 112 = 256. \end{aligned}\]
  5. For (c), the ball hits the ground when its height is 0. Divide by \(-16\), then factor. \[\begin{aligned} -16t^2 + 96t + 112 &= 0 \\ t^2 - 6t - 7 &= 0 \\ (t - 7)(t + 1) &= 0. \end{aligned}\] So \(t = 7\) or \(t = -1\). Time after the throw cannot be negative, so \(t = 7\) seconds.
  6. Find the velocity at \(t = 7\), and take its absolute value for the speed. \[ v(7) = -32(7) + 96 = -224 + 96 = -128 \ \text{ft/s}, \qquad |v(7)| = 128 \ \text{ft/s}. \] The negative sign means the ball is moving down.
  7. For (d), compare the signs of \(v\) and \(a\) on \([0, 7]\). The acceleration is always \(-32 \lt 0\). On \((0, 3)\), \(v(t) = -32t + 96 \gt 0\) (for example \(v(1) = 64\)), so the signs are opposite and the ball slows down. On \((3, 7)\), \(v(t) \lt 0\) (for example \(v(5) = -64\)), so the signs are the same and the ball speeds up.

Answer: (a) \(v(t) = -32t + 96\) ft/s and \(a(t) = -32\) ft/s\(^2\). (b) The maximum height is 256 feet, at \(t = 3\) seconds, by the second derivative test since \(s''(3) = -32 \lt 0\). (c) The ball hits the ground at \(t = 7\) seconds with velocity \(-128\) ft/s, so its speed is 128 ft/s. (d) Slowing down on \((0, 3)\) and speeding up on \((3, 7)\).

Where students lose points: Giving the time \(t = 3\) when the question asks for the maximum height, and reporting the speed at impact as \(-128\) ft/s. Speed is the absolute value, so it is 128 ft/s.

Problem 26
A car drives along a straight road, and its velocity is positive for \(0 \le t \le 6\). The graph below shows its acceleration \(a(t)\) in m/s\(^2\), where \(t\) is in seconds. (a) On which intervals is the velocity increasing, and on which is it decreasing? (b) At what time in \([0, 6]\) is the car moving fastest? Explain. (c) At what time is the velocity increasing most rapidly? (d) On which intervals is the graph of the velocity \(v(t)\) concave up, and on which is it concave down?
Hint
The graph is \(a(t) = v'(t)\). Its sign tells you whether \(v\) goes up or down, and whether the graph of \(a\) rises or falls tells you the concavity of \(v\).
Show full solution
  1. For (a), use the fact that \(a(t) = v'(t)\). The graph is above the axis on \((0, 4)\), so \(v' \gt 0\) there and the velocity is increasing. The graph is below the axis on \((4, 6)\), so \(v' \lt 0\) there and the velocity is decreasing.
  2. For (b), the car moves fastest when its speed is largest. Since \(v \gt 0\), the speed is \(v\) itself. By the first derivative test applied to \(v\), the derivative \(v' = a\) changes from positive to negative at \(t = 4\), so \(v\) has a local maximum there. Because \(v\) increases on the whole interval \([0, 4]\) and decreases on the whole interval \([4, 6]\), the value \(v(4)\) is larger than every other value on \([0, 6]\).
  3. For (c), "the velocity is increasing most rapidly" means \(v'\) is as large as possible. Since \(v' = a\), look for the highest point of the graph of \(a\). That is the point \((2, 4)\), so the velocity increases fastest at \(t = 2\), at a rate of 4 m/s\(^2\).
  4. For (d), the second derivative of \(v\) is \(v'' = a'\), the slope of the graph shown. The graph of \(a\) rises on \((0, 2)\), so \(v'' \gt 0\) and \(v\) is concave up there. It falls on \((2, 6)\), so \(v'' \lt 0\) and \(v\) is concave down there. The graph of \(v\) has an inflection point at \(t = 2\).
  5. Check with the formula behind the picture, \(a(t) = 4t - t^2 = t(4 - t)\). It is positive for \(0 \lt t \lt 4\) and negative for \(4 \lt t \le 6\). Its derivative \(a'(t) = 4 - 2t\) is 0 at \(t = 2\), positive before, and negative after, which agrees with (c) and (d).

Answer: (a) The velocity increases on \((0, 4)\) and decreases on \((4, 6)\). (b) The car is moving fastest at \(t = 4\) seconds, where the acceleration changes from positive to negative. (c) The velocity increases most rapidly at \(t = 2\) seconds. (d) The graph of \(v\) is concave up on \((0, 2)\) and concave down on \((2, 6)\).

Where students lose points: Answering \(t = 2\) for "fastest" because the graph peaks there. The peak of the acceleration graph is where the speed grows fastest, not where the speed is largest.

Problem 27
Let \(f(x) = e^x - \dfrac{x^3}{6} + \dfrac{x^2}{2} + 5x\). (a) Find \(f''(x)\). (b) Find the smallest value of \(f''(x)\). (c) Use (b) to decide whether the graph of \(f\) has any inflection points, and describe its concavity.
Hint
You cannot solve \(e^x - x + 1 = 0\) by algebra. Instead, treat \(g(x) = f''(x)\) as a new function and find its smallest value with \(g'(x) = 0\).
Show full solution
  1. For (a), differentiate term by term. The derivative of \(e^x\) is \(e^x\), and the power rule handles the rest. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= e^x - \frac{3x^2}{6} + \frac{2x}{2} + 5 = e^x - \frac{x^2}{2} + x + 5 \\ f''(x) &= e^x - \frac{2x}{2} + 1 = e^x - x + 1. \end{aligned}\]
  2. For (b), call the second derivative \(g(x) = e^x - x + 1\) and find its critical numbers. \[ g'(x) = e^x - 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^x = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 0. \]
  3. Decide what kind of point \(x = 0\) is for \(g\). For \(x \lt 0\), \(e^x \lt 1\), so \(g'(x) \lt 0\) and \(g\) is decreasing. For \(x \gt 0\), \(e^x \gt 1\), so \(g'(x) \gt 0\) and \(g\) is increasing. So \(g\) falls all the way to \(x = 0\) and rises all the way after it, which makes \(g(0)\) the smallest value of \(g\) on all of \((-\infty, \infty)\). (The second derivative test agrees: \(g''(x) = e^x\), and \(g''(0) = 1 \gt 0\).)
  4. Compute the smallest value. \[ g(0) = e^0 - 0 + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2. \]
  5. For (c), part (b) says \(f''(x) \ge 2\) for every real \(x\). In particular \(f''(x) \gt 0\) everywhere, so by the concavity test the graph of \(f\) is concave up on \((-\infty, \infty)\). The second derivative is never 0, never undefined, and never changes sign, so there is no inflection point.

Answer: (a) \(f''(x) = e^x - x + 1\). (b) The smallest value of \(f''\) is \(f''(0) = 2\). (c) Since \(f''(x) \ge 2 \gt 0\) for all \(x\), the graph is concave up on \((-\infty, \infty)\) and has no inflection points.

Where students lose points: Trying to solve \(e^x - x + 1 = 0\) and guessing at an answer. Also, finding \(g'(0) = 0\) but not showing that \(x = 0\) gives the smallest value of \(g\) on the whole line.

Problem 28
Let \(f(x) = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + 1\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are constants. (a) Find \(a\) and \(b\) so that the graph of \(f\) has an inflection point at \(x = 1\) and a local minimum at \(x = 3\). (b) With these values, find the inflection point and the local maximum, and justify each answer.
Hint
An inflection point at \(x = 1\) gives the equation \(f''(1) = 0\). A local minimum at \(x = 3\) for a function differentiable everywhere gives \(f'(3) = 0\). That is two equations for two unknowns.
Show full solution
  1. Compute the first two derivatives, keeping \(a\) and \(b\) as letters. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 3x^2 + 2ax + b \\ f''(x) &= 6x + 2a. \end{aligned}\]
  2. Use the inflection point. At an inflection point of a polynomial, \(f''\) must be 0. \[ f''(1) = 6 + 2a = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = -3. \]
  3. Use the local minimum. A polynomial is differentiable everywhere, so a local extremum can only be at a number where \(f' = 0\). Substitute \(a = -3\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(3) &= 3(9) + 2(-3)(3) + b = 0 \\ 27 - 18 + b &= 0 \\ b &= -9. \end{aligned}\] So \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 1\).
  4. Check that these values really work. Now \(f''(x) = 6x - 6\), a line with nonzero slope, so it changes sign at \(x = 1\): \(f''(0) = -6 \lt 0\) and \(f''(2) = 6 \gt 0\). That confirms an inflection point at \(x = 1\). Also \(f''(3) = 18 - 6 = 12 \gt 0\), so by the second derivative test \(x = 3\) is a local minimum.
  5. For (b), the inflection point's \(y\)-coordinate comes from \(f\). \[ f(1) = 1^3 - 3(1)^2 - 9(1) + 1 = 1 - 3 - 9 + 1 = -10. \]
  6. Find the other critical number by factoring \(f'\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 3x^2 - 6x - 9 \\ &= 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) \\ &= 3(x - 3)(x + 1). \end{aligned}\] So the critical numbers are \(x = 3\) and \(x = -1\).
  7. Second derivative test at \(x = -1\): \(f''(-1) = -6 - 6 = -12 \lt 0\), so \(f\) has a local maximum there. \[ f(-1) = (-1)^3 - 3(-1)^2 - 9(-1) + 1 = -1 - 3 + 9 + 1 = 6. \] As a check, the local minimum value is \(f(3) = 27 - 27 - 27 + 1 = -26\), and the inflection value \(-10\) is the average of \(6\) and \(-26\), as it always is for a cubic.

Answer: (a) \(a = -3\) and \(b = -9\), so \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 1\). (b) The inflection point is \((1, -10)\), and the local maximum is \(f(-1) = 6\) at \(x = -1\) (the local minimum is \(f(3) = -26\)).

Where students lose points: Using \(f(1) = 0\) or \(f(3) = 0\) as the conditions instead of \(f''(1) = 0\) and \(f'(3) = 0\). Also, not checking at the end that \(x = 3\) is a minimum and not a maximum.

Problem 29
Let \(f(x) = 3x^5 - 5x^4\). (a) Find the critical numbers and classify each one. Use the second derivative test wherever it gives an answer, and the first derivative test where it does not. (b) Find the open intervals where the graph is concave up and concave down, and all inflection points. (c) Draw a rough sketch of the graph and label the local extrema, the inflection points, and the \(x\)-intercepts.
Hint
Factor \(f'\) and \(f''\) completely by pulling out the highest power of \(x\) you can. At \(x = 0\) the second derivative test fails, so check the sign of \(f'\) on each side.
Show full solution
  1. The domain is all real numbers. Compute the first derivative and factor out \(5x^3\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 15x^4 - 20x^3 \\ &= 5x^3(3x - 4). \end{aligned}\] Setting this equal to 0 gives \(x = 0\) or \(x = \tfrac{4}{3}\). Since \(f'\) is a polynomial, there are no other critical numbers.
  2. Compute the second derivative and factor out \(60x^2\). \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= 60x^3 - 60x^2 \\ &= 60x^2(x - 1). \end{aligned}\]
  3. Second derivative test at \(x = \tfrac{4}{3}\). \[ f''\!\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = 60 \cdot \tfrac{16}{9} \cdot \left(\tfrac{4}{3} - 1\right) = 60 \cdot \tfrac{16}{9} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{960}{27} = \tfrac{320}{9} \gt 0, \] so \(f\) has a local minimum at \(x = \tfrac{4}{3}\). Its value uses \(x^5 = x^4 \cdot x\), so factor \(f(x) = x^4(3x - 5)\) first. \[\begin{aligned} f\!\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right) &= \left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right)^4\left(3 \cdot \tfrac{4}{3} - 5\right) \\ &= \tfrac{256}{81} \cdot (4 - 5) = -\tfrac{256}{81}. \end{aligned}\]
  4. Second derivative test at \(x = 0\): \(f''(0) = 60 \cdot 0 \cdot (0 - 1) = 0\), so the test is inconclusive. Switch to the first derivative test with test values \(-1\) and \(1\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(-1) &= 5(-1)^3(3(-1) - 4) = 5(-1)(-7) = 35 \gt 0 \\ f'(1) &= 5(1)^3(3 - 4) = 5(1)(-1) = -5 \lt 0. \end{aligned}\] The derivative changes from positive to negative at 0, so \(f\) has a local maximum \(f(0) = 0\) there. This is an example where \(f''(c) = 0\) and there still is an extremum.
  5. For (b), the candidates are the zeros of \(f''(x) = 60x^2(x - 1)\), which are \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\). Make a sign chart.
    IntervalTest \(x\)\(f''(x) = 60x^2(x-1)\)Concavity
    \((-\infty, 0)\)\(-1\)\(60(1)(-2) = -120 \lt 0\)concave down
    \((0, 1)\)\(\tfrac{1}{2}\)\(60 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot \left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = -\tfrac{15}{2} \lt 0\)concave down
    \((1, \infty)\)\(2\)\(60(4)(1) = 240 \gt 0\)concave up
  6. The concavity does not change at \(x = 0\), because the factor \(x^2\) is positive on both sides, so \((0, 0)\) is not an inflection point. It does change at \(x = 1\). \[ f(1) = 3(1)^5 - 5(1)^4 = 3 - 5 = -2. \] The only inflection point is \((1, -2)\).
  7. For (c), find the \(x\)-intercepts from the factored form. \[ f(x) = x^4(3x - 5) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 0 \ \text{or}\ x = \tfrac{5}{3}. \] The leading term \(3x^5\) makes \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to \infty\) and \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\). The graph rises to the local maximum at the origin, falls through the inflection point \((1, -2)\) to the local minimum \(\left(\tfrac{4}{3}, -\tfrac{256}{81}\right)\), then rises through \(\left(\tfrac{5}{3}, 0\right)\).

Answer: (a) Local maximum \(f(0) = 0\) at \(x = 0\) (first derivative test, since \(f''(0) = 0\)), and local minimum \(f\!\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right) = -\tfrac{256}{81}\) at \(x = \tfrac{4}{3}\) (second derivative test). (b) Concave down on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), concave up on \((1, \infty)\). The only inflection point is \((1, -2)\). (c) The graph rises to \((0, 0)\), falls through \((1, -2)\) to \(\left(\tfrac{4}{3}, -\tfrac{256}{81}\right)\), and rises through the \(x\)-intercept \(\left(\tfrac{5}{3}, 0\right)\).

Where students lose points: Concluding "no extremum at 0" because \(f''(0) = 0\), and calling \((0, 0)\) an inflection point just because \(f''(0) = 0\). Both conclusions need a sign check, and both are wrong here.
