Section 5.4: Curve sketching

The big idea

Curve sketching is drawing a graph by hand from facts you compute. The sign of \(f'\) tells you where the graph goes up or down, and the sign of \(f''\) tells you which way it bends. Find the points where those signs switch, compute their heights with \(f\), plot them, and connect them with the right shapes.

What curve sketching is

Curve sketching means drawing the graph of a function by hand, with no calculator. The picture must have the right shape. It shows where the graph rises and falls, where it bends up or down, where it crosses the axes, and what it does near asymptotes and far out to the left and right. Nothing in this section is a new derivative rule. It puts together everything from Sections 5.1 to 5.3 (critical numbers, the first derivative test, concavity, inflection points) with intercepts and limits, and turns all of it into one picture.

On the professor's practice Midterm 2 the first question was: "Draw a rough sketch of the graph of the function \(f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2\). Label any local extrema and inflection points." A rough sketch does not need to be drawn to an exact scale. It does need three things. The special points must be plotted at their correct coordinates. The coordinates must be written next to them. The curve between them must rise or fall and bend the correct way. The grade comes from the written work (the derivatives, the sign charts, the conclusions), so the work matters as much as the picture.

The facts from Sections 5.1 to 5.3 that you will use

Increasing, decreasing, and critical numbers

If \(f'(x) \gt 0\) for every \(x\) in an open interval, then \(f\) is increasing there (the graph rises as you move to the right). If \(f'(x) \lt 0\) on an open interval, then \(f\) is decreasing there.

A critical number of \(f\) is a number \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) where \(f'(c) = 0\) or \(f'(c)\) does not exist. A number that is not in the domain (for example \(x = 0\) for \(f(x) = x + \frac{1}{x}\)) is never a critical number, even though \(f'\) is undefined there.

In plain words: a positive slope means the graph goes uphill, and a negative slope means downhill. The critical numbers are the only places where the graph can switch between uphill and downhill, so they are the places to check.

First derivative test

Let \(c\) be a critical number of a function \(f\) that is continuous at \(c\).

  • If \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local (relative) maximum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) changes from negative to positive at \(c\), then \(f\) has a local (relative) minimum at \(c\).
  • If \(f'\) has the same sign on both sides of \(c\), then \(f\) has no local extremum at \(c\).

In plain words: going uphill and then downhill means you passed over a peak. Going downhill and then uphill means you passed through a valley. If you keep going the same way, there is no peak or valley there, even if the graph flattens for a moment.

Concavity and inflection points (the professor's definitions)

If the graph of \(f\) lies above all of its tangent lines on an interval, it is concave up there. The professor abbreviates this as C.U. If the graph lies below all of its tangent lines on an interval, it is concave down, written C.D.

Concavity test. If \(f''(x) \gt 0\) for all \(x\) in an interval, the graph is C.U. there. If \(f''(x) \lt 0\) for all \(x\) in an interval, the graph is C.D. there.

A point \(P = (c, f(c))\) on the graph is an inflection point if \(f\) is continuous at \(c\) and the graph changes concavity at \(P\), from C.U. to C.D. or from C.D. to C.U. At an inflection point, \(f''(c)\) is either 0 or does not exist. The reverse is false: \(f''(c) = 0\) does not by itself make \((c, f(c))\) an inflection point.

In plain words: concave up looks like part of a cup that holds water, and concave down looks like part of a cap or an upside-down cup. An inflection point is a point on the graph where the cup shape switches to a cap shape or the other way around.

Second derivative test

Suppose \(f'(c) = 0\) and \(f''\) is continuous on an open interval containing \(c\). If \(f''(c) \gt 0\), then \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\). If \(f''(c) \lt 0\), then \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\). If \(f''(c) = 0\), the test gives no information, and you use the first derivative test instead.

In plain words: a flat spot at the bottom of a cup is a valley, and a flat spot at the top of a cap is a peak. If the bending is zero at the flat spot, this shortcut cannot decide, so go back to the uphill and downhill test.

How to make a sign chart

Almost every step of a sketch needs the sign (positive or negative) of some expression on different intervals: the sign of \(f'\) tells you rising or falling, and the sign of \(f''\) tells you the bending. A sign chart is a small table that records those signs.

It works because of one fact. An expression built from continuous pieces can only switch from positive to negative (or back) at a number where it equals 0 or where it is undefined. Between two such numbers it keeps one sign the whole way. So you only need to find these "break numbers," cut the number line at them, and test one value inside each piece.

In plain words: an expression cannot jump from positive to negative without passing through 0 or breaking. So one test value per piece tells you the sign of the whole piece.

The professor builds the chart with one column for each factor. Each row is an interval. He writes the sign of every factor in that row and then multiplies the signs to get the sign of the whole expression. This is faster and safer than plugging the test value into the whole expression, because each factor's sign is easy to see.

How to make a sign chart
  1. Factor the expression completely. Write it as a product (and quotient) of simple factors such as \(x - 3\), \(x^2\), \(e^{-x}\), or \(x^2 + 1\).
  2. Find the break numbers: every \(x\) where a factor in the numerator is 0, and every \(x\) where a factor in the denominator is 0 (the expression is undefined there). Put them in order on a number line.
  3. Write the open intervals between consecutive break numbers, including \((-\infty, \text{smallest})\) and \((\text{largest}, \infty)\). If the function has a restricted domain, such as \(x \gt 0\), only use intervals inside the domain.
  4. Make a table with one row per interval and one column per factor. In each row, pick a test value strictly inside the interval and write the sign of each factor at that value.
  5. Multiply the signs across the row. An even number of minus signs gives \(+\), an odd number gives \(-\). Write the result in the last column, and then write what it means (increasing or decreasing for \(f'\), C.U. or C.D. for \(f''\)).

Some factors never change sign, and you can say so once instead of testing them: \(e^{\text{anything}} \gt 0\), \(x^2 + 1 \gt 0\), and a square like \((x - 1)^2\) is positive except at \(x = 1\), where it is 0. A squared factor is the reason an expression can touch 0 without changing sign.

Worked example 1 (a sign chart with a squared factor)

Asymptotes and end behavior

An asymptote is a line that the graph gets closer and closer to. You draw asymptotes as dashed lines before drawing the curve.

Vertical asymptotes

The line \(x = a\) is a vertical asymptote if \(f(x)\) goes to \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) from the left or from the right. Where to look:

  • For a rational function (a polynomial divided by a polynomial), first cancel any common factor of the numerator and the denominator. Then each number that still makes the denominator 0 gives a vertical asymptote. A number whose factor cancelled gives a hole in the graph, not an asymptote.
  • For \(\ln x\) and expressions like \(\frac{\ln x}{x}\), check the edge of the domain, \(x \to 0^+\).

One-sided behavior. For each vertical asymptote, find whether the graph goes up to \(\infty\) or down to \(-\infty\) on each side. Do it by signs. As \(x \to a^+\), the numerator approaches some nonzero number, and the denominator approaches 0 through positive values or through negative values. A nonzero number divided by a tiny positive number is huge with the same sign. Divided by a tiny negative number, it is huge with the opposite sign.

In plain words: a vertical asymptote is a wall that the graph shoots up or down along, at a place where the function would divide by zero. Find the walls from the denominator, then decide up or down on each side by asking whether the denominator is a tiny positive or a tiny negative number there.

Horizontal asymptotes and end behavior

The line \(y = L\) is a horizontal asymptote if \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L\) or \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = L\). Always compute both limits, because the two ends can behave differently.

  • Rational functions: divide the numerator and the denominator by the highest power of \(x\) in the denominator. If the numerator has smaller degree, the limit is 0. If the degrees are equal, the limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients. If the numerator has larger degree, there is no horizontal asymptote.
  • Polynomials have no asymptotes at all. Their end behavior comes from the leading term: for example \(x^3 \to \infty\) as \(x \to \infty\) and \(x^3 \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\).
  • Products like \(x e^{-x}\) or \(x \ln x\) give the indeterminate form \(0 \cdot \infty\) or \(\infty \cdot 0\). Rewrite the product as a quotient, for example \(x e^{-x} = \dfrac{x}{e^{x}}\), check that it is now of the form \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\) or \(\frac{0}{0}\), and use L'Hospital's rule: differentiate the top and the bottom separately.
  • The useful fact behind these: \(e^{x}\) grows faster than any power of \(x\), and \(\ln x\) grows slower than any positive power of \(x\). So \(\dfrac{x^2}{e^x} \to 0\) and \(\dfrac{\ln x}{x} \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\). On the exam, show the L'Hospital work rather than quoting this fact.

In plain words: a horizontal asymptote is the height the graph settles down to far to the right or far to the left. Check each side separately, because a graph can settle down on one side and run off to infinity on the other.

Symmetry

Even and odd functions

Compute \(f(-x)\) and simplify.

  • If \(f(-x) = f(x)\) for every \(x\) in the domain, \(f\) is even. The graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis: the left half is the mirror reflection of the right half. Example: \(x^4 - 6x^2 + 5\).
  • If \(f(-x) = -f(x)\) for every \(x\) in the domain, \(f\) is odd. The graph is symmetric about the origin: the left half is the right half rotated \(180^\circ\) around the origin. Example: \(x + \frac{1}{x}\).
  • Most functions are neither. Say so in one line: "\(f(-x) = \ldots\), which is neither \(f(x)\) nor \(-f(x)\), so \(f\) is neither even nor odd."

Symmetry saves work: once you know the right half of an even or odd graph, the left half is determined.

In plain words: an even graph looks the same in a mirror held along the \(y\)-axis. An odd graph looks the same after you turn the page upside down. Plugging in \(-x\) is how you test for either one.

The summary table and the four shapes

After you have the sign chart of \(f'\) and the sign chart of \(f''\), you combine them. Put every break number from both charts on one number line. On each small interval, \(f'\) and \(f''\) each have one sign, and the pair of signs fixes the shape of the graph on that piece. The textbook and the professor use this table:

\(f' \gt 0\) (increasing)\(f' \lt 0\) (decreasing)
\(f'' \gt 0\) (C.U.)rising and bending upward, getting steeper: the right half of a cup \(\cup\)falling and bending upward, leveling off: the left half of a cup \(\cup\)
\(f'' \lt 0\) (C.D.)rising and bending downward, leveling off: the left half of a cap \(\cap\)falling and bending downward, getting steeper: the right half of a cap \(\cap\)

The graph is then a chain of these four pieces glued together at the special points. A local maximum is where a rising C.D. piece meets a falling C.D. piece (the top of a cap). A local minimum is where a falling C.U. piece meets a rising C.U. piece (the bottom of a cup). An inflection point is where the bending flips.

In plain words: every small piece of the graph is one of four shapes: uphill cup, uphill cap, downhill cup, or downhill cap. The two signs \(f'\) and \(f''\) on that piece tell you which one to draw.

In lecture the professor collected the same information on a number line diagram drawn under the graph: one line marked "increasing" and "decreasing", one line marked where \(f\) is positive and negative, and one line marked C.U. and C.D., with the special points labeled on the lines. A summary table and a number line diagram hold the same facts. Use whichever you read faster.

The curve sketching checklist
  1. Domain. Exclude every \(x\) that gives division by 0, an even root of a negative number, or the logarithm of 0 or of a negative number.
  2. Intercepts. The \(y\)-intercept is \((0, f(0))\), if 0 is in the domain. The \(x\)-intercepts come from solving \(f(x) = 0\). If that equation is too hard, you may skip it. If you can factor \(f\), a quick sign chart of \(f\) itself shows where the graph is above or below the \(x\)-axis.
  3. Symmetry. Compute \(f(-x)\) and decide whether \(f\) is even, odd, or neither.
  4. Vertical asymptotes. Find them, and for each one find the one-sided limits \(x \to a^-\) and \(x \to a^+\).
  5. Horizontal asymptotes or end behavior. Compute \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x)\) (only the ends that are in the domain).
  6. First derivative. Compute \(f'\), factor it, and find the critical numbers (where \(f' = 0\) or \(f'\) does not exist, inside the domain). Make a sign chart of \(f'\). Write the intervals where \(f\) is increasing and decreasing, and use the first derivative test to find the local maxima and minima. Compute \(f\) at each one to get the point.
  7. Second derivative. Compute \(f''\), factor it, find where \(f'' = 0\) or does not exist, and make a sign chart of \(f''\). Write the intervals where \(f\) is C.U. and C.D. An inflection point is a point of the graph where the sign of \(f''\) actually changes. Compute \(f\) there to get the point.
  8. Summary table. Put all the break numbers from steps 6 and 7 on one number line. For each interval, write the sign of \(f'\), the sign of \(f''\), and the shape.
  9. Plot and draw. Draw the asymptotes as dashed lines. Plot the intercepts, the local extrema, the inflection points, and one or two extra points if a stretch is long. Connect them with a smooth curve that follows the shapes in the table. Do not connect across a vertical asymptote or across a number that is not in the domain. Label every local extremum and inflection point with its coordinates.

The textbook's list in the professor's notes has one more step, "check your graph using a graphing calculator." That is for homework only, since calculators are not allowed on the exam. The textbook also lists asymptotes before symmetry. The order of steps 1 to 5 does not matter. What matters is that every step appears in your work.

In plain words: first find where the graph lives and where it crosses the axes. Then find its walls and what happens far away. Then use \(f'\) for uphill and downhill and \(f''\) for cup and cap. Last, put the points on paper and connect them.

Cubic polynomials

A cubic \(f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d\) has no asymptotes, and \(f''\) is a linear function, so every cubic has exactly one inflection point. A cubic has either two local extrema (one max and one min) or none.

Worked example 2 (a cubic)

Sketch the graph of \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 27\). Label any local extrema and inflection points.

How to spot this type: the words "sketch" and "label any local extrema and inflection points" with a polynomial mean the full checklist, but with no asymptotes to find, since polynomials have none.

  1. Domain. \(f\) is a polynomial, so its domain is all real numbers, \((-\infty, \infty)\).
  2. Intercepts. For the \(y\)-intercept, substitute \(x = 0\): \(f(0) = 27\), so the point is \((0, 27)\). For the \(x\)-intercepts, factor by grouping. Take \(x^2\) out of the first two terms and \(-9\) out of the last two. \[\begin{aligned} f(x) &= x^2(x - 3) - 9(x - 3) \\ &= (x - 3)(x^2 - 9) \\ &= (x - 3)(x - 3)(x + 3) \\ &= (x - 3)^2(x + 3). \end{aligned}\] So \(f(x) = 0\) when \(x = 3\) or \(x = -3\). The \(x\)-intercepts are \((-3, 0)\) and \((3, 0)\). Since \((x - 3)^2 \ge 0\), the sign of \(f\) is the sign of \(x + 3\): the graph is below the \(x\)-axis for \(x \lt -3\) and above it for \(x \gt -3\), touching the axis at \(x = 3\).
  3. Symmetry. Substitute \(-x\) for \(x\). \[f(-x) = (-x)^3 - 3(-x)^2 - 9(-x) + 27 = -x^3 - 3x^2 + 9x + 27.\] This is not \(f(x)\), and it is not \(-f(x) = -x^3 + 3x^2 + 9x - 27\). So \(f\) is neither even nor odd.
  4. Asymptotes and end behavior. A polynomial has no vertical or horizontal asymptotes. The leading term is \(x^3\), so \[\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = \infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = -\infty.\]
  5. First derivative. Use the power rule term by term, then factor out 3 and factor the quadratic. \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= 3x^2 - 6x - 9 \\ &= 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) \\ &= 3(x - 3)(x + 1). \end{aligned}\] \(f'\) exists everywhere, and \(f'(x) = 0\) when \(x = 3\) or \(x = -1\). The critical numbers are \(-1\) and \(3\).

Quartic polynomials

A quartic (degree 4) polynomial has \(f''\) of degree 2, so it can have zero, one, or two inflection points. Two things often happen with quartics and are tested often: a critical number where \(f'\) does not change sign (so it is not an extremum), and a zero of \(f''\) where \(f''\) does not change sign (so it is not an inflection point).

Worked example 3 (a quartic with a flat inflection point)

Sketch the graph of \(f(x) = 3x^4 - 4x^3\). Label any local extrema and inflection points.

How to spot this type: a polynomial whose terms share a high power of \(x\) (here \(x^3\)) will give \(f'\) a squared factor like \(x^2\), which warns you that one critical number may not be an extremum.

  1. Domain. A polynomial, so \((-\infty, \infty)\).
  2. Intercepts. \(f(0) = 0\), so the \(y\)-intercept is \((0, 0)\). Factor out \(x^3\) to find the \(x\)-intercepts. \[3x^4 - 4x^3 = x^3(3x - 4) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 0 \text{ or } x = \tfrac{4}{3}.\] The \(x\)-intercepts are \((0, 0)\) and \(\left(\tfrac{4}{3}, 0\right)\).
  3. Symmetry. \(f(-x) = 3(-x)^4 - 4(-x)^3 = 3x^4 + 4x^3\). This is neither \(f(x)\) nor \(-f(x) = -3x^4 + 4x^3\), so \(f\) is neither even nor odd.
  4. Asymptotes and end behavior. No asymptotes (polynomial). The leading term \(3x^4\) is positive for large \(|x|\), so \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to \infty\) and as \(x \to -\infty\).
  5. First derivative. Power rule, then factor out the greatest common factor \(12x^2\). \[f'(x) = 12x^3 - 12x^2 = 12x^2(x - 1).\] \(f'(x) = 0\) when \(x = 0\) or \(x = 1\). The critical numbers are \(0\) and \(1\).

Come down from \(\infty\) on the left bending upward, flatten to a horizontal tangent at \((0, 0)\) while switching to C.D., keep falling through \(\left(\tfrac{2}{3}, -\tfrac{16}{27}\right)\) where the bending switches back to C.U., reach the bottom at \((1, -1)\), and rise through \(\left(\tfrac{4}{3}, 0\right)\) to \(\infty\).

Answer: local minimum \((1, -1)\); no local maximum (the critical number \(x = 0\) is not an extremum); inflection points \((0, 0)\) and \(\left(\tfrac{2}{3}, -\tfrac{16}{27}\right)\). Decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), increasing on \((1, \infty)\), C.U. on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((\tfrac{2}{3}, \infty)\), C.D. on \((0, \tfrac{2}{3})\).

Common mistake: \(f''(c) = 0\) does not mean inflection point. For \(f(x) = x^4\), \(f''(x) = 12x^2\), so \(f''(0) = 0\). But \(12x^2 \gt 0\) on both sides of 0, so the graph is C.U. on both sides and \((0, 0)\) is not an inflection point. It is the bottom of a cup. You must show the sign change in a sign chart before you call a point an inflection point. The same goes for \(f'(c) = 0\): it gives a critical number, and only a sign change of \(f'\) makes it a local extremum, as \(x = 0\) in Worked example 3 shows.

Rational functions

Rational functions bring in vertical and horizontal asymptotes. The concavity can change at a vertical asymptote, but that never gives an inflection point, because the function is not defined (so not continuous) there.

Worked example 4 (vertical and horizontal asymptotes)

Sketch the graph of \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2}{x^2 - 1}\). Label any local extrema and inflection points, and show the asymptotes.

How to spot this type: a fraction whose denominator can equal 0 means vertical asymptotes with one-sided limits, and the word "asymptotes" in the question tells you the grader expects them drawn as dashed lines.

  1. Domain. The denominator \(x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)\) is 0 at \(x = 1\) and \(x = -1\). The domain is all \(x\) except \(\pm 1\): \((-\infty, -1) \cup (-1, 1) \cup (1, \infty)\).
  2. Intercepts. \(f(0) = \dfrac{0}{-1} = 0\), so the \(y\)-intercept is \((0, 0)\). Setting \(f(x) = 0\) means the numerator \(2x^2 = 0\), so \(x = 0\). The only intercept is \((0, 0)\).
  3. Symmetry. Substitute \(-x\). \[f(-x) = \frac{2(-x)^2}{(-x)^2 - 1} = \frac{2x^2}{x^2 - 1} = f(x).\] So \(f\) is even, and the graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis.
  4. Vertical asymptotes. No factor cancels (the numerator \(2x^2\) is not 0 at \(\pm 1\)), so \(x = 1\) and \(x = -1\) are vertical asymptotes. Find the one-sided behavior at \(x = 1\). The numerator approaches \(2 \gt 0\). Write the denominator as \((x - 1)(x + 1)\), where \(x + 1 \to 2\).
    • As \(x \to 1^+\), \(x - 1\) is a small positive number, so the denominator is a small positive number and \(f(x) \to \infty\).
    • As \(x \to 1^-\), \(x - 1\) is a small negative number, so the denominator is a small negative number and \(f(x) \to -\infty\).
    By the \(y\)-axis symmetry, \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -1^+\) and \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to -1^-\).
  5. Horizontal asymptote. Divide the top and the bottom by \(x^2\). \[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{2x^2}{x^2 - 1} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{2}{1 - \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{2}{1 - 0} = 2.\] The line \(y = 2\) is a horizontal asymptote at both ends.
  6. First derivative. Use the quotient rule with \(u = 2x^2\), \(u' = 4x\), \(v = x^2 - 1\), \(v' = 2x\). \[\begin{aligned} f'(x) &= \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} = \frac{4x(x^2 - 1) - 2x^2(2x)}{(x^2 - 1)^2} \\ &= \frac{4x^3 - 4x - 4x^3}{(x^2 - 1)^2} = \frac{-4x}{(x^2 - 1)^2}. \end{aligned}\] \(f'(x) = 0\) when \(x = 0\). \(f'\) is undefined at \(x = \pm 1\), but those are not in the domain. The only critical number is \(0\).
  7. Sign chart for \(f'\). The denominator \((x^2 - 1)^2\) is positive on the whole domain, so the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(-4x\). Use \(-1\), \(0\), \(1\) as break numbers, since the asymptotes also split the line.

    \((-1, 0)\)\(-\tfrac{1}{2}\)\(+\)\(+\)\(+\)increasing
    \((0, 1)\)\(\tfrac{1}{2}\)\(-\)\(+\)\(-\)decreasing
    \((1, \infty)\)\(2\)\(-\)\(+\)\(-\)decreasing
    \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(x = 0\), so \(f\) has a local maximum there, at the point \((0, f(0)) = (0, 0)\).
  8. Second derivative. Use the quotient rule on \(f'(x) = \dfrac{-4x}{(x^2 - 1)^2}\) with \(u = -4x\), \(u' = -4\), \(v = (x^2 - 1)^2\), and \(v' = 2(x^2 - 1)\cdot 2x = 4x(x^2 - 1)\) by the chain rule. \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= \frac{-4(x^2 - 1)^2 - (-4x)\cdot 4x(x^2 - 1)}{(x^2 - 1)^4} \\ &= \frac{(x^2 - 1)\left[-4(x^2 - 1) + 16x^2\right]}{(x^2 - 1)^4} \\ &= \frac{-4x^2 + 4 + 16x^2}{(x^2 - 1)^3} = \frac{12x^2 + 4}{(x^2 - 1)^3} = \frac{4(3x^2 + 1)}{(x^2 - 1)^3}. \end{aligned}\] In the second line, the common factor \(x^2 - 1\) was pulled out of the numerator and cancelled against one factor of the denominator.

Answer: vertical asymptotes \(x = -1\) and \(x = 1\), horizontal asymptote \(y = 2\), local maximum \((0, 0)\), no inflection points. Increasing on \((-\infty, -1)\) and \((-1, 0)\), decreasing on \((0, 1)\) and \((1, \infty)\), C.U. on \((-\infty, -1)\) and \((1, \infty)\), C.D. on \((-1, 1)\).

Common mistake: inflection points at asymptotes. In Worked example 4 the sign of \(f''\) changes at \(x = 1\), but \(f(1)\) does not exist, so there is no point of the graph there. An inflection point must be a point \((c, f(c))\) on the graph. Also, when a factor cancels, such as in \(\frac{(x - 2)(x + 2)}{(x - 2)(x - 3)}\), the number \(x = 2\) gives a hole, not a vertical asymptote.

Exponential and logarithm products: L'Hospital at the ends

Functions like \(x e^{-x}\), \(x^2 e^{-x}\), \(x \ln x\), and \(\frac{\ln x}{x}\) need L'Hospital's rule to find what happens at the ends of the domain. The derivatives use the product rule or the quotient rule, and the factor \(e^{-x}\) is always positive, so it drops out of every sign chart.

Worked example 5 (x times e to the minus x)

Sketch the graph of \(f(x) = x e^{-x}\). Label any local extrema and inflection points.

How to spot this type: a power of \(x\) multiplied by \(e^{-x}\) or \(e^{x}\) means one end of the graph needs L'Hospital's rule, and the exponential factor is always positive, so it drops out of every sign chart.

  1. Domain. Both \(x\) and \(e^{-x}\) are defined for every \(x\), so the domain is \((-\infty, \infty)\).
  2. Intercepts. \(f(0) = 0 \cdot e^0 = 0\). Since \(e^{-x} \gt 0\), \(f(x) = 0\) only when \(x = 0\). The only intercept is \((0, 0)\). Also, \(f\) has the sign of \(x\): negative for \(x \lt 0\), positive for \(x \gt 0\).
  3. Symmetry. \(f(-x) = -x e^{x}\). This is neither \(f(x) = x e^{-x}\) nor \(-f(x) = -x e^{-x}\), so \(f\) is neither even nor odd.
  4. Vertical asymptotes. None, because \(f\) is continuous everywhere.
  5. End behavior. As \(x \to \infty\), the product \(x e^{-x}\) has the form \(\infty \cdot 0\). Rewrite it as a quotient, which has the form \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\), and use L'Hospital's rule. \[\lim_{x\to\infty} x e^{-x} = \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x}{e^{x}} \;\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\; \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{1}{e^{x}} = 0.\] So \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote on the right. As \(x \to -\infty\), \(x \to -\infty\) and \(e^{-x} \to \infty\), which is not an indeterminate form: the product goes to \(-\infty\). There is no horizontal asymptote on the left.
  6. First derivative. Use the product rule with \(u = x\), \(u' = 1\), \(v = e^{-x}\), \(v' = -e^{-x}\) (chain rule). \[f'(x) = u'v + uv' = e^{-x} - x e^{-x} = e^{-x}(1 - x).\] Since \(e^{-x} \gt 0\), \(f'(x) = 0\) only when \(1 - x = 0\), so the critical number is \(x = 1\).
  7. Sign chart for \(f'\).
    IntervalTest value\(e^{-x}\)\(1 - x\)\(f'(x)\)\(f\)
    \((-\infty, 1)\)\(0\)\(+\)\(+\)\(+\)increasing
    \((1, \infty)\)\(2\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)decreasing
    \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(x = 1\), so there is a local maximum, and \(f(1) = 1 \cdot e^{-1} = \frac{1}{e}\). Local maximum \(\left(1, \frac{1}{e}\right)\), about \((1, 0.37)\).
  8. Second derivative. Use the product rule on \(f'(x) = e^{-x}(1 - x)\) with \(u = e^{-x}\), \(u' = -e^{-x}\), \(v = 1 - x\), \(v' = -1\). \[\begin{aligned} f''(x) &= -e^{-x}(1 - x) + e^{-x}(-1) \\ &= e^{-x}\left[-(1 - x) - 1\right] \\ &= e^{-x}(x - 2). \end{aligned}\] \(f''(x) = 0\) only when \(x = 2\).
  9. Sign chart for \(f''\).
    IntervalTest value\(e^{-x}\)\(x - 2\)\(f''(x)\)Concavity
    \((-\infty, 2)\)\(0\)\(+\)\(-\)\(-\)C.D.
    \((2, \infty)\)\(3\)\(+\)\(+\)\(+\)C.U.
    The concavity changes at \(x = 2\), and \(f(2) = 2e^{-2} = \frac{2}{e^2}\). Inflection point \(\left(2, \frac{2}{e^2}\right)\), about \((2, 0.27)\).
  10. Summary table.
    Interval\(f'\)\(f''\)Shape

    \((1, 2)\)\(-\)\(-\)decreasing, C.D.
    \((2, \infty)\)\(-\)\(+\)decreasing, C.U.
  11. Plot and draw. The graph comes up from \(-\infty\) on the left, passes through the origin, reaches its top at \(\left(1, \frac{1}{e}\right)\), falls through the inflection point \(\left(2, \frac{2}{e^2}\right)\), and then flattens out toward the \(x\)-axis from above without ever reaching it.

Answer: local maximum \(\left(1, \frac{1}{e}\right)\), inflection point \(\left(2, \frac{2}{e^2}\right)\), horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\) as \(x \to \infty\), and \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\). Increasing on \((-\infty, 1)\), decreasing on \((1, \infty)\), C.D. on \((-\infty, 2)\), C.U. on \((2, \infty)\).

Common mistake: using L'Hospital on a product. L'Hospital's rule only applies to a quotient of the form \(\frac{0}{0}\) or \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\). First rewrite \(x e^{-x}\) as \(\frac{x}{e^x}\), or \(x \ln x\) as \(\frac{\ln x}{1/x}\). Also check the form before using it: as \(x \to -\infty\), \(x e^{-x}\) is \((-\infty)\cdot(\infty)\), which is not indeterminate, so you just read off \(-\infty\).

Corners and cusps: when f' does not exist

Functions with fractional powers, such as \(x^{2/3}\), are defined for every \(x\), because the cube root of a negative number is fine. Their derivatives, however, can blow up at \(x = 0\). Then \(x = 0\) is a critical number because \(f'(0)\) does not exist. The graph can have a cusp there: a sharp point where the tangent line becomes vertical. A corner is a sharp point where the slopes from the left and from the right are different finite numbers, like \(|x|\) at 0.

In plain words: where the slope formula divides by zero but the function itself is fine, the graph has a sharp tip. A cusp is a tip so steep that it points straight up or straight down. A corner is a tip with two ordinary slopes, like the bottom of a V.

Worked example 6 (a cusp)

Sketch the graph of \(f(x) = 3x^{2/3} - 2x\). Label any local extrema and inflection points.

How to spot this type: a fractional exponent such as \(x^{2/3}\) or \(x^{1/3}\) means \(f'\) will have \(x^{1/3}\) in a denominator, so look for a critical number where \(f'\) does not exist and a sharp point (cusp) on the graph.

  1. Domain. \(x^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{x}\right)^2\), and the cube root is defined for every real number. The domain is \((-\infty, \infty)\).
  2. Intercepts. \(f(0) = 0\), so the \(y\)-intercept is \((0, 0)\). For the \(x\)-intercepts, write \(x = x^{1/3}\cdot x^{2/3}\) and factor out \(x^{2/3}\). \[f(x) = 3x^{2/3} - 2x^{1/3}x^{2/3} = x^{2/3}\left(3 - 2x^{1/3}\right).\] This is 0 when \(x^{2/3} = 0\), so \(x = 0\), or when \(3 - 2x^{1/3} = 0\), so \(x^{1/3} = \frac{3}{2}\) and \(x = \left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{27}{8}\). The \(x\)-intercepts are \((0, 0)\) and \(\left(\frac{27}{8}, 0\right)\).
  3. Symmetry. \(f(-x) = 3(-x)^{2/3} + 2x = 3x^{2/3} + 2x\), since \((-x)^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{-x}\right)^2 = \left(-\sqrt[3]{x}\right)^2 = x^{2/3}\). This is neither \(f(x)\) nor \(-f(x)\). Neither even nor odd.
  4. Asymptotes and end behavior. No vertical asymptotes (\(f\) is continuous everywhere). Use the factored form \(x^{2/3}(3 - 2x^{1/3})\). As \(x \to \infty\), \(x^{2/3} \to \infty\) and \(3 - 2x^{1/3} \to -\infty\), so \(f(x) \to -\infty\). As \(x \to -\infty\), \(x^{2/3} \to \infty\) and \(3 - 2x^{1/3} \to \infty\), so \(f(x) \to \infty\). No horizontal asymptotes.
  5. First derivative. Power rule: \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[3x^{2/3}\right] = 3\cdot\frac{2}{3}x^{-1/3} = 2x^{-1/3}\). Then write over a common denominator. \[f'(x) = 2x^{-1/3} - 2 = \frac{2}{x^{1/3}} - \frac{2x^{1/3}}{x^{1/3}} = \frac{2\left(1 - x^{1/3}\right)}{x^{1/3}}.\] \(f'(x) = 0\) when \(x^{1/3} = 1\), so \(x = 1\). \(f'(x)\) does not exist when \(x^{1/3} = 0\), so \(x = 0\), and 0 is in the domain. The critical numbers are \(0\) and \(1\).
  6. Sign chart for \(f'\), with factors \(1 - x^{1/3}\) and \(x^{1/3}\). Test values \(-1\), \(\frac{1}{8}\), \(8\) make the cube roots easy: \(\sqrt[3]{-1} = -1\), \(\sqrt[3]{1/8} = \frac{1}{2}\), \(\sqrt[3]{8} = 2\).
    IntervalTest value\(1 - x^{1/3}\)\(x^{1/3}\)\(f'(x)\)\(f\)
    \((-\infty, 0)\)\(-1\)\(+\) (it is 2)\(-\)\(-\)decreasing
    \((0, 1)\)\(\tfrac{1}{8}\)\(+\) (it is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\))\(+\)\(+\)increasing
    \((1, \infty)\)\(8\)\(-\) (it is \(-1\))\(+\)\(-\)decreasing
    For example, \(f'(-1) = \dfrac{2(2)}{-1} = -4\) and \(f'(8) = \dfrac{2(-1)}{2} = -1\).
  7. Local extrema. At \(x = 0\), \(f'\) changes from negative to positive, so there is a local minimum at \((0, f(0)) = (0, 0)\). The tangent lines become vertical from both sides, so the minimum is a cusp pointing down. At \(x = 1\), \(f'\) changes from positive to negative, so there is a local maximum: \(f(1) = 3(1) - 2(1) = 1\). Local maximum \((1, 1)\).
  8. Second derivative. Since \(x^{4/3} = \left(x^{1/3}\right)^4 \gt 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\), \(f''(x) \lt 0\) for all \(x \ne 0\).

    \((0, \infty)\)\(1\)\(-\)\(+\)\(-\)C.D.

Answer: local minimum \((0, 0)\), which is a cusp; local maximum \((1, 1)\); no inflection points. Decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((1, \infty)\), increasing on \((0, 1)\), C.D. on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((0, \infty)\).

Common mistake: missing the critical number where f' does not exist. If you only solve \(f'(x) = 0\) in Worked example 6, you find \(x = 1\) and miss the local minimum at \(x = 0\). Always look at the denominator of \(f'\) too. If it is 0 at a number in the domain of \(f\), that number is a critical number.

The reverse task: sketch a graph from conditions

Some problems give no formula. They list facts about \(f\), \(f'\), and \(f''\), and ask for a graph of any function with all of those facts. In lecture the professor did one whose graph has a sharp local minimum with a vertical tangent line. Translate each condition into a picture, using the four shapes, then draw one curve that does all of it.

ConditionWhat it means for the picture
\(f(a) = b\)the point \((a, b)\) is on the graph
\(f'(a) = 0\)horizontal tangent line at \(x = a\)
\(f' \gt 0\) or \(f' \lt 0\) on an intervalthe graph rises or falls there
\(f'' \gt 0\) or \(f'' \lt 0\) on an intervalthe graph is C.U. (cup) or C.D. (cap) there
\(f'(a)\) does not exist, \(f\) continuous at \(a\)a corner or a cusp at \(x = a\), with no break in the graph
\(\lim_{x\to a} f'(x) = \pm\infty\)the tangent line becomes vertical at \(x = a\)
\(\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = L\)the graph levels off toward the dashed line \(y = L\) on the right

In plain words: each line of the list is one small instruction for your pencil: go through this point, go uphill here, bend like a cup there. Draw any one curve that obeys every instruction at once.

Worked example 7 (graph from conditions)

Sketch the graph of a function \(f\) with all of these properties.
(a) \(f\) is continuous and differentiable on \((-\infty, \infty)\).
(b) \(f(0) = 2\), \(f(-2) = \frac{3}{2}\), and \(f(2) = \frac{3}{2}\).
(c) \(f'(0) = 0\), \(f'(x) \gt 0\) on \((-\infty, 0)\), and \(f'(x) \lt 0\) on \((0, \infty)\).
(d) \(f''(x) \gt 0\) on \((-\infty, -2)\) and \((2, \infty)\), and \(f''(x) \lt 0\) on \((-2, 2)\).
(e) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = 0\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x) = 0\).

How to spot this type: there is no formula, only a lettered list of facts about \(f\), \(f'\), \(f''\), and limits, and the question says "a function having the following properties", so you translate each line into a piece of the picture.

  1. Draw the asymptote from (e). Both limits are 0, so the \(x\)-axis, \(y = 0\), is a horizontal asymptote at both ends.
  2. Read the rising and falling from (c). The graph rises on \((-\infty, 0)\) and falls on \((0, \infty)\). By the first derivative test, \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x = 0\), and by (b) that point is \((0, 2)\). Since \(f'(0) = 0\), the top is smooth with a horizontal tangent line, not a sharp point.
  3. Read the bending from (d). The graph is C.U. on \((-\infty, -2)\), C.D. on \((-2, 2)\), and C.U. on \((2, \infty)\). The concavity changes at \(x = -2\) and \(x = 2\), and \(f\) is continuous there, so \(\left(-2, \frac{3}{2}\right)\) and \(\left(2, \frac{3}{2}\right)\) are inflection points.
  4. Combine the signs into a summary table.
    Interval\(f'\)\(f''\)Shape
    \((-\infty, -2)\)\(+\)\(+\)increasing, C.U.
    \((-2, 0)\)\(+\)\(-\)increasing, C.D.
    \((0, 2)\)\(-\)\(-\)decreasing, C.D.
    \((2, \infty)\)\(-\)\(+\)decreasing, C.U.
  5. Draw. Start just above the \(x\)-axis on the far left (the graph rises toward the right, and it levels off at height 0, so it must be above 0). Rise while bending upward to \(\left(-2, \frac{3}{2}\right)\), switch to bending downward, round over the top at \((0, 2)\), fall to \(\left(2, \frac{3}{2}\right)\), switch back to bending upward, and level off toward the \(x\)-axis on the right. The result is a bell-shaped curve. One function with all of these properties is \(f(x) = \dfrac{24}{x^2 + 12}\), drawn below. Any graph with the same features is a correct answer.

Answer: a bell-shaped curve above the \(x\)-axis with horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\) at both ends, local maximum \((0, 2)\), and inflection points \(\left(-2, \frac{3}{2}\right)\) and \(\left(2, \frac{3}{2}\right)\), as drawn above.

What to write on the exam. Write each conclusion as a sentence that names the reason. For example: "Since \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(x = -1\), \(f\) has a local maximum at \((-1, 32)\)." "Since \(f''\) changes from negative to positive at \(x = 1\) and \(f\) is continuous there, \((1, 16)\) is an inflection point." "There is no local extremum at \(x = 0\) because \(f'\) is negative on both sides of 0." "\(f''(1) = 0\), but \(f''\) does not change sign at 1, so there is no inflection point there." Then draw the graph with the points plotted and labeled by their coordinates, the asymptotes dashed, and the behavior at the ends visible.
More mistakes to avoid.
  • Computing the \(y\)-coordinate of a point with \(f'\) or \(f''\) instead of \(f\). The heights of extrema and inflection points always come from the original \(f\).
  • Giving an inflection point as just an \(x\)-value. By the professor's definition an inflection point is a point of the graph, so write \((1, 16)\), not "\(x = 1\)."
  • Testing a sign chart at a break number. Test values must be strictly inside the intervals; at a break number the expression is 0 or undefined.
  • Forgetting that a vertical asymptote or an excluded number also splits the sign chart, even though it is not a critical number.
  • Expecting a local maximum value to be larger than a local minimum value. For \(f(x) = x + \frac{1}{x}\), the local maximum \((-1, -2)\) is lower than the local minimum \((1, 2)\), because the two points are on different branches.
  • Connecting the curve across a vertical asymptote, or drawing the graph to the left of \(x = 0\) for \(x \ln x\) or \(\frac{\ln x}{x}\).
  • Drawing a cup where the table says cap. Before drawing each piece, read its row of the summary table.
If you get stuck
  • Write \(f'\) and \(f''\) as one fraction each, then factor the top and the bottom completely before you start a sign chart.
  • Before expanding the numerator of \(f''\), look for a common factor in both terms, such as \(e^{-x}\), \(x - 1\), or \(x^2 + 4\), and pull it out first. The algebra gets much shorter.
  • If a quadratic factor will not factor, compute \(b^2 - 4ac\). If it is negative and the leading coefficient is positive, the factor is always positive, so it never changes the sign.
  • Make a table of \(x\) and \(f(x)\) at every critical number, every inflection number, every intercept, and one point beyond each end. Plot those points first, then connect them.
  • For a limit at an end of the domain that looks like \(0\cdot\infty\), rewrite the product as a quotient and use L'Hospital's rule.
  • If your picture disagrees with your table, check one row by putting its test value into the unfactored \(f'\) or \(f''\). A sign slip shows up right away.
Quick check
Can a critical number fail to be a local extremum? Give an example.

Yes. For \(f(x) = x^3\), \(f'(0) = 0\), but \(f'(x) = 3x^2 \gt 0\) on both sides of 0, so the graph keeps rising and has no peak or valley at 0.

If \(f''(4) = 0\), must the graph have an inflection point at \(x = 4\)?

No. For \(f(x) = (x - 4)^4\), \(f''(x) = 12(x - 4)^2\) is 0 at 4 but positive on both sides, so the graph is concave up on both sides and the concavity never changes.

The graph of \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\) is concave down for \(x \lt 0\) and concave up for \(x \gt 0\). Does it have an inflection point?

No. The concavity changes at \(x = 0\), but 0 is not in the domain, so there is no point of the graph there to call an inflection point.

On some interval, \(f' \gt 0\) and \(f'' \lt 0\). What does the graph look like there?

It rises while bending downward, so it climbs more and more slowly, like the left half of a cap.

Find all asymptotes of \(f(x) = \dfrac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 4}\).

Vertical asymptotes \(x = 2\) and \(x = -2\), because the denominator is 0 there and the numerator is \(13 \ne 0\). Horizontal asymptote \(y = 3\) at both ends, because the degrees are equal and the leading coefficients give \(\frac{3}{1} = 3\).

What to have memorized
  • The checklist: domain, intercepts, symmetry, vertical asymptotes with one-sided limits, horizontal asymptotes or end behavior, \(f'\) with critical numbers and a sign chart, \(f''\) with a sign chart, the summary table, then plot and label.
  • A critical number is a number in the domain where \(f' = 0\) or \(f'\) does not exist.
  • First derivative test: \(f'\) from \(+\) to \(-\) gives a local max, from \(-\) to \(+\) gives a local min, no sign change gives neither.
  • \(f'' \gt 0\) means C.U. (above the tangent lines), \(f'' \lt 0\) means C.D. An inflection point is a point of the graph where \(f\) is continuous and the concavity changes.
  • The four shapes: \(+\,+\) rising cup, \(+\,-\) rising cap, \(-\,+\) falling cup, \(-\,-\) falling cap.
  • Sign chart: factor, find break numbers (zeros and undefined points), one test value per interval, multiply the signs of the factors.
  • Even: \(f(-x) = f(x)\), symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. Odd: \(f(-x) = -f(x)\), symmetric about the origin.
  • Rational functions: vertical asymptotes where the denominator is 0 after cancelling; horizontal asymptote from the degrees (smaller on top gives \(y = 0\), equal degrees give the ratio of the leading coefficients).
  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x^n}{e^x} = 0\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} x \ln x = 0\), both by L'Hospital after writing the expression as a quotient.
  • Every cubic has exactly one inflection point. A polynomial has no asymptotes.

Words that change the method. "Local" and "relative" mean the same thing: a peak or valley compared with nearby points, found with the first derivative test. "Absolute" or "global" means the highest or lowest value of all, which needs the endpoints and Section 6.1. "Rough sketch" means the shape and the labeled points must be right, but the scale does not have to be exact. "Label" means write the coordinates \((x, y)\) next to each point, not just the \(x\)-value. "Find the \(x\)-values" asks only for \(x\), while "find the inflection points" asks for points \((c, f(c))\). "Justify" or "explain" means a sentence that names the test, such as "because \(f'\) changes from positive to negative at \(x = 2\)". "Show the asymptotes" means draw them as dashed lines and give the one-sided limits. "Intercepts where possible" means you may skip \(f(x) = 0\) if it cannot be solved by hand. "A single function that has all of the properties" means any one graph that works is a full answer. "The graph of \(f'\) is shown" means read signs and slopes from that picture, not heights of \(f\). "How many" still requires the sign charts that justify the count.

Problem 4
Find all vertical and horizontal asymptotes of \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x^2 - 8}{x^2 - 5x + 6}\). For each vertical asymptote, give the limits from the left and from the right.
Show full solution
  1. Factor the numerator and the denominator. \[f(x) = \frac{2(x^2 - 4)}{(x - 2)(x - 3)} = \frac{2(x - 2)(x + 2)}{(x - 2)(x - 3)}.\] The domain is all \(x\) except \(2\) and \(3\).
  2. Cancel the common factor \(x - 2\). For \(x \ne 2\), \[f(x) = \frac{2(x + 2)}{x - 3}.\] After cancelling, only \(x = 3\) makes the denominator 0, so \(x = 3\) is the only vertical asymptote. At \(x = 2\) the graph has a hole at height \(\frac{2(4)}{-1} = -8\), not an asymptote.
  3. One-sided limits at \(x = 3\). The numerator \(2(x + 2) \to 10 \gt 0\). As \(x \to 3^+\), \(x - 3\) is a small positive number, so \(f(x) \to \infty\). As \(x \to 3^-\), \(x - 3\) is a small negative number, so \(f(x) \to -\infty\).
  4. Horizontal asymptote. The degrees are equal, so divide the top and the bottom of the original fraction by \(x^2\). \[\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{2x^2 - 8}{x^2 - 5x + 6} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty} \frac{2 - \frac{8}{x^2}}{1 - \frac{5}{x} + \frac{6}{x^2}} = \frac{2 - 0}{1 - 0 + 0} = 2.\]

Answer: vertical asymptote \(x = 3\), with \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to 3^-\) and \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to 3^+\). Horizontal asymptote \(y = 2\) at both ends. The graph has a hole at \((2, -8)\), not a vertical asymptote.

Problem 5
Decide whether each function is even, odd, or neither, and say what that means for its graph: (a) \(f(x) = x^3 - 5x\), (b) \(g(x) = \dfrac{6}{x^2 + 2}\), (c) \(h(x) = x^4 + x\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Substitute \(-x\) and simplify. \[f(-x) = (-x)^3 - 5(-x) = -x^3 + 5x = -(x^3 - 5x) = -f(x).\] So \(f\) is odd, and its graph is symmetric about the origin (a \(180^\circ\) rotation).
  2. (b) Substitute \(-x\). \[g(-x) = \frac{6}{(-x)^2 + 2} = \frac{6}{x^2 + 2} = g(x).\] So \(g\) is even, and its graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis (a mirror reflection).
  3. (c) Substitute \(-x\). \[h(-x) = (-x)^4 + (-x) = x^4 - x.\] This is not \(h(x) = x^4 + x\). It is also not \(-h(x) = -x^4 - x\). For example \(h(1) = 2\) while \(h(-1) = 0\), which is neither \(2\) nor \(-2\). So \(h\) is neither even nor odd.

Answer: (a) odd, symmetric about the origin; (b) even, symmetric about the \(y\)-axis; (c) neither.

Problem 6
Let \(f(x) = (x + 2)e^{-x}\). Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x)\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} f(x)\), and state any horizontal asymptote. Then find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} x^2\ln x\).
Show full solution
  1. As \(x \to \infty\), \(x + 2 \to \infty\) and \(e^{-x} \to 0\), the indeterminate form \(\infty \cdot 0\). Rewrite as a quotient of the form \(\frac{\infty}{\infty}\) and use L'Hospital's rule. \[\lim_{x\to\infty} (x + 2)e^{-x} = \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{x + 2}{e^{x}} \;\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\; \lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{1}{e^{x}} = 0.\]
  2. As \(x \to -\infty\), \(x + 2 \to -\infty\) and \(e^{-x} \to \infty\). This is not an indeterminate form: a huge negative number times a huge positive number gives \(-\infty\). \[\lim_{x\to-\infty} (x + 2)e^{-x} = -\infty.\] So \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote on the right only.
  3. As \(x \to 0^+\), \(x^2 \to 0\) and \(\ln x \to -\infty\), the form \(0 \cdot (-\infty)\). Rewrite as \(\dfrac{\ln x}{x^{-2}}\), which has the form \(\frac{-\infty}{\infty}\), and use L'Hospital's rule. The derivative of \(x^{-2}\) is \(-2x^{-3}\). \[\lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\ln x}{x^{-2}} \;\overset{\text{L'H}}{=}\; \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{\frac{1}{x}}{-2x^{-3}} = \lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{x^3}{-2} = \lim_{x\to 0^+} \left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\right) = 0.\]

Answer: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} (x + 2)e^{-x} = 0\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty} (x + 2)e^{-x} = -\infty\), so \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote on the right only. \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} x^2\ln x = 0\).

Problem 22
Decide whether each statement is true or false, and explain why. For each false statement, give one specific function that shows it is false.
(a) If \(f\) is an even function, then \(f'\) is an even function.
(b) If \(f''(5) = 0\), then the graph of \(f\) has an inflection point at \(x = 5\).
(c) Every function has a relative maximum and a relative minimum.
(d) The function \(f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2 + 5x - 7\) has one local maximum and one local minimum.
(e) If \(f''(x) \gt 0\) for every \(x\) in an interval, then \(f'\) is increasing on that interval.
Hint
For each false statement, try the simplest functions you know, such as \(x^2\), \(x^3\), and \((x - 5)^4\), and see whether one of them breaks it.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Test the even function \(f(x) = x^2\). It is even because \(f(-x) = (-x)^2 = x^2 = f(x)\). Its derivative is \(f'(x) = 2x\), and \[f'(-x) = 2(-x) = -2x = -f'(x).\] For example \(f'(1) = 2\) but \(f'(-1) = -2\), so \(f'(-1) \ne f'(1)\) and \(f'\) is not even. The statement is false. (The mirror image of an uphill slope is a downhill slope, so the derivative of an even function is odd.)
  2. (b) Test \(f(x) = (x - 5)^4\). By the chain rule, \(f'(x) = 4(x - 5)^3\) and \(f''(x) = 12(x - 5)^2\). So \(f''(5) = 12(0)^2 = 0\). But \(12(x - 5)^2 \gt 0\) for every \(x \ne 5\), so the graph is concave up on both sides of 5. The concavity does not change, so \((5, 0)\) is not an inflection point. It is the bottom of a cup. The statement is false.
  3. (c) Test \(f(x) = x^3\). Its derivative \(f'(x) = 3x^2\) is 0 only at \(x = 0\) and is positive on both sides of 0. Since \(f'\) never changes sign, the first derivative test gives no relative extremum anywhere. The statement is false.
  4. (d) Compute the derivative: \(f'(x) = 3x^2 + 6x + 5\). Its discriminant is \[b^2 - 4ac = 6^2 - 4(3)(5) = 36 - 60 = -24 \lt 0.\] So \(f'(x) = 0\) has no real solution. Completing the square shows the sign: \[3x^2 + 6x + 5 = 3(x^2 + 2x + 1) + 2 = 3(x + 1)^2 + 2 \gt 0.\] So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty, \infty)\), has no critical numbers, and has no local extrema at all. The statement is false.
  5. (e) \(f''\) is the derivative of \(f'\). A function whose derivative is positive on an interval is increasing there (the rule from Section 5.1, applied to the function \(f'\)). So \(f'\) is increasing on the interval. The statement is true. This is exactly why concave up means the slopes get larger as you move to the right.

Answer: (a) false, \(x^2\) is even but \(2x\) is odd; (b) false, \((x - 5)^4\) has \(f''(5) = 0\) and no inflection point; (c) false, \(x^3\) has no relative extrema; (d) false, \(f'(x) = 3(x + 1)^2 + 2 \gt 0\), so there are no local extrema; (e) true.

Where students lose points: writing "false" with no example, or giving an example without checking it, such as using \(x^3\) in (b), where the concavity really does change at its zero of \(f''\).

Write \(f(x) = (x^2 - 2x - 3)^{-1}\) and use the chain rule (the general power rule). \[f'(x) = -1\cdot(x^2 - 2x - 3)^{-2}\cdot(2x - 2) = \frac{-2(x - 1)}{(x^2 - 2x - 3)^2} = \frac{-2(x - 1)}{(x - 3)^2(x + 1)^2}.\] \(f'(x) = 0\) at \(x = 1\), which is in the domain. The only critical number is \(1\).
  7. Sign chart for \(f'\). The denominator is a square, so it is positive on the domain, and the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(-2(x - 1)\). The break numbers are \(-1\), \(1\), \(3\).

Where students lose points: giving only one side of each vertical asymptote, or calling \(x = -1\) and \(x = 3\) inflection points because the concavity changes there, even though they are not in the domain.
