Section 6.3: Further business applications

The big idea

This section answers two business questions with derivatives. First: if a store changes its price, does it take in more money or less? The elasticity \(E\) answers that, and the one fact to remember is that revenue goes up with price when \(E \lt 1\), goes down when \(E \gt 1\), and is largest when \(E = 1\). Second: how big should each batch or order be so the yearly cost is smallest? That is one optimization problem whose answer is \(q = \sqrt{2fM/k}\), as long as the usual assumptions hold.

Section 6.3 has three business applications of calculus: the economic lot size, the economic order quantity, and the elasticity of demand. The professor lectured on all three in Lecture 18. Elasticity of demand is the one that showed up on his real Fall 2025 Midterm 2. That question had a quadratic demand function, asked for the price of unit elasticity, and asked whether the price should be increased or decreased. So this lesson starts with elasticity and gives it the most space.

The lot size and the order quantity come second. They are one optimization problem with one formula. You still have to know how the cost function is built, because the professor showed in lecture that changing one assumption changes the answer.

Part A. Elasticity of demand

Demand as a function of price

A demand function tells you how many units customers will buy at a given price. In this section the demand is written as \(q = f(p)\). Here \(p\) is the price per unit (in dollars) and \(q\) is the quantity demanded, which means the number of units customers buy at that price. Before this section you usually saw it the other way around, as \(p = D(q)\). Both describe the same relationship: \(f\) is the inverse function of \(D\).

Demand goes down when the price goes up. In the professor's words, demand \(q = f(p)\) is a decreasing function of price \(p\). So \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) is negative.

What elasticity measures

Suppose a store raises a price by \$1. Whether that is a big change depends on the starting price. A \$1 raise on a \$2 item is a 50% raise, and a \$1 raise on a \$200 item is a 0.5% raise. The same goes for quantity. So economists compare relative changes (percent changes), not raw changes. The relative change in price is \(\dfrac{\Delta p}{p}\) and the relative change in quantity is \(\dfrac{\Delta q}{q}\). Elasticity compares the two:

\[\frac{\Delta q / q}{\Delta p / p} = \frac{\Delta q}{q}\cdot\frac{p}{\Delta p} = \frac{p}{q}\cdot\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta p}.\]

As \(\Delta p \to 0\), the ratio \(\dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta p}\) becomes the derivative \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) (this is the definition of the derivative). So the ratio becomes \(\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). This number is negative, because \(p\) and \(q\) are positive and \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) is negative. The elasticity of demand is defined as its opposite, so that it comes out positive.

Elasticity of demand

If the demand is \(q = f(p)\), the elasticity of demand at price \(p\) is

\[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\frac{dq}{dp}.\]

The minus sign is there because \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) is negative (demand decreases as price increases), so the minus sign makes \(E\) positive. In words, \(E\) is approximately the percent decrease in the quantity demanded caused by a 1% increase in price.

In plain words: elasticity tells you how strongly customers react to a price change. Raise the price by one percent, and the number of units sold drops by about \(E\) percent.

For example, if \(E = 2\) at the current price, then raising the price by 1% makes the quantity demanded drop by about 2%. If \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}\), raising the price by 1% makes the quantity demanded drop by only about half a percent. The same estimate works for small changes other than 1%: if \(E = 2\) and the price rises 3%, the quantity drops by about \(2\cdot 3\% = 6\%\).

Elastic, inelastic, and unit elasticity

Value of \(E\)NameMeaning in words
\(E \lt 1\)inelasticThe relative change in demand is less than the relative change in price. A small change in price has little effect on demand.
\(E \gt 1\)elasticThe relative change in demand is larger than the relative change in price. A small change in price has a bigger effect on demand.
\(E = 1\)unit elasticityThe relative change in demand and the relative change in price are equal.

In plain words: compare \(E\) with the number 1. Below 1, customers barely react to the price. Above 1, customers react a lot. Exactly 1 is the dividing line.

A product people need, like insulin or gasoline, tends to have inelastic demand: customers keep buying even when the price goes up. A product with many substitutes, like one brand of soda, tends to have elastic demand: a price increase sends customers elsewhere.

Two edge cases from the textbook exercises. If \(E = 0\) at every price, then \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = 0\), so the demand is constant: customers buy the same amount no matter the price. A Giffen good is a product whose demand increases with price. Then \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} \gt 0\), so \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) is negative. Every demand function in the course problems decreases with price, so in practice your \(E\) should come out positive.

How to find the elasticity of demand
  1. Make sure the demand is written as \(q\) in terms of \(p\). (If it is given as \(p\) in terms of \(q\), or as an equation mixing both, see Worked example 5.)
  2. Differentiate to get \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) as a formula in \(p\). Do this before plugging in any number for \(p\).
  3. Substitute \(q\) and \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) into \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) and simplify. Two minus signs multiply to a plus.
  4. If a price is given, substitute it now and compare \(E\) with 1.
Worked example 1 (linear demand)

The demand for a product is \(q = 600 - 3p\), for \(0 \le p \le 200\). Find the elasticity of demand as a function of \(p\). Then find \(E\) at \(p = 50\) and at \(p = 150\), say whether demand is elastic or inelastic at each price, and interpret each number.

How to spot this type: the demand is given as \(q =\) (something in \(p\)) and the problem says "find the elasticity" and "elastic or inelastic", so you use the definition of \(E\) directly and compare with 1.

  1. Differentiate the demand function. The derivative of 600 is 0 and the derivative of \(-3p\) is \(-3\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -3\]
  2. Substitute \(q = 600 - 3p\) and \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -3\) into the definition of \(E\). The two minus signs multiply to a plus. \[E = -\frac{p}{600 - 3p}\cdot(-3) = \frac{3p}{600 - 3p}\]
  3. Simplify by factoring 3 out of the denominator and canceling it with the 3 in the numerator. \[E = \frac{3p}{3(200 - p)} = \frac{p}{200 - p}\]
  4. Evaluate at \(p = 50\). The result is less than 1, so demand is inelastic at this price. \[E(50) = \frac{50}{200 - 50} = \frac{50}{150} = \frac{1}{3}\] Interpretation: at a price of \$50, a 1% increase in price makes the quantity demanded drop by about \(\tfrac{1}{3}\%\).
  5. Evaluate at \(p = 150\). The result is greater than 1, so demand is elastic at this price. \[E(150) = \frac{150}{200 - 150} = \frac{150}{50} = 3\] Interpretation: at a price of \$150, a 1% increase in price makes the quantity demanded drop by about 3%.

Answer: \(E = \dfrac{p}{200 - p}\). At \(p = 50\), \(E = \tfrac{1}{3}\) (inelastic). At \(p = 150\), \(E = 3\) (elastic).

Common mistake. Forgetting the minus sign in the definition and getting a negative elasticity. If your \(E\) is negative for an ordinary product, you dropped the minus sign in front or the minus sign of \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\).

Elasticity and revenue

The reason businesses care about elasticity is revenue. The revenue is the price per unit times the number of units sold, \(R = pq\). Raising the price has two effects that pull in opposite directions. Each unit brings in more money, but fewer units are sold. Elasticity tells you which effect wins.

Here is the derivation the professor did in lecture. Think of \(q = f(p)\) as a function of \(p\), so \(R = p\,f(p)\).

  1. Differentiate \(R = p\cdot q\) with respect to \(p\) using the product rule, with \(u = p\), \(v = q\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = \dfrac{dq}{dp}\). \[\frac{dR}{dp} = u'v + uv' = 1\cdot q + p\,\frac{dq}{dp} = q + p\,\frac{dq}{dp}\]
  2. Multiply and divide the second term by \(q\), so that the combination \(\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) appears. (Multiplying by \(\dfrac{q}{q} = 1\) changes nothing.) \[\frac{dR}{dp} = q + q\cdot\frac{p}{q}\cdot\frac{dq}{dp}\]
  3. By the definition of elasticity, \(\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -E\). Substitute that in and factor out \(q\). \[\frac{dR}{dp} = q + q(-E) = q - qE = q(1 - E)\]
Revenue and elasticity
\[\frac{dR}{dp} = q\,(1 - E)\]

The quantity \(q\) is positive, so the sign of \(\dfrac{dR}{dp}\) is the sign of \(1 - E\).

  • If the demand is inelastic (\(E \lt 1\)), then \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} \gt 0\), so revenue increases as the price increases. Raising the price raises revenue.
  • If the demand is elastic (\(E \gt 1\)), then \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} \lt 0\), so revenue decreases as the price increases. Lowering the price raises revenue.
  • At the price where the demand has unit elasticity (\(E = 1\)), \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = 0\). This is where revenue is largest.

In plain words: if customers barely react (inelastic), charge more and you take in more money. If customers react strongly (elastic), charge less and you take in more money. The best price for revenue is the one where \(E\) equals exactly 1.

A way to remember it: inelastic means customers barely react, so you can charge more and keep almost all of them. Elastic means customers react strongly, so a lower price brings in enough extra customers to make up for it.

For the demand in Worked example 1, revenue is \(R = p(600 - 3p) = 600p - 3p^2\), so \(R'(p) = 600 - 6p\). The formula \(q(1 - E)\) gives the same thing. Write \(600 - 3p = 3(200 - p)\) and put \(1 - E\) over the common denominator \(200 - p\):

\[\begin{aligned} q(1 - E) &= (600 - 3p)\left(1 - \frac{p}{200 - p}\right) \\ &= 3(200 - p)\cdot\frac{(200 - p) - p}{200 - p} \\ &= 3(200 - 2p) = 600 - 6p. \end{aligned}\]

Unit elasticity happens where \(\dfrac{p}{200 - p} = 1\), that is \(p = 200 - p\), so \(2p = 200\) and \(p = 100\). The revenue is largest there: \(R(100) = 100(600 - 300) = 100\cdot 300 = 30000\) dollars. The graph shows revenue rising while demand is inelastic and falling once demand is elastic.

You can always find the price that maximizes revenue without elasticity too. Write \(R = pq\) as a function of \(p\) and maximize it with the methods of Sections 6.1 and 6.2. The professor asked exactly this in lecture. Both ways give the same price.

A shortcut for straight-line demand. If \(q = m - np\) (with \(m, n \gt 0\)), then \(E = \dfrac{np}{m - np}\). At the price \(p = \dfrac{m}{2n}\), the top is \(np = \dfrac{m}{2}\) and the bottom is \(m - \dfrac{m}{2} = \dfrac{m}{2}\), so \(E = 1\). That price is halfway between \(p = 0\) and the price \(\dfrac{m}{n}\) where demand hits zero. So for a straight-line demand, unit elasticity is always at the midpoint between the two intercepts (Homework 6.3 #11). In Worked example 1, demand hits zero at \(p = 200\), and the midpoint is \(p = 100\).

How to answer "should the price be increased or decreased to increase revenue?"
  1. Find \(E\) as a function of \(p\).
  2. Substitute the given price and compute the number \(E\).
  3. Compare \(E\) with 1 and name the case (elastic or inelastic).
  4. Connect to revenue with \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\): state its sign and what that means.
  5. Answer the question: increase the price if \(E \lt 1\), decrease it if \(E \gt 1\).
What to write on the exam. For an "explain" part, write five things in full sentences: the number, the comparison with 1, the name of the case, the revenue fact, and the decision. For example: "At \(p = 5\), \(E = \tfrac{50}{23}\), which is greater than 1, so demand is elastic at this price. Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) and \(q \gt 0\), \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} \lt 0\) when \(E \gt 1\), so revenue decreases as the price increases. Therefore the price should be decreased in order to increase revenue." Writing only "decrease" without the reason will not get full credit, because the exam says "Explain."
Worked example 2 (quadratic demand, exam style)

The demand for a product is \(q = 48 - p^2\), where \(p\) is the price in dollars. Recall that the elasticity of demand is \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). (a) At what price does the demand have unit elasticity? (b) If the price is \$5, should it be increased or decreased in order to increase revenue? Explain. (c) Same question if the price is \$3.

How to spot this type: the words "unit elasticity" mean solve \(E = 1\), and "increased or decreased in order to increase revenue" means find \(E\) at that price and use the sign of \(q(1 - E)\). This is the exact shape of the Fall 2025 exam question.

  1. State the domain. The quantity must be positive, so \(48 - p^2 \gt 0\), which means \(p^2 \lt 48\) and \(0 \lt p \lt \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt{3}\).
  2. Differentiate the demand function with the power rule. \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -2p\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). The two minus signs multiply to a plus. \[E = -\frac{p}{48 - p^2}\cdot(-2p) = \frac{2p^2}{48 - p^2}\]
  4. (a) Set \(E = 1\) and multiply both sides by \(48 - p^2\), which is positive on the domain. \[\frac{2p^2}{48 - p^2} = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2p^2 = 48 - p^2\]
  5. Add \(p^2\) to both sides, divide by 3, and take the positive square root (a price is positive). \[3p^2 = 48 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^2 = 16 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = 4\] The price \(p = 4\) is in the domain, since \(q(4) = 48 - 16 = 32 \gt 0\).
  6. (b) Substitute \(p = 5\). First \(q = 48 - 25 = 23\), then \[E(5) = \frac{2(5)^2}{48 - 5^2} = \frac{50}{23}\] Since \(50 \gt 23\), \(E(5) \gt 1\), so demand is elastic at \(p = 5\).
  7. Connect to revenue. Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\), at \(p = 5\) \[\frac{dR}{dp} = 23\left(1 - \frac{50}{23}\right) = 23 - 50 = -27 \lt 0,\] so revenue decreases as the price increases. To increase revenue, decrease the price.
  8. (c) Substitute \(p = 3\). First \(q = 48 - 9 = 39\), then \[E(3) = \frac{2(3)^2}{48 - 3^2} = \frac{18}{39} = \frac{6}{13}\] Since \(6 \lt 13\), \(E(3) \lt 1\), so demand is inelastic at \(p = 3\).
  9. Connect to revenue. At \(p = 3\), using \(\tfrac{39}{13} = 3\), \[\frac{dR}{dp} = 39\left(1 - \frac{6}{13}\right) = 39\cdot\frac{7}{13} = 3\cdot 7 = 21 \gt 0,\] so revenue increases as the price increases. To increase revenue, increase the price.
  10. Double check with the revenue function directly. \(R = p(48 - p^2) = 48p - p^3\), so \(R'(p) = 48 - 3p^2\). Then \(R'(5) = 48 - 75 = -27\) and \(R'(3) = 48 - 27 = 21\), matching the two steps above.

Answer: (a) \(p = 4\) dollars. (b) At \$5, \(E = \tfrac{50}{23} \gt 1\), demand is elastic, so \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \lt 0\): decrease the price. (c) At \$3, \(E = \tfrac{6}{13} \lt 1\), demand is inelastic, so \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} \gt 0\): increase the price.

Common mistake. Getting the direction backwards. Elastic (\(E \gt 1\)) means lower the price to raise revenue. Inelastic (\(E \lt 1\)) means raise the price. If you forget, rederive \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) in three lines; that settles it.
Common mistake. Plugging the price in before differentiating. If you substitute \(p = 5\) into \(q = 48 - p^2\) first, you get the constant 23, whose derivative is 0, and \(E\) comes out 0. Always find \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) as a formula first.
Worked example 3 (exponential demand)

The demand for a product is \(q = 400e^{-p/20}\). Find \(E\). For which prices is demand elastic, and for which is it inelastic? At what price is revenue maximized, and what is the maximum revenue?

How to spot this type: an \(e\) to a power of \(p\) in the demand means the chain rule for \(e^{g(p)}\), and the exponential always cancels in \(E\). "For which prices" means solve \(E \lt 1\) and \(E \gt 1\).

  1. Differentiate with the rule \(\dfrac{d}{dp}e^{g(p)} = e^{g(p)}\,g'(p)\), where \(g(p) = -\dfrac{p}{20}\) and \(g'(p) = -\dfrac{1}{20}\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = 400e^{-p/20}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{20}\right) = -20e^{-p/20}\]
  2. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). The factor \(e^{-p/20}\) cancels. \[E = -\frac{p}{400e^{-p/20}}\cdot\left(-20e^{-p/20}\right) = \frac{20p\,e^{-p/20}}{400e^{-p/20}} = \frac{20p}{400} = \frac{p}{20}\]
  3. Compare with 1. \(E \lt 1\) means \(\dfrac{p}{20} \lt 1\), that is \(p \lt 20\). \(E \gt 1\) means \(p \gt 20\). So demand is inelastic for \(0 \lt p \lt 20\) and elastic for \(p \gt 20\).
  4. Revenue is maximized at unit elasticity. Set \(E = 1\). \[\frac{p}{20} = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = 20\] Justification: \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) is positive for \(p \lt 20\) (inelastic) and negative for \(p \gt 20\) (elastic), so by the first derivative test revenue has its largest value at \(p = 20\).
  5. Compute the maximum revenue. At \(p = 20\), \(q = 400e^{-20/20} = 400e^{-1} = \dfrac{400}{e}\), so \[R = pq = 20\cdot\frac{400}{e} = \frac{8000}{e}\]

Answer: \(E = \dfrac{p}{20}\). Inelastic for \(0 \lt p \lt 20\), elastic for \(p \gt 20\). Revenue is maximized at \(p = 20\) dollars, with maximum revenue \(\dfrac{8000}{e}\) dollars.

Worked example 4 (power demand)

The demand for a product is \(q = \dfrac{2000}{p^2}\). Find \(E\) and explain what it says about how the price affects revenue.

How to spot this type: the demand is a constant divided by a power of \(p\). Expect every \(p\) to cancel, leaving a constant \(E\), so the answer is the same at every price.

  1. Rewrite with a negative exponent so you can use the power rule. \[q = 2000p^{-2}\]
  2. Differentiate with the power rule. \[\frac{dq}{dp} = 2000\cdot(-2)p^{-3} = -4000p^{-3} = -\frac{4000}{p^3}\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). Dividing by \(q = \dfrac{2000}{p^2}\) is the same as multiplying by \(\dfrac{p^2}{2000}\). \[E = -p\cdot\frac{p^2}{2000}\cdot\left(-\frac{4000}{p^3}\right) = \frac{4000p^3}{2000p^3} = 2\]
  4. Interpret. \(E = 2 \gt 1\) at every price, so demand is elastic at every price. Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - 2) = -q \lt 0\), revenue decreases whenever the price increases, so lowering the price always raises revenue. Check: \(R = p\cdot\dfrac{2000}{p^2} = \dfrac{2000}{p}\), and \(R'(p) = -\dfrac{2000}{p^2} = -q\), which is negative.

Answer: \(E = 2\) at every price, so demand is always elastic and lowering the price always increases revenue.

The same computation shows that a demand of the form \(q = c\,p^{-n}\) (with \(c\) and \(n\) positive constants) has constant elasticity \(E = n\). Here is the line: \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -nc\,p^{-n-1}\), so \(E = -\dfrac{p}{c\,p^{-n}}\cdot\left(-nc\,p^{-n-1}\right) = \dfrac{nc\,p^{-n}}{c\,p^{-n}} = n\). For example, \(q = \dfrac{64}{\sqrt{p}} = 64p^{-1/2}\) has \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}\) at every price. This also answers the reverse question "find a demand function whose elasticity is the constant 2": \(q = \dfrac{1}{p^2}\), or any \(q = \dfrac{c}{p^2}\).

When the demand is given as p in terms of q

Sometimes the problem gives the demand equation as \(p = D(q)\). The formula for \(E\) still needs \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). There are two ways to get it. You can solve the equation for \(q\) and differentiate as before. Or you can differentiate both sides of \(p = D(q)\) with respect to \(p\), treating \(q\) as a function of \(p\). (This is implicit differentiation, Section 6.4.) The left side gives 1, and the chain rule gives \(D'(q)\,\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) on the right, so

\[1 = D'(q)\,\frac{dq}{dp} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dp} = \frac{1}{D'(q)} = \frac{1}{dp/dq}.\]

In plain words: if you know how fast the price changes when quantity changes, flip that fraction upside down to get how fast quantity changes when price changes.

The same move works when the demand is an equation that mixes \(p\) and \(q\), such as \(q^2 + pq + 2p^2 = 23\). Differentiate every term with respect to \(p\), write \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) every time you differentiate a \(q\), and solve for \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). Your \(E\) will then contain both \(p\) and \(q\), which is fine unless the problem asks for \(E(p)\), meaning \(E\) written with \(p\) only.

Worked example 5 (demand given as p in terms of q)

The demand equation for a product is \(p = 100 - q^2\), for \(0 \lt q \lt 10\). Find the elasticity of demand when \(q = 5\), and say whether the price should be raised or lowered to increase revenue. Then find the price at which demand has unit elasticity.

How to spot this type: the equation starts with "\(p =\)" and the problem tells you how many units are sold (a value of \(q\)), so you need \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) as the reciprocal of \(\dfrac{dp}{dq}\), and the price comes from plugging \(q\) into the demand equation.

  1. Differentiate both sides of \(p = 100 - q^2\) with respect to \(p\), treating \(q\) as a function of \(p\). The chain rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dp}[q^2] = 2q\,\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). \[1 = -2q\,\frac{dq}{dp}\]
  2. Divide both sides by \(-2q\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -\frac{1}{2q}\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2q}\right) = \frac{p}{2q^2}\]
  4. Find the price when \(q = 5\), then evaluate \(E\). \[p = 100 - 5^2 = 75, \qquad E = \frac{75}{2(5)^2} = \frac{75}{50} = \frac{3}{2}\] Since \(\tfrac{3}{2} \gt 1\), demand is elastic, so \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \lt 0\). Revenue decreases as the price increases, so the price should be lowered.
  5. For unit elasticity, set \(E = 1\), which gives \(p = 2q^2\). Combine this with the demand equation \(p = 100 - q^2\). \[2q^2 = 100 - q^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 3q^2 = 100 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = \frac{100}{3}\]
  6. Find the price from \(p = 2q^2\). \[p = 2\cdot\frac{100}{3} = \frac{200}{3}\]
  7. Check by the other method. Solving \(p = 100 - q^2\) for \(q\) (with \(q \gt 0\)) gives \(q = \sqrt{100 - p} = (100 - p)^{1/2}\). The chain rule gives \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = \tfrac{1}{2}(100 - p)^{-1/2}\cdot(-1) = -\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{100 - p}}\), so \[E = -\frac{p}{\sqrt{100 - p}}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{100 - p}}\right) = \frac{p}{2(100 - p)}.\] At \(p = 75\) this is \(\dfrac{75}{2\cdot 25} = \dfrac{3}{2}\). Setting it equal to 1 gives \(p = 200 - 2p\), so \(3p = 200\) and \(p = \dfrac{200}{3}\). Both methods agree.

Answer: At \(q = 5\) (price \$75), \(E = \tfrac{3}{2}\), demand is elastic, and the price should be lowered. Unit elasticity occurs at \(p = \dfrac{200}{3}\) dollars.

Common mistake. Using \(\dfrac{dp}{dq}\) in place of \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). When the demand is \(p = 100 - q^2\), the derivative \(-2q\) is \(\dfrac{dp}{dq}\). The formula needs its reciprocal, \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -\dfrac{1}{2q}\).

Marginal revenue: the derivative with respect to quantity

Some past Berkeley exams ask for the marginal revenue instead of the elasticity. Marginal revenue is \(\dfrac{dR}{dq}\), the rate at which revenue changes per extra unit sold (Section 4.1). It is a derivative with respect to \(q\), not \(p\). Think of \(p\) as a function of \(q\) and use the product rule on \(R = pq\):

\[\frac{dR}{dq} = \frac{dp}{dq}\cdot q + p\cdot 1 = p + q\,\frac{dp}{dq}.\]

If \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} \gt 0\), selling more units raises revenue (and selling more means lowering the price). If \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} \lt 0\), selling fewer units raises revenue. This always agrees with the elasticity answer, because \(\dfrac{dp}{dq} = \dfrac{1}{dq/dp}\) turns the formula into \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} = p\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{E}\right)\), which is positive exactly when \(E \gt 1\). Level 5 has a full problem of this type.

In plain words: marginal revenue answers "if I sell one more unit, does my revenue go up or down?" A positive number means sell more; a negative number means sell fewer.

Part B. Economic lot size and economic order quantity

The economic lot size problem

A company makes \(M\) units of a product each year, and it makes them in batches (lots) of equal size. Making one huge batch means paying the setup cost only once, but then a big pile of inventory sits in storage all year. Making many small batches keeps storage low, but the setup cost is paid over and over. The batch size \(q\) that makes the total annual cost as small as possible is the economic lot size.

The model uses these letters. All of them are positive constants except \(q\).

  • \(M\) = number of units produced per year.
  • \(q\) = number of units produced in each batch (the variable).
  • \(f\) = fixed setup cost for each batch, in dollars.
  • \(g\) = manufacturing cost per unit, in dollars.
  • \(k\) = cost of storing one unit for one year, in dollars.

Number of batches. To make \(M\) units in batches of \(q\), the company runs \(\dfrac{M}{q}\) batches per year. For example, \(q = M\) means 1 batch, \(q = \dfrac{M}{2}\) means 2 batches, and \(q = \dfrac{M}{4}\) means 4 batches.

Manufacturing cost. One batch of \(q\) units costs the setup fee plus \(g\) dollars per unit, which is \(f + gq\). Multiply by the number of batches to get the annual manufacturing cost:

\[(f + gq)\cdot\frac{M}{q} = \frac{fM}{q} + \frac{gqM}{q} = \frac{fM}{q} + gM.\]

Storage cost. The model assumes demand is constant, so the inventory goes down at a constant rate (along a straight line) from \(q\) to 0, and then the next batch arrives. The picture is a sawtooth. The inventory is \(q\) at the start of each cycle and 0 at the end, so the average inventory is \(\dfrac{q}{2}\) units. Storing \(\dfrac{q}{2}\) units for a year at \(k\) dollars per unit per year costs

\[k\cdot\frac{q}{2} = \frac{kq}{2}.\]

Total cost. Add the two pieces. The professor writes \(C(q)\); the textbook writes \(T(q)\) for the same function.

Total annual cost (economic lot size and order quantity)
\[C(q) = \frac{fM}{q} + gM + \frac{kq}{2}, \qquad 0 \lt q \le M\]

The first term is the setup (or ordering) cost, the middle term is the cost of the units themselves, and the last term is the storage cost. The domain is \((0, M]\) because a batch must have a positive number of units and can be at most the whole year's production.

In plain words: yearly cost equals (number of batches times the setup fee) plus (the cost of all the units) plus (storage on half a batch, since on average the shelf is half full).

Minimizing. Differentiate with respect to \(q\). Write \(\dfrac{fM}{q} = fMq^{-1}\), whose derivative is \(-fMq^{-2}\). The term \(gM\) is a constant, so its derivative is 0. The derivative of \(\dfrac{kq}{2}\) is \(\dfrac{k}{2}\).

\[C'(q) = -\frac{fM}{q^2} + \frac{k}{2}.\]

Set \(C'(q) = 0\), move the negative term to the other side, multiply both sides by \(2q^2\), and divide by \(k\):

\[\frac{fM}{q^2} = \frac{k}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2fM = kq^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = \frac{2fM}{k} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q = \sqrt{\frac{2fM}{k}},\]

taking the positive square root because \(q\) must be positive. For the second derivative, the derivative of \(-fMq^{-2}\) is \(2fMq^{-3}\):

\[C''(q) = \frac{2fM}{q^3} \gt 0 \quad\text{for every } q \gt 0.\]

So the critical number is a local minimum by the second derivative test. It is the only critical number on the interval \((0, M]\). By the critical point theorem (one critical number on an interval, and it is a local minimum), it is the absolute minimum, as long as it lies in \((0, M]\).

Economic lot size
\[q = \sqrt{\frac{2fM}{k}} \qquad \text{(valid when this number is in } (0, M]\text{)}\]

The number of batches per year is \(\dfrac{M}{q}\).

In plain words: the best batch size is the square root of (2 times the setup fee times the yearly amount, divided by the yearly storage cost per unit). Then divide the yearly amount by the batch size to count the batches.

Two of the professor's lecture questions, with answers. First, the constant term \(gM\) is the cost of the units themselves: making (or buying) all \(M\) units at \(g\) dollars each costs \(gM\) dollars no matter how the year is split into batches. Second, that is also why \(g\) does not appear in the formula. A constant term has derivative 0, so it cannot change where the minimum is.

The economic order quantity

A store that reorders an item it sells has the same problem. Ordering a lot at once means few ordering fees but high storage. Ordering a little at a time means low storage but many fees. The number of units per order that minimizes cost is the economic order quantity. Now \(M\) is the number of units ordered per year, \(q\) is the number of units per order, \(f\) is the fixed cost of placing one order, \(g\) is the price per unit, and \(k\) is the cost of storing one unit for one year. An order costs \(f + gq\), there are \(\dfrac{M}{q}\) orders, and the item is used at a constant rate, so the average inventory is again \(\dfrac{q}{2}\). The total cost is the exact same formula,

\[C(q) = \frac{fM}{q} + gM + \frac{kq}{2},\]

so the economic order quantity is also \(q = \sqrt{\dfrac{2fM}{k}}\), when this number is in \((0, M]\).

If the formula gives a number that is not a whole number (for example \(\sqrt{9000} \approx 94.9\) bottles in Homework 6.3 #16), the order must still be a whole number of units. Since \(C\) decreases before the critical number and increases after it, the best whole number is one of the two integers on either side. Compute \(C\) at both and pick the smaller value.

How to solve a lot size or order quantity problem
  1. Identify \(M\) (units per year), \(f\) (cost per batch or per order), \(g\) (cost per unit), and \(k\) (storage cost per unit per year). If storage is quoted per month, multiply by 12.
  2. Check the assumptions. If the storage cost is charged on the average inventory, the storage term is \(\dfrac{kq}{2}\) and you may use \(q = \sqrt{2fM/k}\). If anything is different, build \(C(q)\) from its parts.
  3. Write \(C(q)\) and its domain \(0 \lt q \le M\).
  4. Find \(C'(q)\), set it equal to 0, and solve for the positive \(q\).
  5. Justify the minimum: \(C''(q) \gt 0\) for all \(q \gt 0\), and this is the only critical number in the domain. Check that it is at most \(M\).
  6. Answer in a sentence: the batch (or order) size, and the number of batches \(\dfrac{M}{q}\) if asked.
Worked example 6 (economic lot size)

A company makes 20,000 bicycle helmets per year. Setting up the factory for a batch costs \$400, each helmet costs \$3 to make, and storing one helmet for one year costs \$4. How many helmets should be made in each batch to minimize the total annual cost? How many batches should be made per year, and what is the minimum total annual cost?

How to spot this type: a yearly amount, a cost "to set up" each batch, and a cost "to store one unit for one year" are the three ingredients \(M\), \(f\), \(k\) of the lot size model, with storage on the average inventory.

  1. Identify the constants: \(M = 20000\) helmets per year, \(f = 400\) dollars per batch, \(g = 3\) dollars per helmet, \(k = 4\) dollars per helmet per year. Let \(q\) be the number of helmets per batch, with \(0 \lt q \le 20000\).
  2. Build the total annual cost. Setup: \(\dfrac{fM}{q} = \dfrac{400\cdot 20000}{q} = \dfrac{8000000}{q}\). Units: \(gM = 3\cdot 20000 = 60000\). Storage: \(\dfrac{kq}{2} = \dfrac{4q}{2} = 2q\). \[C(q) = \frac{8000000}{q} + 60000 + 2q\]
  3. Differentiate. \[C'(q) = -\frac{8000000}{q^2} + 2\]
  4. Set the derivative equal to 0 and solve, keeping the positive root. Multiply both sides by \(q^2\), then divide by 2. \[\frac{8000000}{q^2} = 2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 8000000 = 2q^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = 4000000 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q = 2000\] This agrees with the formula: \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2fM}{k}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\cdot 400\cdot 20000}{4}} = \sqrt{4000000} = 2000\). It lies in \((0, 20000]\).
  5. Justify the minimum with the second derivative. \[C''(q) = \frac{16000000}{q^3} \gt 0 \quad\text{for all } q \gt 0\] So \(q = 2000\) is a local minimum, and since it is the only critical number in \((0, 20000]\), it is the absolute minimum.
  6. Number of batches: \(\dfrac{M}{q} = \dfrac{20000}{2000} = 10\).
  7. Minimum cost: substitute \(q = 2000\). \[C(2000) = \frac{8000000}{2000} + 60000 + 2(2000) = 4000 + 60000 + 4000 = 68000\]

Answer: Make 2000 helmets per batch, in 10 batches per year. The minimum total annual cost is \$68,000.

Notice that at the optimal \(q\), the setup cost (\$4000) equals the storage cost (\$4000). That always happens with this model. The equation \(C'(q) = 0\) says \(\dfrac{fM}{q^2} = \dfrac{k}{2}\), and multiplying both sides by \(q\) gives \(\dfrac{fM}{q} = \dfrac{kq}{2}\). It makes a quick check of your arithmetic.

Worked example 7 (changed assumption: build the cost from scratch)

A grocery chain sells 4500 cases of sparkling water per year. Each order costs \$10 to place, and each case costs \$20. The chain rents warehouse space large enough to hold a full order, and the rent is \$2 per case of space per year, charged whether the space is full or empty. How many cases should be ordered each time to minimize the total annual cost? How many orders are placed per year?

How to spot this type: the words "large enough to hold a full order" and "whether the space is full or empty" mean storage is charged on the maximum inventory \(q\), not the average \(\dfrac{q}{2}\). That changes the cost function, so the usual formula is off limits.

  1. Spot the change. The storage cost is charged on the space for the maximum inventory, not the average inventory. This is the situation the professor worked in lecture (Homework 6.3 #17). The maximum inventory is a full order, \(q\) cases, so storage costs \(kq\), not \(\dfrac{kq}{2}\), and the usual formula does not apply.
  2. Define the variable and constants. Let \(q\) be the number of cases per order, with \(0 \lt q \le 4500\). Here \(M = 4500\), \(f = 10\), \(g = 20\), \(k = 2\).
  3. Build the cost. Ordering: \(\dfrac{fM}{q} = \dfrac{10\cdot 4500}{q} = \dfrac{45000}{q}\). Cases: \(gM = 20\cdot 4500 = 90000\). Storage: \(kq = 2q\). \[C(q) = \frac{45000}{q} + 90000 + 2q\]
  4. Differentiate. \[C'(q) = -\frac{45000}{q^2} + 2\]
  5. Set the derivative equal to 0 and solve, keeping the positive root. Multiply both sides by \(q^2\), then divide by 2. \[\frac{45000}{q^2} = 2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 45000 = 2q^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = 22500 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q = 150\] This is in \((0, 4500]\).
  6. Justify the minimum. \[C''(q) = \frac{90000}{q^3} \gt 0 \quad\text{for all } q \gt 0,\] so \(q = 150\) is a local minimum, and it is the only critical number in the domain, so it is the absolute minimum.
  7. Number of orders: \(\dfrac{4500}{150} = 30\).
  8. Compare with the blind formula. \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2fM}{k}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\cdot 10\cdot 4500}{2}} = \sqrt{45000} = 150\sqrt{2}\), which is wrong here. In general, with storage charged on the maximum inventory, the same steps give \(\dfrac{fM}{q^2} = k\), so \(q = \sqrt{\dfrac{fM}{k}}\). Storage is more expensive in this model, so the best order is smaller.

Answer: Order 150 cases each time, 30 times per year.

Common mistake. Using \(q = \sqrt{2fM/k}\) when the problem changes an assumption (storage on maximum inventory, an extra cost per unit of space, storage quoted per month). The formula is only valid under the assumptions used to derive it. When in doubt, build \(C(q)\) from its parts and differentiate; it takes three lines.
Common mistake. Writing the number of batches as \(\dfrac{q}{M}\) instead of \(\dfrac{M}{q}\), or using the full batch \(q\) as the average inventory instead of \(\dfrac{q}{2}\).
Common mistake. Forgetting to check that the critical number is at most \(M\). If \(\sqrt{2fM/k} \gt M\), then \(C'(q) \lt 0\) on all of \((0, M]\). The cost is decreasing there, so the best plan is one batch of size \(M\).
What to write on the exam. For the justification: "Since \(C''(q) = \dfrac{2fM}{q^3} \gt 0\) for all \(q \gt 0\), the critical number is a local minimum by the second derivative test. It is the only critical number in \((0, M]\), so it gives the absolute minimum cost." Then answer the question in a sentence with units.
If you get stuck
  • Write the definition \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) at the top of the page before anything else. Then find \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) as a formula and substitute both pieces.
  • If the equation starts with "\(p =\)" or mixes \(p\) and \(q\), differentiate every term with respect to \(p\) and solve for \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). You will get 1 on the left side of \(p = \ldots\).
  • Simplify \(E\) to one fraction and cancel common factors (an exponential, a power of \(p\), a constant) before you set it equal to 1 or plug in a price.
  • To decide raise or lower, compute the number \(E\), compare with 1, and write \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\). If you doubt the direction, compute \(R = pq\) as a function of \(p\) and check the sign of \(R'(p)\) directly.
  • For a lot size or order problem, write the three cost pieces in words first (setup or ordering, the units themselves, storage) and only then turn each into a formula in \(q\).
  • After you find \(q\), check that \(0 \lt q \le M\), and check that setup cost equals storage cost at your answer. If they are not equal (in the standard model), the arithmetic slipped.
Quick check
When the price rises 2%, the quantity demanded falls 6%. About what is \(E\), and is demand elastic?

\(E \approx \dfrac{6\%}{2\%} = 3\), which is greater than 1, so demand is elastic.

At the price where demand has unit elasticity, will raising the price a little increase revenue?

No. There \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - 1) = 0\), and this is the price of maximum revenue, so moving the price away from it in either direction does not raise revenue.

Why does the per-unit cost \(g\) not appear in \(q = \sqrt{2fM/k}\)?

It enters \(C(q)\) only as the constant \(gM\), whose derivative is 0, so it cannot move the critical number.

Can \(\sqrt{2fM/k}\) be the wrong answer even with correct arithmetic? Give an example.

Yes, when it is larger than \(M\). With \(M = 200\), \(f = 900\), \(k = 1\) it gives 600, which is outside \((0, 200]\), so the cost is decreasing on the whole domain and the best plan is one batch of 200.

For the demand \(q = \dfrac{500}{p^3}\), should a seller raise or lower the price to increase revenue?

Lower it. A demand \(c\,p^{-n}\) has \(E = n\), so \(E = 3 \gt 1\) at every price, and \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - 3) \lt 0\).

What to have memorized
  • Elasticity of demand: \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). It is about the percent drop in quantity demanded for a 1% rise in price. The minus sign makes \(E\) positive because demand decreases as price increases.
  • \(E \lt 1\) inelastic, \(E \gt 1\) elastic, \(E = 1\) unit elasticity.
  • \(R = pq\) and \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\), from the product rule. Inelastic: raise the price to raise revenue. Elastic: lower the price to raise revenue. Revenue is largest where \(E = 1\).
  • An "explain" answer names the value of \(E\), compares it with 1, names the case, cites \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) and its sign, and states the decision.
  • Demand given as \(p = D(q)\): differentiate both sides with respect to \(p\) to get \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = \dfrac{1}{dp/dq}\).
  • Power demand \(q = c\,p^{-n}\) has constant elasticity \(E = n\). Straight-line demand has unit elasticity halfway between its intercepts.
  • Marginal revenue is \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} = p + q\,\dfrac{dp}{dq}\): positive means sell more units, negative means sell fewer.
  • Lot size and order quantity: \(C(q) = \dfrac{fM}{q} + gM + \dfrac{kq}{2}\) on \((0, M]\). Number of batches \(\dfrac{M}{q}\), average inventory \(\dfrac{q}{2}\).
  • \(C'(q) = -\dfrac{fM}{q^2} + \dfrac{k}{2}\), so \(q = \sqrt{\dfrac{2fM}{k}}\); \(C''(q) = \dfrac{2fM}{q^3} \gt 0\) shows it is the minimum. The formula has no \(g\) because \(gM\) is constant.
  • If storage is charged on the maximum inventory, the storage term is \(kq\) and \(q = \sqrt{\dfrac{fM}{k}}\). Any changed assumption means rebuilding \(C(q)\).

Problem 1
The demand for a product is \(q = 120 - 4p\). Find the elasticity of demand \(E\) as a function of \(p\). Then find \(E\) at \(p = 10\) and say whether demand is elastic or inelastic there.
Show full solution
  1. Differentiate the demand function. The derivative of 120 is 0 and the derivative of \(-4p\) is \(-4\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -4\]
  2. Substitute into \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). The two minus signs multiply to a plus. \[E = -\frac{p}{120 - 4p}\cdot(-4) = \frac{4p}{120 - 4p}\]
  3. Factor 4 out of the denominator and cancel it. \[E = \frac{4p}{4(30 - p)} = \frac{p}{30 - p}\]
  4. Substitute \(p = 10\). \[E(10) = \frac{10}{30 - 10} = \frac{10}{20} = \frac{1}{2}\] Since \(\tfrac{1}{2} \lt 1\), demand is inelastic at \(p = 10\).

Answer: \(E = \dfrac{p}{30 - p}\). At \(p = 10\), \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}\), so demand is inelastic.

Problem 2
At the current price of a product, the elasticity of demand is \(E = \tfrac{3}{5}\). Is demand elastic or inelastic? If the price goes up by 1%, approximately what happens to the quantity demanded?
Show full solution
  1. Compare \(E\) with 1. Since \(\tfrac{3}{5} \lt 1\), demand is inelastic.
  2. Use the meaning of elasticity: \(E\) is approximately the percent decrease in quantity demanded caused by a 1% increase in price. Here that is \(\tfrac{3}{5}\) of one percent. \[\tfrac{3}{5}\cdot 1\% = 0.6\%\]

Answer: Demand is inelastic. A 1% price increase makes the quantity demanded drop by about 0.6%.

Problem 3
The demand for a product is \(q = \dfrac{64}{\sqrt{p}}\). Find \(E\) and say for which prices demand is elastic.
Show full solution
  1. Rewrite with an exponent so the power rule applies. \[q = 64p^{-1/2}\]
  2. Differentiate with the power rule. \[\frac{dq}{dp} = 64\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)p^{-3/2} = -32p^{-3/2}\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). Dividing by \(q = 64p^{-1/2}\) is the same as multiplying by \(\dfrac{p^{1/2}}{64}\), and \(p\cdot p^{1/2} = p^{3/2}\). \[E = -p\cdot\frac{p^{1/2}}{64}\cdot\left(-32p^{-3/2}\right) = \frac{32p^{3/2}p^{-3/2}}{64} = \frac{32}{64} = \frac{1}{2}\]
  4. Compare with 1. \(E = \tfrac{1}{2} \lt 1\) at every price, so demand is never elastic; it is inelastic at every price.

Answer: \(E = \tfrac{1}{2}\) at every price, so demand is inelastic at every price and elastic at none.

Problem 4
The demand for a product is \(q = 50e^{-p/8}\). Find \(E\), and evaluate it at \(p = 12\).
Show full solution
  1. Differentiate with the rule \(\dfrac{d}{dp}e^{g(p)} = e^{g(p)}g'(p)\), where \(g(p) = -\dfrac{p}{8}\) and \(g'(p) = -\dfrac{1}{8}\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = 50e^{-p/8}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{8}\right) = -\frac{50}{8}e^{-p/8}\]

Problem 7
The demand for a concert ticket is \(q = 2400 - 8p\), where \(p\) is the price in dollars. (a) Find \(E\) as a function of \(p\). (b) Find \(E\) at \(p = 100\) and classify the demand. (c) Use elasticity to find the price that maximizes revenue, and find the maximum revenue.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Differentiate the demand function. \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -8\]
  2. Substitute into \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\), then factor 8 out of the denominator and cancel it. \[E = -\frac{p}{2400 - 8p}\cdot(-8) = \frac{8p}{2400 - 8p} = \frac{8p}{8(300 - p)} = \frac{p}{300 - p}\]

  4. (c) Revenue is maximized where demand has unit elasticity, because \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) is 0 there. Problem 8
The demand for a product is \(q = 300 - p^2\), where \(p\) is in dollars. (a) At what price does the demand have unit elasticity? (b) If the price is \$8, should it be increased or decreased in order to increase revenue? Explain.

Answer: (a) \(p = \$10\). (b) At \$8, \(E = \tfrac{32}{59} \lt 1\), so demand is inelastic and \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \gt 0\). Revenue increases with price, so the price should be increased.

(b) (4 points) If the price is \$5, should it be increased or decreased in order to increase revenue? Explain.
Hint
Find \(E\) as one fraction, set it equal to 1, and cross multiply. For (b), compute \(E(5)\) and use \(\frac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\).
Show full solution
  1. State the domain. The quantity must be positive, so \(108 - p^2 \gt 0\), which means \(0 \lt p \lt \sqrt{108} = 6\sqrt{3}\).
  2. Differentiate the demand function with the power rule. \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -2p\]
  3. Substitute \(q = 108 - p^2\) and \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -2p\) into the definition. The two minus signs multiply to a plus. \[E = -\frac{p}{108 - p^2}\cdot(-2p) = \frac{2p^2}{108 - p^2}\]
  4. (a) Unit elasticity means \(E = 1\). Multiply both sides by \(108 - p^2\), which is positive on the domain. \[\frac{2p^2}{108 - p^2} = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2p^2 = 108 - p^2\]
  5. Add \(p^2\) to both sides, divide by 3, and take the positive square root, since a price is positive. \[3p^2 = 108 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^2 = 36 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = 6\] At \(p = 6\), \(q = 108 - 36 = 72 \gt 0\), so this price is in the domain.
  6. (b) Find the quantity and the elasticity at \(p = 5\). \[q = 108 - 25 = 83, \qquad E(5) = \frac{2(5)^2}{108 - 5^2} = \frac{50}{83}\] Since \(50 \lt 83\), \(E(5) \lt 1\), so the demand is inelastic at \(p = 5\).
  7. Explain with revenue. Revenue is \(R = pq\). By the product rule, \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q + p\,\dfrac{dq}{dp} = q\left(1 + \dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\right) = q(1 - E)\). At \(p = 5\), \[\frac{dR}{dp} = 83\left(1 - \frac{50}{83}\right) = 83 - 50 = 33 \gt 0.\] So revenue increases as the price increases.
  8. Check directly: \(R = p(108 - p^2) = 108p - p^3\), so \(R'(p) = 108 - 3p^2\) and \(R'(5) = 108 - 75 = 33\), the same value.

Answer: (a) \(p = \$6\). (b) At \$5, \(E = \tfrac{50}{83} \lt 1\), so demand is inelastic. Since \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \gt 0\) when \(E \lt 1\), revenue increases as the price increases, so the price should be increased.

Where students lose points: Writing only "increase" without the reason (\(E \lt 1\), so \(\frac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \gt 0\)), which loses most of part (b). Also plugging \(p = 5\) into \(q\) before differentiating, which makes \(E\) come out 0.

Problem 19
The demand for a product is \(q = 30e^{-p^2/50}\), where \(p\) is the price in dollars.
(a) Find the elasticity of demand \(E\) as a function of \(p\).
(b) At what price does the demand have unit elasticity?
(c) If the price is \$6, should it be increased or decreased in order to increase revenue? Explain.
(d) What is the maximum revenue?
Hint
Use the chain rule on \(e^{-p^2/50}\); the exponential cancels in \(E\). The maximum revenue happens at the price you found in (b).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Differentiate with the rule \(\dfrac{d}{dp}e^{g(p)} = e^{g(p)}g'(p)\), where \(g(p) = -\dfrac{p^2}{50}\) and \(g'(p) = -\dfrac{2p}{50} = -\dfrac{p}{25}\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = 30e^{-p^2/50}\cdot\left(-\frac{p}{25}\right) = -\frac{30p}{25}e^{-p^2/50} = -\frac{6p}{5}e^{-p^2/50}\]
  2. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). The factor \(e^{-p^2/50}\) cancels. \[E = -\frac{p}{30e^{-p^2/50}}\cdot\left(-\frac{6p}{5}e^{-p^2/50}\right) = \frac{6p^2\,e^{-p^2/50}}{5\cdot 30\,e^{-p^2/50}} = \frac{6p^2}{150} = \frac{p^2}{25}\]
  3. (b) Set \(E = 1\) and take the positive root. \[\frac{p^2}{25} = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^2 = 25 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = 5\]
  4. (c) Substitute \(p = 6\). \[E(6) = \frac{36}{25}\] Since \(36 \gt 25\), \(E(6) \gt 1\), so demand is elastic at \(p = 6\).
  5. Explain with revenue. Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) and \(q \gt 0\), at \(p = 6\) \[\frac{dR}{dp} = q\left(1 - \frac{36}{25}\right) = -\frac{11}{25}\,q \lt 0,\] so revenue decreases as the price increases. To increase revenue, decrease the price.
  6. (d) For \(0 \lt p \lt 5\), \(E = \dfrac{p^2}{25} \lt 1\) and \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} \gt 0\). For \(p \gt 5\), \(E \gt 1\) and \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} \lt 0\). By the first derivative test, revenue has its absolute maximum at \(p = 5\).
  7. Compute the revenue at \(p = 5\). First \(q = 30e^{-25/50} = 30e^{-1/2}\). Then \[R = pq = 5\cdot 30e^{-1/2} = 150e^{-1/2} = \frac{150}{\sqrt{e}}\]

Answer: (a) \(E = \dfrac{p^2}{25}\). (b) \(p = \$5\). (c) At \$6, \(E = \tfrac{36}{25} \gt 1\), demand is elastic, \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \lt 0\), so decrease the price. (d) The maximum revenue is \(\dfrac{150}{\sqrt{e}}\) dollars, at \(p = 5\).

Where students lose points: Dropping the inside derivative \(-\frac{p}{25}\) of the exponent. Also giving the price 5 as the maximum revenue, when the question wants \(R = pq = \frac{150}{\sqrt{e}}\).

Problem 20
A company manufactures 6000 electric scooters per year, in batches of equal size. Each batch has a setup cost of \$45, and each scooter costs \$8 to make (not counting the setup). Scooters sell at a constant rate. Storage costs \$2 per scooter per year on the average inventory. In addition, the company pays insurance on its storage space, which must hold a full batch: \$2 per year for each scooter of maximum inventory. Find the batch size that minimizes the total annual cost and explain why your answer gives the smallest possible cost. How many batches are made per year, and what is the minimum cost?
Hint
There are two storage-type costs here: one charged on the average inventory \(\frac{q}{2}\) and one charged on the maximum inventory \(q\). Write every cost piece before differentiating.
Show full solution
  1. Define the variable and its domain. Let \(q\) be the number of scooters per batch, with \(0 \lt q \le 6000\). The constants are \(M = 6000\), \(f = 45\), \(g = 8\).
  2. Manufacturing cost. There are \(\dfrac{6000}{q}\) batches per year, each costing \(45 + 8q\). \[(45 + 8q)\cdot\frac{6000}{q} = \frac{270000}{q} + 48000\]
  3. Storage cost. The average inventory is \(\dfrac{q}{2}\), at \$2 per scooter per year. \[2\cdot\frac{q}{2} = q\]
  4. Insurance cost. The maximum inventory is a full batch, \(q\) scooters, at \$2 each per year. \[2\cdot q = 2q\]
  5. Add the pieces to get the total annual cost. \[C(q) = \frac{270000}{q} + 48000 + q + 2q = \frac{270000}{q} + 48000 + 3q\]
  6. Differentiate. \[C'(q) = -\frac{270000}{q^2} + 3\]
  7. Set the derivative equal to 0 and solve, keeping the positive root since \(q \gt 0\). \[\frac{270000}{q^2} = 3 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = 90000 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q = 300\] This is in \((0, 6000]\).
  8. Explain why this is the smallest cost. The second derivative is \[C''(q) = \frac{540000}{q^3} \gt 0 \quad\text{for all } q \gt 0,\] so \(q = 300\) is a local minimum by the second derivative test. It is the only critical number in \((0, 6000]\), so it gives the absolute minimum cost.
  9. Number of batches: \(\dfrac{6000}{300} = 20\).
  10. Minimum cost. \[C(300) = \frac{270000}{300} + 48000 + 3(300) = 900 + 48000 + 900 = 49800\]
  11. Note that the textbook formula would give \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2\cdot 45\cdot 6000}{2}} = \sqrt{270000} = 300\sqrt{3}\), which is wrong here because the insurance term changes the cost function.

Answer: Make 300 scooters per batch, 20 batches per year. Since \(C''(q) \gt 0\) and \(q = 300\) is the only critical number in \((0, 6000]\), it gives the absolute minimum total annual cost, \$49,800.

Where students lose points: Quoting the textbook formula and getting \(300\sqrt{3}\), or forgetting the insurance term (or charging it on \(\frac{q}{2}\) instead of \(q\)). Also skipping the reason why \(C'' \gt 0\) with one critical number gives the absolute minimum.

Problem 21
A company sells \(q\) thousand units of a product per month when the price is \(p\) dollars per unit, where \(p = 64 - q^2\) for \(0 \lt q \lt 8\).
(a) Find the elasticity of demand \(E\) in terms of \(p\) and \(q\).
(b) The company currently sells 4 thousand units per month. Should it raise or lower the price to increase revenue? Explain.
(c) Find the price that maximizes revenue.
Hint
Differentiate both sides of \(p = 64 - q^2\) with respect to \(p\) to get \(\frac{dq}{dp}\). For (c), combine \(E = 1\) with the demand equation.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Differentiate both sides of \(p = 64 - q^2\) with respect to \(p\), treating \(q\) as a function of \(p\). The chain rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dp}[q^2] = 2q\,\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). \[1 = -2q\,\frac{dq}{dp}\]
  2. Divide both sides by \(-2q\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -\frac{1}{2q}\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2q}\right) = \frac{p}{2q^2}\]
  4. (b) At \(q = 4\), the price is \(p = 64 - 16 = 48\). Then \[E = \frac{48}{2(4)^2} = \frac{48}{32} = \frac{3}{2}\] Since \(\tfrac{3}{2} \gt 1\), demand is elastic.
  5. Explain with revenue. Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\) and \(q \gt 0\), when \(E \gt 1\) we get \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} \lt 0\): revenue decreases as the price increases. So the company should lower the price.
  6. (c) Revenue is maximized where \(E = 1\). Setting \(\dfrac{p}{2q^2} = 1\) gives \(p = 2q^2\). Combine with the demand equation. \[2q^2 = 64 - q^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 3q^2 = 64 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = \frac{64}{3}\]
  7. Find the price. \[p = 2q^2 = 2\cdot\frac{64}{3} = \frac{128}{3}\]
  8. Justify that this is the maximum. Write revenue in terms of \(q\): \(R = pq = (64 - q^2)q = 64q - q^3\) (in thousands of dollars), so \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} = 64 - 3q^2\), which is 0 at \(q^2 = \dfrac{64}{3}\), the same point. Also \(\dfrac{d^2R}{dq^2} = -6q \lt 0\) for \(q \gt 0\), so it is a local maximum, and as the only critical number in \((0, 8)\) it is the absolute maximum.

Answer: (a) \(E = \dfrac{p}{2q^2}\). (b) At \(q = 4\) (price \$48), \(E = \tfrac{3}{2} \gt 1\), demand is elastic, so \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} \lt 0\) and the company should lower the price. (c) Revenue is maximized at \(p = \dfrac{128}{3}\) dollars per unit.

Where students lose points: Treating \(\frac{dp}{dq} = -2q\) as if it were \(\frac{dq}{dp}\), which gives \(E = 2p\) (the nonsense value 96 at \(q = 4\)). Also answering with the quantity \(q^2 = \frac{64}{3}\) when part (c) asks for the price.

Problem 22
Answer each part and give a one-line reason.
(a) When the price of a product rises 5%, the quantity demanded falls 10%. Estimate \(E\). Is demand elastic or inelastic?
(b) At the current price, \(E = \tfrac{3}{2}\). If the price rises 4%, estimate the percent change in the quantity demanded.
(c) True or false: if demand has unit elasticity at the current price, raising the price a little will increase revenue.
(d) What must be true about the demand function if \(E = 0\) at every price?
(e) A Giffen good is a product whose demand increases when its price increases. What is true about its elasticity?
(f) Explain why the cost per unit \(g\) does not appear in the economic lot size formula \(q = \sqrt{2fM/k}\).
Hint
Every part comes straight from the definition \(E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\frac{dq}{dp}\), its meaning as a ratio of percent changes, or the formula \(\frac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Elasticity is approximately the percent drop in quantity divided by the percent rise in price. \[E \approx \frac{10\%}{5\%} = 2\] Since \(2 \gt 1\), demand is elastic.
  2. (b) The percent drop in quantity is approximately \(E\) times the percent rise in price. \[\frac{3}{2}\cdot 4\% = 6\%\] So the quantity demanded falls by about 6%.
  3. (c) At unit elasticity, \(E = 1\), so \[\frac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) = q(1 - 1) = 0.\] This is the price where revenue is largest, so a price increase cannot raise revenue. The statement is false.
  4. (d) If \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp} = 0\) and \(p, q \gt 0\), then \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = 0\) at every price. So the demand is constant: customers buy the same amount at every price. (Then \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - 0) = q \gt 0\), so raising the price always raises revenue.)
  5. (e) For a Giffen good \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} \gt 0\). Since \(p \gt 0\) and \(q \gt 0\), the factor \(-\dfrac{p}{q}\) is negative, so \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\frac{dq}{dp} \lt 0.\] The elasticity of a Giffen good is negative.
  6. (f) In \(C(q) = \dfrac{fM}{q} + gM + \dfrac{kq}{2}\), the cost \(g\) appears only in the term \(gM\), the cost of making all \(M\) units. That term does not depend on \(q\), so its derivative is 0 and it drops out of \[C'(q) = -\frac{fM}{q^2} + \frac{k}{2}.\] Since the critical number comes from \(C'(q) = 0\), \(g\) cannot affect it.

Answer: (a) \(E \approx 2\), elastic. (b) The quantity falls by about 6%. (c) False: \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = 0\) there and revenue is at its maximum. (d) Demand is constant (\(\tfrac{dq}{dp} = 0\)). (e) \(E \lt 0\). (f) \(g\) only appears in the constant \(gM\), whose derivative is 0.

Problem 23
The graph shows a straight-line demand curve \(q = f(p)\) for a product, with the price \(p\) in dollars on the horizontal axis and the quantity \(q\) on the vertical axis.
(a) Use the two intercepts to write the demand function.
(b) Without computing \(E\), use the midpoint fact for straight-line demand to find the price of unit elasticity.
(c) Confirm part (b) with the formula for \(E\).
(d) The current price is \$20. Should it be raised or lowered to increase revenue? Explain.

A straight line through \((0, b)\) and \((a, 0)\) has slope \(-\frac{b}{a}\). For any straight-line demand, unit elasticity sits at the price halfway between the two intercepts.
Show full solution
  1. (a) The line passes through \((0, 90)\) and \((30, 0)\). Its slope is the change in \(q\) over the change in \(p\). \[\text{slope} = \frac{0 - 90}{30 - 0} = -3\] The vertical intercept is 90, so \[q = 90 - 3p, \qquad 0 \le p \le 30.\]
  2. (b) For a straight-line demand \(q = m - np\), unit elasticity is at the midpoint between the intercepts. The price intercept is \(p = 30\), so the midpoint price is \[p = \frac{0 + 30}{2} = 15,\] with quantity \(q = 90 - 3(15) = 45\), which is halfway between 90 and 0.
  3. (c) Differentiate: \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -3\). Substitute into the definition and cancel the common factor 3. \[E = -\frac{p}{90 - 3p}\cdot(-3) = \frac{3p}{90 - 3p} = \frac{3p}{3(30 - p)} = \frac{p}{30 - p}\] At \(p = 15\): \[E(15) = \frac{15}{30 - 15} = \frac{15}{15} = 1.\] This confirms part (b).

Answer: (a) \(q = 90 - 3p\). (b) and (c) Unit elasticity at \(p = \$15\) (where \(E = \tfrac{15}{15} = 1\)). (d) At \$20, \(E = 2 \gt 1\), demand is elastic and \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \lt 0\), so the price should be lowered.

Problem 24
(a) The demand for a product is \(q = 540 - kp^2\), where \(k\) is a positive constant. Demand has unit elasticity at \(p = 6\). Find \(k\), and then find the maximum revenue.
(b) Find a demand function \(q = f(p)\) whose elasticity equals 3 at every price, and for which 400 units are demanded when the price is \$2.

In (a), find \(E\) with the letter \(k\) still inside, then plug in \(p = 6\) and set it equal to 1. In (b), recall which family of demand functions has a constant elasticity.

  2. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). \[E = -\frac{p}{540 - kp^2}\cdot(-2kp) = \frac{2kp^2}{540 - kp^2}\]

Problem 25
A shop sells 120 cases of sparkling cider per year, at a constant rate. Placing an order costs \$2, and storing one case for one year costs \$5. Orders must be a whole number of cases.
(a) Find the number of cases per order that minimizes the annual cost of ordering plus storage.
(b) How many orders are placed per year, and about how often?
(c) If the ordering cost and yearly sales stayed the same, what storage cost per case per year would make the best order size exactly 12 cases?
Hint
The formula gives \(\sqrt{96}\), which is not a whole number. The cost decreases up to the critical number and increases after it, so compare the cost at the two whole numbers on either side.
Show full solution
  1. Identify the constants: \(M = 120\) cases per year, \(f = 2\) dollars per order, \(k = 5\) dollars per case per year. Let \(q\) be the number of cases per order, \(0 \lt q \le 120\).
  2. Write the annual ordering plus storage cost. Ordering: \(\dfrac{fM}{q} = \dfrac{2\cdot 120}{q} = \dfrac{240}{q}\). Storage on the average inventory: \(\dfrac{kq}{2} = \dfrac{5q}{2}\). \[C(q) = \frac{240}{q} + \frac{5q}{2}\]
  3. (a) Differentiate and set the derivative equal to 0. \[C'(q) = -\frac{240}{q^2} + \frac{5}{2} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{240}{q^2} = \frac{5}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 480 = 5q^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 = 96\]
  4. Take the positive root and simplify, using \(96 = 16\cdot 6\). \[q = \sqrt{96} = 4\sqrt{6}\] Since \(81 \lt 96 \lt 100\), this number is between 9 and 10.
  5. Decide the shape. \(C''(q) = \dfrac{480}{q^3} \gt 0\) for \(q \gt 0\), and \(4\sqrt{6}\) is the only critical number, so \(C\) decreases on \((0, 4\sqrt{6})\) and increases after it. The best whole number is therefore 9 or 10.
  6. Compare the costs at 9 and 10. Use the common denominator 6 for \(C(9)\). \[C(9) = \frac{240}{9} + \frac{45}{2} = \frac{80}{3} + \frac{45}{2} = \frac{160}{6} + \frac{135}{6} = \frac{295}{6}\] \[C(10) = \frac{240}{10} + \frac{50}{2} = 24 + 25 = 49 = \frac{294}{6}\] Since \(\tfrac{294}{6} \lt \tfrac{295}{6}\), ordering 10 cases is cheaper.

  8. (c) For the best order size to be \(q = 12\), the critical number must be 12, so \(q^2 = \dfrac{2fM}{k}\) with \(q = 12\). \[144 = \frac{2\cdot 2\cdot 120}{k} = \frac{480}{k} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 144k = 480 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k = \frac{480}{144} = \frac{10}{3}\] Check: \(C'(12) = -\dfrac{240}{144} + \dfrac{10/3}{2} = -\dfrac{5}{3} + \dfrac{5}{3} = 0\).

Problem 26
The demand equation for a product is \(p = 120e^{-q/4}\), where \(p\) is the price per unit in dollars and \(q\) is the number of units sold, \(q \gt 0\).
(a) Find the elasticity \(E\) in terms of \(q\), and then the elasticity \(E(p)\) as a function of \(p\) only.
(b) If the company is selling 3 units, should it increase or decrease the price to improve revenue? Justify your answer.
(c) For which prices is demand elastic?
(d) Find the number of units and the price at which revenue is maximized, and the maximum revenue.
Hint
Differentiate both sides with respect to \(p\) and notice that \(120e^{-q/4}\) is just \(p\) again. To get \(E(p)\), solve the demand equation for \(q\) by taking ln of both sides.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Differentiate both sides of \(p = 120e^{-q/4}\) with respect to \(p\), treating \(q\) as a function of \(p\). The chain rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dp}e^{-q/4} = e^{-q/4}\cdot\left(-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). \[1 = 120e^{-q/4}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{dq}{dp}\]
  2. Replace \(120e^{-q/4}\) by \(p\), since that is the demand equation, and solve for \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\). \[1 = -\frac{p}{4}\cdot\frac{dq}{dp} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dp} = -\frac{4}{p}\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). The \(p\) cancels. \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\left(-\frac{4}{p}\right) = \frac{4}{q}\]
  4. Solve the demand equation for \(q\). Divide by 120, take ln of both sides, and multiply by \(-4\). \[\frac{p}{120} = e^{-q/4} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{p}{120} = -\frac{q}{4} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q = -4\ln\frac{p}{120} = 4\ln\frac{120}{p}\] Here \(0 \lt p \lt 120\), because \(q \gt 0\) needs \(\tfrac{120}{p} \gt 1\).
  5. Substitute into \(E = \dfrac{4}{q}\). \[E(p) = \frac{4}{4\ln(120/p)} = \frac{1}{\ln(120/p)}\]
  6. (b) At \(q = 3\): \[E = \frac{4}{3} \gt 1,\] so demand is elastic. Then \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) = 3\left(1 - \dfrac{4}{3}\right) = -1 \lt 0\), so revenue decreases as the price increases. The company should decrease the price.
  7. (c) Demand is elastic when \(E \gt 1\), that is \(\dfrac{4}{q} \gt 1\), which means \(q \lt 4\). Convert to prices. Since \(q = 4\ln(120/p)\), \[4\ln\frac{120}{p} \lt 4 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{120}{p} \lt 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{120}{p} \lt e \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p \gt \frac{120}{e}.\] So demand is elastic for \(\dfrac{120}{e} \lt p \lt 120\) (and inelastic for \(0 \lt p \lt \dfrac{120}{e}\)).
  8. (d) Revenue is maximized at unit elasticity: \(\dfrac{4}{q} = 1\) gives \(q = 4\), and the price is \[p = 120e^{-4/4} = 120e^{-1} = \frac{120}{e}.\]
  9. Justify the maximum with revenue as a function of \(q\). \(R(q) = pq = 120q\,e^{-q/4}\). By the product rule with \(u = 120q\), \(v = e^{-q/4}\), \(u' = 120\), \(v' = -\tfrac{1}{4}e^{-q/4}\): \[R'(q) = 120e^{-q/4} - 30q\,e^{-q/4} = 30e^{-q/4}(4 - q).\] This is positive for \(q \lt 4\) and negative for \(q \gt 4\), so by the first derivative test \(q = 4\) gives the absolute maximum.
  10. Compute the maximum revenue. \[R = pq = \frac{120}{e}\cdot 4 = \frac{480}{e}\]

Answer: (a) \(E = \dfrac{4}{q}\), so \(E(p) = \dfrac{1}{\ln(120/p)}\) for \(0 \lt p \lt 120\). (b) At 3 units \(E = \tfrac{4}{3} \gt 1\), so \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} \lt 0\): decrease the price. (c) Elastic for \(\tfrac{120}{e} \lt p \lt 120\). (d) Revenue is maximized at \(q = 4\) units and \(p = \tfrac{120}{e}\) dollars, with maximum revenue \(\tfrac{480}{e}\) dollars.

Problem 27
A company is selling a product. The demand equation is \(q = 162 - 2p^2\), where \(p\) is the price per unit and \(q\) is the number of units sold.
(a) Determine the marginal revenue \(\dfrac{dR}{dq}\) when the number of units sold is 90.
(b) Should the company aim to sell more or fewer units to increase revenue? Justify your answer, and check it against the elasticity at that point.
(c) Find the marginal revenue when the price is \$8.
Hint
Marginal revenue is the derivative of \(R = pq\) with respect to \(q\), so treat \(p\) as the function: \(\frac{dR}{dq} = p + q\,\frac{dp}{dq}\). Get \(\frac{dp}{dq}\) as the reciprocal of \(\frac{dq}{dp}\).
Show full solution
  1. (a) Write revenue as \(R = pq\) and differentiate with respect to \(q\) by the product rule, with \(p\) a function of \(q\). \[\frac{dR}{dq} = \frac{dp}{dq}\cdot q + p\cdot 1 = p + q\,\frac{dp}{dq}\]
  2. Find \(\dfrac{dp}{dq}\). From \(q = 162 - 2p^2\), \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -4p\), so \[\frac{dp}{dq} = \frac{1}{dq/dp} = -\frac{1}{4p}.\] So \[\frac{dR}{dq} = p - \frac{q}{4p}.\]
  3. Find the price when 90 units are sold. Take the positive root, since a price is positive. \[90 = 162 - 2p^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2p^2 = 72 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p^2 = 36 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p = 6\]
  4. Substitute \(p = 6\), \(q = 90\). Simplify \(\tfrac{90}{24} = \tfrac{15}{4}\). \[\frac{dR}{dq} = 6 - \frac{90}{4\cdot 6} = 6 - \frac{90}{24} = 6 - \frac{15}{4} = \frac{24}{4} - \frac{15}{4} = \frac{9}{4}\]
  5. Check by writing \(R\) as a function of \(q\). Solve for \(p\): \(p = \sqrt{\dfrac{162 - q}{2}}\), so \(R(q) = q\left(\dfrac{162 - q}{2}\right)^{1/2}\). By the product and chain rules, \[R'(q) = \left(\frac{162 - q}{2}\right)^{1/2} + q\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{162 - q}{2}\right)^{-1/2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right).\] At \(q = 90\), \(\dfrac{162 - 90}{2} = 36\), so \(R'(90) = 6 - \dfrac{90}{4\cdot 6} = \dfrac{9}{4}\), the same value.
  6. (b) Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} = \dfrac{9}{4} \gt 0\), revenue increases as the number of units sold increases. The company should aim to sell more units (which means lowering the price).
  7. Check against the elasticity. With \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -4p\), \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot(-4p) = \frac{4p^2}{q} = \frac{4(36)}{90} = \frac{144}{90} = \frac{8}{5}.\] Since \(\tfrac{8}{5} \gt 1\), demand is elastic, so lowering the price raises revenue. That agrees with "sell more units." The formula \(\dfrac{dR}{dq} = p\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{E}\right) = 6\left(1 - \dfrac{5}{8}\right) = 6\cdot\dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{9}{4}\) also matches.
  8. (c) At \(p = 8\), \(q = 162 - 2(64) = 162 - 128 = 34\). Then \[\frac{dR}{dq} = 8 - \frac{34}{4\cdot 8} = 8 - \frac{34}{32} = 8 - \frac{17}{16} = \frac{128}{16} - \frac{17}{16} = \frac{111}{16}.\]

Answer: (a) The marginal revenue at 90 units is \(\tfrac{9}{4}\) dollars per unit. (b) It is positive, so the company should sell more units (lower the price); this agrees with \(E = \tfrac{8}{5} \gt 1\). (c) At \$8, the marginal revenue is \(\tfrac{111}{16}\) dollars per unit.

Problem 28
A product is being sold. The demand equation is \(q^2 + pq + 2p^2 = 23\), where \(p\) is the price per unit and \(q\) is the number of units sold.
(a) Calculate the elasticity of demand. Your answer may involve both \(p\) and \(q\).
(b) If \(q = 3\), should the price be increased or decreased to raise revenue? Explain.
Hint
Differentiate every term with respect to \(p\), writing \(\frac{dq}{dp}\) each time you differentiate something with \(q\) in it; the term \(pq\) needs the product rule. For (b), the price is not given: plug \(q = 3\) into the equation to find it.
Show full solution
  1. (a) Differentiate both sides with respect to \(p\), treating \(q\) as a function of \(p\). Term by term: \(\dfrac{d}{dp}[q^2] = 2q\,\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) (chain rule); \(\dfrac{d}{dp}[pq] = 1\cdot q + p\,\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) (product rule); \(\dfrac{d}{dp}[2p^2] = 4p\); and the constant 23 gives 0. \[2q\,\frac{dq}{dp} + q + p\,\frac{dq}{dp} + 4p = 0\]
  2. Collect the \(\dfrac{dq}{dp}\) terms on one side and factor. \[(2q + p)\frac{dq}{dp} = -(q + 4p) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dp} = -\frac{q + 4p}{2q + p}\]
  3. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). The two minus signs multiply to a plus. \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\left(-\frac{q + 4p}{2q + p}\right) = \frac{p(q + 4p)}{q(2q + p)}\]
  4. (b) Find the price when \(q = 3\) by substituting into the demand equation. \[9 + 3p + 2p^2 = 23 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2p^2 + 3p - 14 = 0\]
  5. Factor. We need two numbers with product \(2\cdot(-14) = -28\) and sum 3, namely 7 and \(-4\). \[2p^2 + 7p - 4p - 14 = p(2p + 7) - 2(2p + 7) = (2p + 7)(p - 2) = 0\] So \(p = 2\) or \(p = -\tfrac{7}{2}\). A price is positive, so \(p = 2\). Check that \((p, q) = (2, 3)\) is on the curve: \(9 + 6 + 8 = 23\).
  6. Evaluate \(E\) at \(p = 2\), \(q = 3\). \[E = \frac{2(3 + 8)}{3(6 + 2)} = \frac{22}{24} = \frac{11}{12}\] Since \(11 \lt 12\), \(E \lt 1\), so demand is inelastic. (Also \(\dfrac{dq}{dp} = -\dfrac{11}{8} \lt 0\) there, so demand is decreasing, as it should be.)
  7. Connect to revenue. Since \(\dfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E)\), \[\frac{dR}{dp} = 3\left(1 - \frac{11}{12}\right) = 3\cdot\frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{4} \gt 0,\] so revenue increases as the price increases.

Answer: (a) \(E = \dfrac{p(q + 4p)}{q(2q + p)}\). (b) At \(q = 3\) the price is \$2 and \(E = \tfrac{11}{12} \lt 1\), so demand is inelastic and \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \gt 0\). The price should be increased.

Problem 29
The supply equation for a product is \(p = q + 10\), and the demand equation is \(p = \dfrac{160}{q + 4}\), where \(p\) is the price per unit in dollars and \(q\) is the number of units.
(a) Find the equilibrium quantity and the equilibrium price.
(b) Calculate the elasticity of demand at the equilibrium.
(c) At the equilibrium price, would a small increase in price raise or lower revenue? Explain.
Hint
Equilibrium is where supply and demand give the same price, so set the two right sides equal. Elasticity of demand uses the demand equation only, never the supply equation.
Show full solution
  1. (a) At equilibrium the two prices are equal. \[q + 10 = \frac{160}{q + 4}\]
  2. Multiply both sides by \(q + 4\) and expand. \[(q + 10)(q + 4) = 160 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 + 14q + 40 = 160 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q^2 + 14q - 120 = 0\]
  3. Factor. We need two numbers with product \(-120\) and sum 14, namely 20 and \(-6\). \[(q + 20)(q - 6) = 0\] A quantity is positive, so \(q = 6\), and the price is \(p = 6 + 10 = 16\). Check with demand: \(\dfrac{160}{6 + 4} = 16\).
  4. (b) Use the demand equation. Multiply it out first so it is easy to differentiate: \(p(q + 4) = 160\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(p\), using the product rule on the left with \(u = p\), \(v = q + 4\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = \dfrac{dq}{dp}\). \[1\cdot(q + 4) + p\,\frac{dq}{dp} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dp} = -\frac{q + 4}{p}\]
  5. Substitute into the definition of \(E\). The \(p\) cancels. \[E = -\frac{p}{q}\cdot\left(-\frac{q + 4}{p}\right) = \frac{q + 4}{q}\]
  6. At the equilibrium \(q = 6\): \[E = \frac{6 + 4}{6} = \frac{10}{6} = \frac{5}{3}\]
  7. (c) Since \(\tfrac{5}{3} \gt 1\), demand is elastic at the equilibrium. Then \[\frac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) = 6\left(1 - \frac{5}{3}\right) = 6\cdot\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -4 \lt 0,\] so revenue decreases as the price increases.

Answer: (a) Equilibrium at \(q = 6\) units and \(p = \$16\). (b) \(E = \tfrac{5}{3}\). (c) Demand is elastic there, so \(\tfrac{dR}{dp} = q(1 - E) \lt 0\): a small price increase would lower revenue.
