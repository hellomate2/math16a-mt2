The two exams on this page

This page holds the two Midterm 2 exams Prof. Gomez wrote in Fall 2025, with a complete solution for every question.

  • The practice Midterm 2 is Level 1 below. He posted it with handwritten solutions. The solutions here follow his method and his layout, with every step he skipped filled in.
  • The actual Midterm 2 is Level 2 below. This is the exam he gave in class. No official solutions were posted, so the solutions here are not his. They use the methods from his lectures and the same layout as his practice key.

Every question on both exams comes from a section on this fall's Midterm 2 list (4.1 to 4.5, 13.2, 12.7, 5.1 to 5.4, 6.1 to 6.6). Read this short guide first, then take the exams under real conditions before you look at any solution.

What these exams show about how he writes questions

The cover page of both exams says the same four things: there are 5 questions, each question is worth 10 points, you must write complete, clear, legible solutions, and calculators are not allowed. Here is what the ten questions have in common.

  • Each question tests one section: a curve sketch, a differentials estimate, a chain rule derivative, an implicit derivative, an optimization problem, an elasticity problem, a related rates problem. No question mixes two big topics. The small skills (product rule, quotient rule, derivative of \(e^x\)) show up inside almost every question, so you still need all of them.
  • The question often names the method: "Use differentials to estimate", "use implicit differentiation to find". When the method is named, use exactly that method. An answer reached a different way may not show the skill being tested.
  • The question sometimes tells you what not to do. The actual exam's Question 1 says "You do NOT need to graph the function." Read every word so you do not spend time on work that earns nothing.
  • Word problems ask you to justify your answer. The actual exam's box problem says to find the dimensions "and explain why your answer gives you the smallest possible cost." Even on the practice can problem, which did not ask for it, his own solution includes a second derivative test and a sentence saying why the answer is a minimum. Treat the justification as part of every optimization answer.
  • Only one question had parts with stated points (actual Question 2: part (a) 6 points, part (b) 4 points). Part (b) asks you to "Explain", so a one-word answer is not enough there.
  • The numbers either work out by hand or the answer stays exact. On the actual exam everything came out clean: \(x^3 = 1000\) gives \(x = 10\), and \(p^2 = 25\) gives \(p = 5\). On the practice can problem the radius is \(\sqrt[3]{500/\pi}\), and his key leaves it in that form. An exact answer with a root or \(\pi\) in it is a finished answer.
  • Formulas may or may not be given. The actual exam printed the elasticity formula \(E = -\tfrac{p}{q}\cdot\tfrac{dq}{dp}\) in the question. The practice exam gave no formulas at all (you needed the volume of a sphere and the surface area of a can from memory). Memorize every formula anyway.

His handwritten practice solutions also show what he considers a complete answer.

  • For the curve sketch he makes a sign chart with one column per factor (\(3x\), \(x + 2\), then \(f'(x)\)), writes "f increasing" or "f decreasing" next to each row, and names each extremum as a point, for example "local max at \(x = -2\): \((-2, 4)\)."
  • For the can he draws the can with \(r\) and \(h\) labeled, draws the unfolded side as a rectangle, writes the function to minimize with its domain ("\(A(r) = \ldots,\ r \gt 0\)"), and then writes the second derivative test as a sentence.
  • For implicit differentiation he starts with the sentence "Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\) and remember that \(y\) is a function of \(x\)."
  • For the chain rule question he writes "Use the chain rule repeatedly" and stops at an unsimplified product of factors. He did not rewrite \(\cos/\sin\) as \(\cot\).
  • He underlines each final answer.

How to take these exams

How to use the two exams for practice
  1. Take the practice exam (Level 1) first. Set a timer for 60 minutes. Closed book, no notes, no calculator, no phone. Use blank paper and write every solution as if a grader who cannot ask you questions will read it.
  2. When the timer ends, stop. Then open the solutions and grade yourself out of 10 per question. Use the "Grading note" in each solution as a guide to where the points sit. Be strict: a step you did in your head and did not write counts as missing.
  3. For each question, write one line about what cost you points: a formula you did not remember, an algebra slip, a missing justification, a missing unit.
  4. Go to the unit for each question you missed (use the table above) and redo its Level 3 and Level 4 problems.
  5. A day or more later, take the actual exam (Level 2) the same way: 60 minutes, closed book, then grade yourself.
  6. Before Nov 17, redo any question you got less than 10 on, from a blank page, without looking at the solution.
How partial credit works on written exams

This is general advice for any written calculus exam. It does not describe his actual rubric. On a 10 point question the grader can only give points for what is on the page, so put every piece of your reasoning on the page.

  • Write the setup even if you are stuck. Draw the picture, label it, define each variable with its units, and write the equations that connect them. On a word problem the setup is often a real share of the points, and it is the part you can do without finishing.
  • Say what you are differentiating and with respect to which variable: "Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\)" or "\(C'(x) = \ldots\)". A correct derivative can earn credit even if the algebra after it goes wrong.
  • If you make an algebra mistake and do not find it, keep going with your wrong number. A grader can often give credit for correct steps that follow an earlier error, but only if those steps are written down.
  • For a max or min, name the test you used and write the sentence ("\(C''(x) \gt 0\) for all \(x \gt 0\), so ...").
  • Box or underline the final answer, and answer the question that was asked. If the question asks for dimensions, give the radius and the height, not only \(r\).
  • Keep units on every final answer: cm3, ft, cm/sec, dollars.
  • Do not spend time simplifying an answer the question does not ask you to simplify. His own key leaves the chain rule answer unsimplified.
Formulas these ten questions needed
  • Volume of a sphere: \(V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\).
  • Cylinder: volume \(V = \pi r^2 h\). Surface area of a closed can: \(A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h\) (two disks plus the side, which unrolls into a \(2\pi r\) by \(h\) rectangle).
  • Differential: \(dy = f'(x)\,dx\).
  • Elasticity of demand: \(E = -\tfrac{p}{q}\cdot\tfrac{dq}{dp}\). If \(E \gt 1\) (elastic), price and revenue move in opposite directions. If \(E \lt 1\) (inelastic), they move in the same direction. Revenue is largest where \(E = 1\).
  • Derivatives: \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\ln u = \tfrac{u'}{u}\), \(\tfrac{d}{dx}e^u = e^u u'\), \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\sin u = \cos u \cdot u'\), the product rule \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\), and the quotient rule \(\left(\tfrac{u}{v}\right)' = \tfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\).

Problem 1
(10 points) Draw a rough sketch of the graph of \(f(x) = x^3 + 3x^2\). Label every local extremum and every inflection point.
Show full solution
  1. Find the intercepts, so the sketch has fixed points to pass through. The \(y\)-intercept is \(f(0) = 0 + 0 = 0\). For the \(x\)-intercepts, set \(f(x) = 0\) and factor out \(x^2\): \[x^3 + 3x^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2(x + 3) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 0 \ \text{ or } \ x = -3.\] The graph crosses or touches the \(x\)-axis at \((-3, 0)\) and \((0, 0)\).
  2. Note the domain and the end behavior. \(f\) is a polynomial, so its domain is all real numbers and it has no asymptotes. The leading term is \(x^3\), so \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(x \to \infty\) and \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\).
  3. Find the first derivative with the power rule and factor it. \[f'(x) = 3x^2 + 6x = 3x(x + 2).\]
  4. Find the critical numbers. \(f'(x)\) is defined for every \(x\), so the critical numbers are where \(f'(x) = 0\): \[3x(x + 2) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 0 \ \text{ or } \ x = -2.\]
  5. Make a sign chart for \(f'\), one column per factor, the way the professor does. The critical numbers split the line into \((-\infty, -2)\), \((-2, 0)\), and \((0, \infty)\). Test points: \(x = -3\), \(x = -1\), \(x = 1\).

Grading note. On a sketch question, expect the points to come from the correct \(f'\) and critical numbers, the increasing and decreasing chart, \(f''\) with the concavity chart, and a graph whose labeled points match your charts. A correct-looking picture with no calculus written next to it shows almost none of that work.

Answer: Local maximum at \((-2, 4)\), local minimum at \((0, 0)\), inflection point at \((-1, 2)\). \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty, -2)\) and \((0, \infty)\) and decreasing on \((-2, 0)\); concave down on \((-\infty, -1)\) and concave up on \((-1, \infty)\); \(x\)-intercepts at \(x = -3\) and \(x = 0\).

Problem 2
(10 points) The radius of a sphere is measured to be 5 cm, and the measurement could be off by as much as 0.1 cm. Use differentials to estimate the largest possible error in the volume computed from this measurement.
Show full solution
  1. Define the variables. Let \(r\) be the radius of the sphere in cm and \(V\) its volume in cm3. The formula for the volume of a sphere is \[V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3.\]
  2. Take the differential. The differential is \(dV = V'(r)\,dr\), and by the power rule \(V'(r) = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 3r^2 = 4\pi r^2\), so \[dV = 4\pi r^2\, dr.\]
  3. Identify the numbers. The measured radius is \(r = 5\). The error in the radius is \(dr\), and its largest size is \(dr = 0.1\) (the true radius could be 0.1 cm more or less, and the size of the error is the same either way).
  4. Substitute. \[\begin{aligned} dV &= 4\pi (5)^2 (0.1) \\ &= 4\pi (25)(0.1) \\ &= 100\pi (0.1) \\ &= 10\pi. \end{aligned}\]
  5. Interpret. The volume computed from \(r = 5\) is \(\tfrac{4}{3}\pi(125) = \tfrac{500\pi}{3}\) cm3, and it could be off by about \(10\pi\) cm3. If a question also asks for the relative error, it is \[\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{10\pi}{500\pi/3} = \frac{30}{500} = \frac{3}{50} = 6\%,\] which is three times the 2% relative error in the radius (\(0.1/5 = 2\%\)). This part was not asked on the exam.
Grading note. Most of the credit here sits in two lines: the volume formula with its differential \(dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr\), and the correct substitution \(r = 5\), \(dr = 0.1\). Write the units on the answer. Computing \(V(5.1) - V(5)\) exactly instead does not use differentials, which the question asks for.

Answer: The maximum error in the volume is approximately \(dV = 10\pi\) cm3.

Problem 3
(10 points) Find the derivative of \(f(x) = \ln\!\left[\sin\!\left(e^{x^2 + x}\right)\right]\).
Show full solution
  1. Identify the layers from the outside in. The outermost function is \(\ln(\,\cdot\,)\), inside it is \(\sin(\,\cdot\,)\), inside that is \(e^{(\,\cdot\,)}\), and the innermost piece is \(x^2 + x\). The plan, as the professor writes it, is to use the chain rule repeatedly, peeling one layer at a time.
  2. Peel the \(\ln\) layer. The rule is \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\ln u = \tfrac{1}{u}\cdot u'\) with \(u = \sin(e^{x^2 + x})\): \[f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(e^{x^2 + x})}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin(e^{x^2 + x})\right].\]
  3. Peel the \(\sin\) layer. The rule is \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\sin w = \cos w \cdot w'\) with \(w = e^{x^2 + x}\): \[f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(e^{x^2 + x})}\cdot\cos(e^{x^2 + x})\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^{x^2 + x}\right].\]
  4. Peel the exponential layer. The rule is \(\tfrac{d}{dx}e^v = e^v \cdot v'\) with \(v = x^2 + x\): \[f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(e^{x^2 + x})}\cdot\cos(e^{x^2 + x})\cdot e^{x^2 + x}\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^2 + x\right].\]
  5. Differentiate the innermost piece with the power rule: \(\tfrac{d}{dx}[x^2 + x] = 2x + 1\). This is where the professor's key stops: \[f'(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(e^{x^2 + x})}\cdot\cos(e^{x^2 + x})\cdot e^{x^2 + x}\cdot(2x + 1).\]
  6. Optionally simplify. Since \(\tfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \cot\theta\), the same answer can be written \[f'(x) = (2x + 1)\,e^{x^2 + x}\cot\!\left(e^{x^2 + x}\right).\] Either form is a complete answer.
Common mistake. Stopping a layer early, for example writing \(\tfrac{1}{\sin(e^{x^2+x})}\cdot\cos(e^{x^2+x})\) and forgetting the derivative of \(e^{x^2+x}\). Another one: differentiating \(e^{x^2+x}\) as \((x^2 + x)e^{x^2 + x - 1}\). The power rule does not apply to \(e^{\text{something}}\); its derivative is \(e^{\text{something}}\) times the derivative of the something.
Grading note. On a pure chain rule question each layer is a piece of the answer. Writing one line per layer, as above, lets a grader give credit for every layer you got right even if one factor is wrong.

Answer: \(f'(x) = \dfrac{\cos(e^{x^2+x})\cdot e^{x^2+x}\cdot(2x+1)}{\sin(e^{x^2+x})} = (2x + 1)\,e^{x^2 + x}\cot\!\left(e^{x^2 + x}\right)\).

Problem 4
(10 points) The equation \(e^y \sin x = x + xy\) defines \(y\) as a function of \(x\). Use implicit differentiation to find \(y' = \dfrac{dy}{dx}\).
Show full solution
  1. State the plan in the professor's words: differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), and remember that \(y\) is a function of \(x\), so every time you differentiate something with \(y\) in it, the chain rule produces a factor \(y'\).
  2. Differentiate the left side with the product rule. Let \(u = e^y\) and \(v = \sin x\). Then \(u' = e^y\, y'\) (chain rule, because \(y\) depends on \(x\)) and \(v' = \cos x\). \[\frac{d}{dx}\left[e^y \sin x\right] = u'v + uv' = e^y\, y' \sin x + e^y \cos x.\]
  3. Differentiate the right side. The derivative of \(x\) is \(1\). For \(xy\) use the product rule with \(u = x\), \(v = y\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = y'\): \[\frac{d}{dx}\left[x + xy\right] = 1 + (1\cdot y + x\, y') = 1 + y + x\,y'.\]
  4. Set the two derivatives equal. \[e^y\, y' \sin x + e^y \cos x = 1 + y + x\, y'.\]
  5. Move every term with \(y'\) to the left and every other term to the right. Subtract \(x\,y'\) and subtract \(e^y\cos x\) from both sides: \[e^y \sin x \cdot y' - x\, y' = 1 + y - e^y \cos x.\]
  6. Factor out \(y'\) and divide. \[\begin{aligned} y'\left(e^y \sin x - x\right) &= 1 + y - e^y \cos x, \\ y' &= \frac{1 + y - e^y \cos x}{e^y \sin x - x}. \end{aligned}\]
Common mistake. Writing the derivative of \(e^y\) as \(e^y\) without the \(y'\), or writing the derivative of \(xy\) as \(y'\) (or as \(1\cdot y'\)) instead of using the product rule. Both are chain rule or product rule slips, and each one changes the final answer.
Grading note. Expect most of the credit to come from differentiating both sides correctly (the two product rules and the \(y'\) from \(e^y\)). The rest comes from collecting the \(y'\) terms, factoring, and dividing.

Answer: \(y' = \dfrac{1 + y - e^y \cos x}{e^y \sin x - x}\).

Problem 5
(10 points) A cylindrical can must hold exactly 1000 cm3. Find the radius and height of the can that uses the least material, meaning the can with the smallest possible surface area. The surface is made of two circular disks (the top and the bottom) and a side sheet, which becomes a rectangle when you unroll it.
Show full solution
  1. Define the variables from the picture. Let \(r\) be the radius of the can in cm, \(h\) its height in cm, and \(A\) its total surface area in cm2.
  2. Write the constraint and solve it for \(h\). The volume of a cylinder is \(\pi r^2 h\), so \[\pi r^2 h = 1000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{1000}{\pi r^2}.\]
  3. Write the quantity to minimize. The top and bottom are disks of area \(\pi r^2\) each, together \(2\pi r^2\). The side unrolls into a rectangle whose width is the circumference \(2\pi r\) and whose height is \(h\), so its area is \(2\pi r h\). \[A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h.\]
  4. Substitute the constraint so \(A\) depends on \(r\) alone. \[A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r\cdot\frac{1000}{\pi r^2} = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2000\pi r}{\pi r^2} = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2000}{r}.\]
  5. State the domain. A radius must be positive, and any \(r \gt 0\) gives a positive height \(h = \tfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}\). So we minimize \(A(r)\) on \(r \gt 0\), the open interval \((0, \infty)\).
  6. Differentiate. Write \(\tfrac{2000}{r} = 2000r^{-1}\) and use the power rule: \[A'(r) = 4\pi r - 2000r^{-2} = 4\pi r - \frac{2000}{r^2}.\]
  7. Set \(A'(r) = 0\) and solve. \[\begin{aligned} 4\pi r - \frac{2000}{r^2} &= 0 \\ 4\pi r &= \frac{2000}{r^2} \\ 4\pi r^3 &= 2000 \\ r^3 &= \frac{2000}{4\pi} = \frac{500}{\pi} \\ r &= \sqrt[3]{\frac{500}{\pi}}. \end{aligned}\] \(A'(r)\) is undefined only at \(r = 0\), which is not in the domain, so this is the only critical number.
  8. Justify that this is the minimum with the second derivative test, as the professor does. \[A''(r) = 4\pi + 4000r^{-3} = 4\pi + \frac{4000}{r^3}.\] For every \(r \gt 0\) both terms are positive, so \(A''(r) \gt 0\) on the whole domain. The graph of \(A\) is concave up everywhere on \((0, \infty)\), so the only critical number gives the absolute minimum. His key also notes that \(A(r) \to \infty\) as \(r \to 0^+\) (the \(\tfrac{2000}{r}\) term blows up) and as \(r \to \infty\) (the \(2\pi r^2\) term blows up), which agrees. The first derivative test works too: \(A'(r) = \tfrac{4\pi r^3 - 2000}{r^2}\) is negative when \(r^3 \lt \tfrac{500}{\pi}\) and positive when \(r^3 \gt \tfrac{500}{\pi}\), so \(A\) decreases and then increases.
  9. Find the height. A short way: multiply the top and bottom of \(h = \tfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}\) by \(r\) and use \(\pi r^3 = 500\): \[h = \frac{1000\,r}{\pi r^3} = \frac{1000\,r}{500} = 2r = 2\sqrt[3]{\frac{500}{\pi}}.\] The professor's key gets the same thing with exponents: \[h = \frac{1000}{\pi\left(\frac{500}{\pi}\right)^{2/3}} = \frac{2\cdot 500}{\pi^{1/3}\cdot 500^{2/3}} = 2\cdot\frac{500^{1/3}}{\pi^{1/3}} = 2\left(\frac{500}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}.\]
  10. Write the dimensions in a simpler equivalent form if you like. Since \(500 = 125\cdot 4\) and \(\sqrt[3]{125} = 5\), \[r = \sqrt[3]{\frac{500}{\pi}} = 5\sqrt[3]{\frac{4}{\pi}}, \qquad h = 10\sqrt[3]{\frac{4}{\pi}}.\] The best can has height equal to its diameter.
Grading note. On an optimization question, expect the points to be spread over the setup (picture, constraint, and \(A(r)\) in one variable), the derivative, solving \(A'(r) = 0\), the justification that it is a minimum, and the final dimensions with units. Leaving out the justification or the height costs points even when \(r\) is right.

Answer: Radius \(r = \sqrt[3]{500/\pi} = 5\sqrt[3]{4/\pi}\) cm and height \(h = 2r = 2\sqrt[3]{500/\pi} = 10\sqrt[3]{4/\pi}\) cm. This is a minimum because \(A''(r) = 4\pi + \tfrac{4000}{r^3} \gt 0\) for all \(r \gt 0\).

Problem 6
(10 points) Find the intervals on which \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{e^x}\) is increasing and the intervals on which it is decreasing. You do not need to graph the function.
Show full solution
  1. Find the domain. The denominator \(e^x\) is positive for every \(x\), so it is never zero and the domain is all real numbers.
  2. Differentiate with the quotient rule. Let \(u = x^2\) and \(v = e^x\). Then \(u' = 2x\) and \(v' = e^x\). \[f'(x) = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} = \frac{2x\cdot e^x - x^2\cdot e^x}{(e^x)^2}.\]
  3. Simplify by factoring \(e^x\) out of the numerator and canceling it with one factor of \(e^x\) in the denominator (\((e^x)^2 = e^{2x} = e^x\cdot e^x\)). \[f'(x) = \frac{e^x(2x - x^2)}{e^x\cdot e^x} = \frac{2x - x^2}{e^x} = \frac{x(2 - x)}{e^x}.\] Another route gives the same thing: write \(f(x) = x^2 e^{-x}\) and use the product rule, \(f'(x) = 2x\,e^{-x} + x^2\cdot(-e^{-x}) = e^{-x}(2x - x^2)\).
  4. Find the critical numbers. \(f'(x)\) is defined for every \(x\) because \(e^x\) is never zero. Since \(e^x \gt 0\), \(f'(x) = 0\) exactly when the numerator is zero: \[x(2 - x) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 0 \ \text{ or } \ x = 2.\]
  5. Make a sign chart for \(f'\). The critical numbers split the line into \((-\infty, 0)\), \((0, 2)\), and \((2, \infty)\). The factor \(e^x\) is always positive, so the sign of \(f'\) comes from \(x\) and \(2 - x\). Test points: \(x = -1\), \(x = 1\), \(x = 3\).

    \((0, 2)\), test \(x = 1\)\(1\), positive\(1\), positivepositive\(f'(1) = \tfrac{1}{e}\), positiveincreasing
    \((2, \infty)\), test \(x = 3\)\(3\), positive\(-1\), negativepositive\(f'(3) = -\tfrac{3}{e^3}\), negativedecreasing
  6. Read off the answer. \(f\) is increasing where \(f'(x) \gt 0\) and decreasing where \(f'(x) \lt 0\). The question did not ask for extrema, but the chart also shows a local minimum at \((0, 0)\) and a local maximum at \(\left(2, \tfrac{4}{e^2}\right)\), because \(f(2) = \tfrac{2^2}{e^2} = \tfrac{4}{e^2}\). The graph below is only for checking your chart; the exam said no graph was needed.
Common mistake. Forgetting the minus sign in the quotient rule numerator (it is \(u'v - uv'\), top derivative first), or concluding that \(f'\) has a critical number where \(e^x = 0\). The exponential is never zero, so the only critical numbers come from \(x(2 - x) = 0\).
Grading note. Expect most of the credit to come from a correct \(f'(x)\) (quotient rule and the simplification) and the correct critical numbers. The rest comes from the sign chart with test values and each interval labeled as increasing or decreasing.

Answer: \(f\) is increasing on \((0, 2)\) and decreasing on \((-\infty, 0)\) and \((2, \infty)\).

Problem 7
The demand for a product is \(q = 75 - p^2\), where \(p\) is the price in dollars. The elasticity of demand is \(E = -\dfrac{p}{q}\cdot\dfrac{dq}{dp}\).
(a) (6 points) At what price does the demand have unit elasticity?
(b) (4 points) If the price is $6, should the price be raised or lowered to increase revenue? Explain.
Show full solution
  1. Note which prices make sense. We need \(p \gt 0\) and a positive demand \(q = 75 - p^2 \gt 0\), so \(p^2 \lt 75\). Both \(p = 5\) and \(p = 6\) below satisfy this (\(25 \lt 75\) and \(36 \lt 75\)).
  2. Differentiate the demand function with respect to \(p\). \[\frac{dq}{dp} = -2p.\]
  3. Write the elasticity as a function of \(p\). Substitute \(q = 75 - p^2\) and \(\tfrac{dq}{dp} = -2p\): \[E(p) = -\frac{p}{75 - p^2}\cdot(-2p) = \frac{2p^2}{75 - p^2}.\]
  4. Part (a): unit elasticity means \(E = 1\). Set \(E(p) = 1\), multiply both sides by \(75 - p^2\) (which is positive), and solve. \[\begin{aligned} \frac{2p^2}{75 - p^2} &= 1 \\ 2p^2 &= 75 - p^2 \\ 3p^2 &= 75 \\ p^2 &= 25 \\ p &= 5 \quad (\text{we reject } p = -5 \text{ because a price is positive}). \end{aligned}\] Check: \(E(5) = \tfrac{2(25)}{75 - 25} = \tfrac{50}{50} = 1\).
  5. Part (b): compute the elasticity at \(p = 6\). \[q(6) = 75 - 36 = 39, \qquad E(6) = \frac{2(6)^2}{75 - 36} = \frac{72}{39} = \frac{24}{13}.\] Since \(\tfrac{24}{13} \gt 1\), demand is elastic at \(p = 6\).
  6. Explain what elastic demand means for revenue. When demand is elastic, the percent change in quantity is larger than the percent change in price, so price and revenue move in opposite directions: raising the price lowers revenue, and lowering the price raises revenue. So at \(p = 6\) the price should be lowered.
  7. Confirm with the revenue function (a second explanation that also earns credit). Revenue is \(R(p) = pq = p(75 - p^2) = 75p - p^3\), so \[R'(p) = 75 - 3p^2, \qquad R'(6) = 75 - 3(36) = 75 - 108 = -33 \lt 0.\] Revenue is decreasing at \(p = 6\), so moving the price down from 6 increases revenue. Notice also that \(R'(p) = 0\) gives \(3p^2 = 75\), so \(p = 5\): revenue is largest exactly at the unit elasticity price from part (a).
Common mistake. Dropping the minus sign in the formula for \(E\) and getting a negative elasticity, or answering part (b) with "decrease" and no reason. The question says "Explain", so the comparison \(E(6) = \tfrac{24}{13} \gt 1\) and the sentence about what elastic means are the answer.
Grading note. In part (a), expect the points to come from \(\tfrac{dq}{dp}\), the formula \(E(p)\) written out, the equation \(E = 1\), and solving it with the negative root rejected. In part (b), they come from computing \(E(6)\), comparing it with 1, and a conclusion with a reason.

Answer: (a) Unit elasticity at \(p = \$5\). (b) At \(p = \$6\), \(E = \tfrac{24}{13} \gt 1\), so demand is elastic and the price should be decreased to increase revenue (also \(R'(6) = -33 \lt 0\)).

Problem 8
(10 points) You must build an open rectangular box (no top) with a square base and a volume of 1000 ft3. Material for the base costs $4 per square foot and material for the sides costs $2 per square foot. Find the dimensions that minimize the total cost, and explain why your answer gives the smallest possible cost.
Show full solution
  1. Define the variables from the picture. Let \(x\) be the side length of the square base in feet, \(h\) the height in feet, and \(C\) the total cost of material in dollars.
  2. Write the constraint and solve it for \(h\). The volume is base area times height: \[x^2 h = 1000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{1000}{x^2}.\]
  3. Write the cost. The base is one \(x\) by \(x\) square, area \(x^2\), at $4 per square foot: cost \(4x^2\). There are four sides, each an \(x\) by \(h\) rectangle of area \(xh\), at $2 per square foot: cost \(4\cdot xh\cdot 2 = 8xh\). There is no top. \[C = 4x^2 + 8xh.\]
  4. Substitute the constraint so \(C\) depends on \(x\) alone. \[C(x) = 4x^2 + 8x\cdot\frac{1000}{x^2} = 4x^2 + \frac{8000x}{x^2} = 4x^2 + \frac{8000}{x}.\]
  5. State the domain. The side length must be positive, and every \(x \gt 0\) gives a positive height \(h = \tfrac{1000}{x^2}\). So we minimize \(C(x)\) on \(x \gt 0\), the open interval \((0, \infty)\).
  6. Differentiate. Write \(\tfrac{8000}{x} = 8000x^{-1}\) and use the power rule: \[C'(x) = 8x - 8000x^{-2} = 8x - \frac{8000}{x^2}.\]
  7. Set \(C'(x) = 0\) and solve. \[\begin{aligned} 8x - \frac{8000}{x^2} &= 0 \\ 8x &= \frac{8000}{x^2} \\ 8x^3 &= 8000 \\ x^3 &= 1000 \\ x &= 10. \end{aligned}\] \(C'(x)\) is undefined only at \(x = 0\), which is not in the domain, so \(x = 10\) is the only critical number.
  8. Explain why \(x = 10\) gives the smallest cost (the question asks for this). Second derivative test: \[C''(x) = 8 + 16000x^{-3} = 8 + \frac{16000}{x^3}.\] For every \(x \gt 0\) both terms are positive, so \(C''(x) \gt 0\) and \(C\) is concave up on all of \((0, \infty)\). A function that is concave up on the whole interval and has only one critical number there has its absolute minimum at that critical number. The first derivative test gives the same conclusion: \(C'(x) = \tfrac{8x^3 - 8000}{x^2} = \tfrac{8(x^3 - 1000)}{x^2}\) is negative for \(0 \lt x \lt 10\) and positive for \(x \gt 10\), so \(C\) decreases until \(x = 10\) and increases after it.
  9. Find the height and the cost. \[h = \frac{1000}{10^2} = \frac{1000}{100} = 10, \qquad C(10) = 4(100) + \frac{8000}{10} = 400 + 800 = 1200.\]
Common mistake. Counting only two sides, adding a top, or using one price for all the material. Write the cost of each piece separately (base, four sides) before adding. Also, finding \(x = 10\) is not the end: the question asks for the dimensions, so give the height too, and it asks for an explanation, so write the derivative test.
Grading note. Expect the points to be spread over the setup (constraint and the cost function in one variable), the derivative, solving \(C'(x) = 0\), the written explanation that it is a minimum, and the dimensions with units. On this question the explanation is part of what is asked, not an extra.

Answer: The base is 10 ft by 10 ft and the height is 10 ft (minimum cost $1200). It is the smallest cost because \(C''(x) = 8 + \tfrac{16000}{x^3} \gt 0\) for all \(x \gt 0\), so the only critical number \(x = 10\) is the absolute minimum on \((0, \infty)\).

Problem 9
(10 points) The length and width of a rectangle change, but its area stays fixed at 100 cm2. At the moment the length is 20 cm and the length is decreasing at 3 cm/sec, how fast is the width increasing?
Show full solution
  1. Define the variables. Let \(t\) be time in seconds, \(l\) the length of the rectangle in cm, and \(w\) its width in cm. Both \(l\) and \(w\) are functions of \(t\).
  2. Write what is given and what is asked. The length is decreasing at 3 cm/sec, so \(\tfrac{dl}{dt} = -3\) (negative because \(l\) is getting smaller). At that moment \(l = 20\). We want \(\tfrac{dw}{dt}\).
  3. Write the equation that holds at every moment. \[l\,w = 100.\]
  4. Find \(w\) at the moment in question, using the equation with \(l = 20\). \[20w = 100 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w = 5.\]
  5. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\). The left side is a product, so use the product rule; the right side is a constant, so its derivative is \(0\). \[\frac{dl}{dt}\cdot w + l\cdot\frac{dw}{dt} = 0.\]
  6. Substitute \(l = 20\), \(w = 5\), \(\tfrac{dl}{dt} = -3\), and solve. \[\begin{aligned} (-3)(5) + 20\,\frac{dw}{dt} &= 0 \\ -15 + 20\,\frac{dw}{dt} &= 0 \\ 20\,\frac{dw}{dt} &= 15 \\ \frac{dw}{dt} &= \frac{15}{20} = \frac{3}{4}. \end{aligned}\] The answer is positive, so the width is increasing, as the question says.
  7. Another way to check: solve for \(w = \tfrac{100}{l} = 100\,l^{-1}\) and use the chain rule: \[\frac{dw}{dt} = -100\,l^{-2}\cdot\frac{dl}{dt} = -\frac{100}{400}\cdot(-3) = \frac{3}{4}.\]
Common mistake. Substituting \(l = 20\) before differentiating (then \(20w = 100\) differentiates to \(20\tfrac{dw}{dt} = 0\), which is wrong), or using \(\tfrac{dl}{dt} = +3\) for a length that is decreasing.
Grading note. Expect the points to come from the equation \(lw = 100\) with variables defined, differentiating it with respect to \(t\) (product rule), finding \(w = 5\), the correct sign on \(\tfrac{dl}{dt}\), and the final rate with units.

Answer: The width is increasing at \(\tfrac{3}{4}\) cm/sec.

Problem 10
(10 points) Use implicit differentiation to find the slope of the tangent line to the curve \(x^2 - xy - y^2 = 1\) at the point \((2, 1)\).
Show full solution
  1. Check that the point is on the curve. \[2^2 - (2)(1) - 1^2 = 4 - 2 - 1 = 1.\] The left side equals 1, so \((2, 1)\) is on the curve.
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), remembering that \(y\) is a function of \(x\). Term by term: \(\tfrac{d}{dx}[x^2] = 2x\). For \(xy\) use the product rule with \(u = x\), \(v = y\), \(u' = 1\), \(v' = y'\), which gives \(y + x\,y'\). For \(y^2\) the chain rule gives \(2y\,y'\). The derivative of the constant 1 is 0. \[2x - (y + x\,y') - 2y\,y' = 0.\]
  3. Distribute the minus sign in front of the product. \[2x - y - x\,y' - 2y\,y' = 0.\]
  4. Move the terms without \(y'\) to the right side and factor out \(y'\). \[\begin{aligned} -x\,y' - 2y\,y' &= y - 2x \\ -y'(x + 2y) &= y - 2x \\ y'(x + 2y) &= 2x - y \\ y' &= \frac{2x - y}{x + 2y}. \end{aligned}\]
  5. Substitute the point \((2, 1)\). \[y'\Big|_{(2,1)} = \frac{2(2) - 1}{2 + 2(1)} = \frac{4 - 1}{2 + 2} = \frac{3}{4}.\]
  6. A faster route that is just as valid: substitute \(x = 2\), \(y = 1\) right after step 3, then solve for \(y'\). \[2(2) - 1 - 2y' - 2(1)y' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3 - 4y' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = \frac{3}{4}.\] The question asks only for the slope. If a question asks for the tangent line, it is \(y - 1 = \tfrac{3}{4}(x - 2)\), which is \(y = \tfrac{3}{4}x - \tfrac{1}{2}\).
Common mistake. Losing the minus sign in front of \(xy\): the derivative of \(-xy\) is \(-(y + x\,y') = -y - x\,y'\), and both terms change sign. Another one is writing the derivative of \(y^2\) as \(2y\) without the \(y'\).
Grading note. Expect most of the credit to come from the implicit derivative (the product rule on \(xy\), the \(y'\) on \(y^2\), and the signs), and the rest from solving for \(y'\) and substituting the point to get a number.

Answer: The slope of the tangent line at \((2, 1)\) is \(\tfrac{3}{4}\).
